Top Story of the Week
D.C. Council Members Vote Down Self-Driving Cars, for Now (link [ https://substack.com/redirect/31fc69e5-c0de-4dca-8991-9e3aac56949d?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]).
D.C. Council members Charles Allen and Christina Henderson voted for the bill on Thursday, while Zachary Parker, Elissa Silverman and Mayor-elect Janeese Lewis George voted against. In a symbolic move, as the bill would have failed regardless, council Chair Phil Mendelson — who’s allowed to vote in any committee — popped in to vote “yes.” Though lawmakers shared a general sense of support for autonomous vehicles, they disagreed about when they should be rolled out and under what conditions.
My take: A bit embarrassing for D.C., to be honest. The District first passed AV legislation back in 2012, and nearly 15 years later, it still can’t agree on a framework for commercial driverless operations.
Meanwhile, Waymo is providing more than 500,000 paid rides per week and operating in 15 U.S. cities, with its latest safety data showing 82% fewer injury-causing crashes compared to human drivers in its established markets.
And it’s not like the proposed D.C. legislation was a free-for-all either; in fact, it was restrictive from the AV industry’s perspective. The bill included collective bargaining rights for rideshare drivers and AV maintenance workers, a fund to help displaced drivers, wheelchair-accessibility requirements, extensive safety and reporting provisions, and an 800-vehicle cap across all operators (just 200 per company). That vehicle cap is a low ceiling, considering Waymo already operates more than 1,000 robotaxis in the San Francisco Bay Area alone.
There are legitimate concerns about job displacement and how AVs should be regulated, but this seemed like a reasonable, cautious approach to deployment. At some point, continuing to delay has costs of its own, particularly when the technology is already operating at scale elsewhere.
Uber’s new parking feature has a robotaxi twist (link [ https://substack.com/redirect/c67dfbe7-83a9-4f72-87f3-5f0e7bf49282?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]).
Uber says it plans to explore using parking facilities in the SpotHero network as staging areas for idle robotaxis. Instead of cruising city streets between fares, autonomous vehicles could wait in garages or lots until they’re summoned.
My Take: I’m bearish on Uber’s acquisition of SpotHero since Uber is the app people use when they do not want to drive. So I’m not convinced parking reservations are a natural fit. Just because both businesses involve cars doesn’t mean they belong together. By that logic, Uber could also get into gas stations, car insurance, or auto repairs. I’m sure there’s some customer overlap, but that alone doesn’t make this a compelling acquisition.
The robotaxi angle is more interesting though. We know robotaxis are going to need places to park, charge, get cleaned and serviced between rides. SpotHero has a network of more than 13,000 parking facilities across 400+ cities, but it doesn’t actually own or operate them. So it’s unclear whether Uber could use these locations for robotaxi staging under existing agreements or would need to negotiate separately with parking operators.
And there’s the question of how this might affect regular drivers. We recently saw complaints about Waymo vehicles [ https://substack.com/redirect/6a826daf-310c-4f2e-b57d-104dc2882eb8?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ] crowding public EV chargers in San Diego, with some drivers waiting up to an hour to charge. Those charging stations are also privately owned but open to the public.
Could we see similar issues with robotaxis taking up parking spaces, especially in busy urban areas? It may not be a problem if Uber can secure dedicated spots, but that remains to be seen.

His departure follows several other high-profile exits, including Sarfraz Maredia, Uber’s global head of autonomous mobility and delivery, along with the AV division’s heads of business development and legal.
My Take: I don’t think there’s any way to spin this as good news for Uber. Losing this many senior leaders in such a short period is concerning, especially as the company is ramping up its robotaxi ambitions. We don’t know what’s driving these departures, and it’s hard to say what impact they’ll have, but I’d much rather see Uber retaining experienced leaders as it tries to execute on its AV strategy. One departure might not mean much, but this many starts to look like a pattern.

My Take: Waymo has faced some [ https://substack.com/redirect/fb82ce5b-74f1-4dc0-bea5-4a37c008c78e?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ] criticism [ https://substack.com/redirect/2cfb1ee1-7f0f-4155-8894-8e0ed2552fcd?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ] from disability advocates over its lack of wheelchair-accessible robotaxis, a concern that extends to its newest Ojai vehicle.
This will be particularly interesting in London, where all 13,599 licensed black cabs are required to be wheelchair accessible, and more than 70% are already zero-emission capable (source [ https://substack.com/redirect/90dedbdf-bde5-4d84-85f0-e1655ed691bb?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]).
Waymo won’t necessarily face the same requirements since it would operate as a private hire service, but London’s black cab fleet shows that wheelchair-accessible vehicles can be deployed at scale. Obviously, making them fully autonomous adds another layer of complexity, but it’s not like this is some unproven concept. I’ll be curious to see whether Waymo develops a wheelchair-accessible robotaxi or continues to rely on partnerships with other transportation providers to serve these riders.
