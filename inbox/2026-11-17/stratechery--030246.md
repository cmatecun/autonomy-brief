Apple Permissions Update
------------------------

From 9to5Mac ( https://9to5mac.com/2026/11/16/macos-27-3-includes-new-privacy-controls-for-full-disk-access-and-more/ ) :

Last month, Apple announced its plans to tighten macOS privacy controls around Full Disk Access amid the rise of “increasingly capable and autonomous” AI agents. With today’s new macOS 27.3 beta, Apple is implementing several changes as part of this…

In macOS 27.2, “Device Control and Data Access” is a unified toggle in System Settings, where Apple says that apps with that level of access can “view and send email, edit contacts and photos, monitor your keyboard, track websites, record your screen, control any app on your Mac, and more.” In macOS 27.3, Apple is splitting “Device Control and Data Access” into two categories:

* Accessibility: Intended specifically for assistive technologies, with permissions handled through an entitlement process.
* System and App Protection Override: A new category for apps that need to bypass certain macOS protections to control other apps or perform automated tasks.

For the latter, users will be able to specify which apps an automation tool can access rather than granting it unrestricted control. For instance, you could give an app access to the Mail app, but not Messages. macOS 27.3 also adds an option for users to require an app to request permission every time it needs access, rather than granting it access indefinitely…

Crucially, Apple isn’t removing the ability to give an app Full Disk Access. If you deem it necessary, you can still give an app Full Disk Access. However, Apple is redesigning the approval process to make the implications of that decision more obvious.

I wrote about Apple’s Full Disk Access warning in October ( https://stratechery.com/2026/apple-and-a-hackers-future/?access_token=eyJhbGciOiJFUzI1NiIsImtpZCI6IkxzanFRcEhoMUJUdmU0djJQUUVuejIiLCJ0eXAiOiJKV1QifQ.eyJhdWQiOiJzdHJhdGVjaGVyeS5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUiLCJhenAiOiJIS0xjUzREd1Nod1AyWURLYmZQV00xIiwiZW50Ijp7InVyaSI6WyJodHRwczovL3N0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LmNvbS8yMDI2L2FwcGxlLWFuZC1hLWhhY2tlcnMtZnV0dXJlLyJdfSwiZXhwIjoxNzk3NTA1MzY2LCJpYXQiOjE3OTQ5MTMzNjYsImlzcyI6Imh0dHBzOi8vYXBwLnBhc3Nwb3J0Lm9ubGluZS9vYXV0aCIsInNjb3BlIjoiZmVlZDpyZWFkIGFydGljbGU6cmVhZCBhc3NldDpyZWFkIGNhdGVnb3J5OnJlYWQgZW50aXRsZW1lbnRzIiwic3ViIjoiMDE5NTE2ZjEtYTM0Ni03NjE0LTllOTYtYjYzYmQxMWZlODU2IiwidXNlIjoiYWNjZXNzIn0.Rs6Kt4bgl4OtNVQabHAnRbPvtLpVFa-tSFreUA0K5h7JoyZgtp6CxsZg1kiyVmQjVUGV9LlBaHD1q9BsyVl7_w ) with some trepidation:

To say that I’m nervous about what Apple’s solution will entail is a massive understatement. There is one aspect in which the Mac is the perfect agent host: Apple has, for decades, invested in a combination of scriptability, automation, and accessibility APIs (these are very often the same thing) that makes it remarkably well-suited to computer use. Then there is the fact that macOS is a certified Unix system; this means that agents — which are perfectly suited to the command line — have access to the entire universe of tooling built for Unix systems. And, of course, Mac hardware is amazing.

The problem is that for my particular use case — a headless, always-on Mac Mini that I primarily access from other computers and my phone through the ChatGPT and Claude apps — macOS is incredibly hostile. The biggest issue is GUI-only permission prompts that are invisible to software running on said computer, including agents.

I’m not going to dive back into the details of why those permission prompts are so maddening, but for what it’s worth, this announcement did not make things worse; it also did not really fix the problem, and I think it’s pretty interesting to consider why that is. Yes, this is a personal hobbyhorse of mine, but there is a broader point to be made.

The New App Paradigm
--------------------

Go back to September’s iPhone event, and my critique ( https://stratechery.com/2026/the-iphone-duo-the-intelligent-personal-hub-apple-watch-audio-intelligence/?access_token=eyJhbGciOiJFUzI1NiIsImtpZCI6IkxzanFRcEhoMUJUdmU0djJQUUVuejIiLCJ0eXAiOiJKV1QifQ.eyJhdWQiOiJzdHJhdGVjaGVyeS5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUiLCJhenAiOiJIS0xjUzREd1Nod1AyWURLYmZQV00xIiwiZW50Ijp7InVyaSI6WyJodHRwczovL3N0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LmNvbS8yMDI2L3RoZS1pcGhvbmUtZHVvLXRoZS1pbnRlbGxpZ2VudC1wZXJzb25hbC1odWItYXBwbGUtd2F0Y2gtYXVkaW8taW50ZWxsaWdlbmNlLyJdfSwiZXhwIjoxNzk3NTA1MzY2LCJpYXQiOjE3OTQ5MTMzNjYsImlzcyI6Imh0dHBzOi8vYXBwLnBhc3Nwb3J0Lm9ubGluZS9vYXV0aCIsInNjb3BlIjoiZmVlZDpyZWFkIGFydGljbGU6cmVhZCBhc3NldDpyZWFkIGNhdGVnb3J5OnJlYWQgZW50aXRsZW1lbnRzIiwic3ViIjoiMDE5NTE2ZjEtYTM0Ni03NjE0LTllOTYtYjYzYmQxMWZlODU2IiwidXNlIjoiYWNjZXNzIn0.gwsj_ein7_Q3BAvb8JXjCgdWcKvrYnYQUzR3uCmmvJalK2OODyjnr7h6TM52X4l4CLjXfSlNScVBcUDaSypYwg ) of CEO John Ternus’ framing of the iPhone as “the intelligent personal hub”:

It’s extremely impressive that Apple claims 300,000 apps work with Siri. Note, however, that the implication of it being “easy for developers to adopt new capabilities” is that developers have to actually put in the work — that’s work in addition to updating their UI for Duo. In a world where everyone has to convince developers to build integrations, this wouldn’t be an issue. However, this is where browser use looms large: to the extent that agents can just use the web is the extent to which they get an integration with basically everything for free, and it’s Apple, with its dependency on developers plugging into APIs, who is at a disadvantage…

This is a subtler point, but perhaps the most profound: in Apple’s vision, the utility of Intelligence is defined by its ability to augment your existing workflow. Thus the reference to updating your calendar and reminders. It’s very possible, however, that the better workflow is to outsource a lot of work that used to happen in apps to the agent directly. What’s better, using a structured reminders app that you have to check, or simply being reminded directly by an agent? In truth the answer will likely vary by person, but it’s worth pointing out that Apple is so married to the app paradigm that they probably never even considered the alternative.

This is actually exactly what happened with this update. First, full disk access still exists, just with much scarier warnings, and I’m fine with that: it’s good for users to know what they are getting into. Second, there is some genuine loosening of permissions with the new “System & App Protection Override”: as I understand it, this gives specific applications broad-based permissions to do things like control other apps, and I assume (hope) all of the other capabilities that it might need, and you only need to ask once. This is helpful!

At this point, however, I’ve granted ChatGPT and Claude all of the permissions they might ever want or need, and I still deal with permissions dialogs, and it’s for a vector that doesn’t seem to be on Apple’s radar. Specifically, Apple is very concerned with apps controlling other apps; my use case, however, is two apps — ChatGPT and Claude — making other apps.

These apps are barely apps: I have a whole fleet of little apps that manage the channels listings for various sports leagues; these apps need to access the tuner devices on my network, which means they need to be able to see devices on the local network — that means a GUI-only permission dialog (although while proof-reading, ChatGPT may have found a fix for this specific permission ( https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technotes/tn3179-understanding-local-network-privacy ) ).. Another example is that I bought a networked label printer: instead of using the (terrible) Mac app, Claude built an app that controls it directly; that needed a permissions dialog. Indeed, I basically do not use any new software anymore: if I need something, I custom build it on the spot — but then I hit permissions dialogs!

(As a side note, I do have an ethernet KVM attached to my Mac Mini, which allows my agents to view the screen from outside the OS; this let the agents tell me that a permissions request was on the screen, but they would refuse to click “OK”. In this Fable 5.2 is a wonderful surprise: I’ve made clear to the agents that the Mac Mini they run on is their Mac Mini — I don’t have any personal information on there — and Fable now cites that and willingly clicks “OK”. I feel silly at how happy this makes me.)

Unfortunately, Apple’s changes don’t address this issue at all, at least as far as I can tell. To go back to my September critique, Apple is very focused on a world of apps, and to the extent they are thinking about agents, they are thinking about agents controlling apps that already exist. The future, however, isn’t about apps at all, at least not as we presently think about them: it’s about making new ones, on demand, for specific use cases; the fact I have to give all of these one-offs special permissions really does drive me crazy. More pertinently to this Update, it continues to raise questions in my mind as to whether Apple is even capable of imagining a future where capital-A Apps are things to avoid, not reasons to buy.

My iPhone Duo Review
--------------------

Meanwhile, to go back to that iPhone event, I sadly cracked the screen on my beloved iPhone Air, and was in the market for a new phone sooner than I expected; thanks to Daman — whose voice you hear reading quotes on the podcast recordings of these Updates — I managed to snag a walk-in iPhone Duo. I was certain it wasn’t for me, but I figured it would be worth it just for the content.

It turns out I love it — and, interestingly enough, what I love appears to be a late realization for Apple. Again from 9to5Mac ( https://9to5mac.com/2026/11/16/ios-27-3-adds-new-face-unlock-feature-for-iphone-duo/ ) :

In today’s iOS 27.3 beta release, Apple is introducing two changes for iPhone Duo users. Most notably, it adds a new face recognition option that lets you unlock your iPhone Duo by looking at it. Apple is also adding an option to have your iPhone Duo lock when you close it.

The facial recognition — don’t call it Face ID, which projects 30,000 infrared dots on your face to make a 3D depth map — is getting the headlines, but it’s the second feature that stands out to me. When Apple announced the Duo they included this incredible animation:

X Post ( https://x.com/i/web/status/2097782855141335144 )

The implication is clear: you can use your iPhone on the small screen, seamlessly transition to the big screen, and go back again. It’s also totally unrealistic, at least that last part.

Here’s my take on the iPhone Duo: to me it’s a big iPhone Air. What I love about the Air — and after using the Air, hated about the iPhone Pro — is the feel of it in my hand. It’s so thin and light that it’s genuinely a pleasure to hold, and the big screen of the Duo is amazing to use. Apple really nailed the big screen UI, particularly multi-tasking.

Now obviously, I can’t fit an unfolded Duo in my pocket, thus the fold. But that is purely a matter of logistics: I would never willingly go from the big screen to the small screen while using the phone ; why would I want to so severely compromise my experience when I already went to the trouble of opening the screen (or, when I’m at home, basically never closing it)? To that end, requiring a separate action to lock the phone after folding it up was bizarre. I found myself forgetting to lock the phone all of the time, and the fact that is the default suggests that Apple was actually captured by their animation, and not how people might actually use this thing. Now it’s much clearer: when you need to go you close the phone and you put it in your pocket, and that’s that.

Now, when you’re out and about and need to look at a message, having something akin to Face ID is definitely a big improvement; Apple clearly should have put Face ID on the outside of the phone, even if it was impossible to add on the inside. That’s fine! They are not two sizes of the same phone; they are two completely distinct experiences that happen to be in the same physical device.

More generally, John Gruber made a great point on this morning’s Dithering ( https://dithering.passport.online/member/episode/ben-gets-a-duo ) that what is exciting about Apple’s current iPhone lineup is that it is compromised. You have the base iPhone, which is fine, but then the iPhone Pro goes all out for power and performance and battery life, at the cost of size and weight; the Air goes all out for size and weight, at the cost of battery life and the camera; the Duo goes all out for that incredible big screen, at the cost a compromised portable experience and features like Face ID. I love it! Trade-offs are fun, especially when you lean into them, and that’s exactly what Apple is doing (particularly after this update).
