One More Note on Agents
-----------------------

Both that podcast and yesterday’s Article Apps, Agents, and Aggregation ( https://stratechery.com/2026/apps-agents-and-aggregation/?access_token=eyJhbGciOiJSUzI1NiIsImtpZCI6InN0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LnBhc3Nwb3J0Lm9ubGluZSIsInR5cCI6IkpXVCJ9.eyJhdWQiOiJzdHJhdGVjaGVyeS5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUiLCJhenAiOiJIS0xjUzREd1Nod1AyWURLYmZQV00xIiwiZW50Ijp7InVyaSI6WyJodHRwczovL3N0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LmNvbS8yMDI2L2FwcHMtYWdlbnRzLWFuZC1hZ2dyZWdhdGlvbi8iXX0sImV4cCI6MTc5MzI2ODE2MSwiaWF0IjoxNzkwNjc2MTYxLCJpc3MiOiJodHRwczovL2FwcC5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUvb2F1dGgiLCJzY29wZSI6ImZlZWQ6cmVhZCBhcnRpY2xlOnJlYWQgYXNzZXQ6cmVhZCBjYXRlZ29yeTpyZWFkIGVudGl0bGVtZW50cyIsInN1YiI6IjAxOTUxNmYxLWEzNDYtNzYxNC05ZTk2LWI2M2JkMTFmZTg1NiIsInVzZSI6ImFjY2VzcyJ9.NxgnggrogOaAF6NHM5bzKTm03m5JCU9FRcbhe1pziqrOYQ4LXxjX2auORHCOf_5yI0g0fNLl9HHonnqfAeiapDSMHqPGHfN107ozf7Y3u8qffPH5BlidJpwejivxPYkFGXr8adXZdDaA0QETky8k6uUPKa0TyMhxwZpzFwL6EHmnDtZclwmRCLR_CQy3d6VhlJTc2htm20bgV-XcA7D20NN3ah1OiY9fsCZsDhc3fL_otOOI4hHg0B2K38fxjm90jpFLB8zuHTv6mDInfFyE6HO2xdT71Se-9F5U3W1g0SgsHex4fodntDQIT3LZicY8EU7-AckWw1BNi94VzqQ6yg ) were primarily focused on the strategic implication of agents and their potential to aggregate Aggregators; however, there is one more point I made on this morning’s episode of Dithering ( https://dithering.passport.online/member/episode/personal-ai ) that I should have emphasized more.

Specifically, it’s very fair to wonder if consumers will use agents; I’ve made the argument repeatedly that normal people don’t care about being productive, which is all that Silicon Valley seems to talk about. At the same time, the fact of the matter is that technology — even app-mediated smartphones — is still really hard and complicated for normal people.

You can make the case the smartphone is the ultimate form factor — small enough to be portable, large enough to be your only device — but there is no way that the current app and web paradigm is the ultimate in user experience. Simply talking to an agent who does the difficult things for you — and to be clear, I, someone who is very adept at computers, find agents tremendously more capable for a good many things — is a better experience.

That right there is why there is reason for optimism about agents in the long run: yes, it will take normal consumers a while to figure out how to use them, but when they do I think they will love them even more than technical people do. And, once end users do end up figuring them out and loving them, all of the strategic considerations that I wrote about will come into play.

Meta Connect
------------

I briefly touched on Meta’s Connect keynote ( https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SdKFDIAGF24 ) last week, but to briefly summarize the announcement with three stories from Mark Gurman at Bloomberg ( https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-23/meta-launches-1-299-vr-headset-that-look-like-glasses-to-rival-apple-vision-pro ) :

Meta Platforms Inc. on Wednesday launched $1,299 virtual-reality glasses that undercut Apple Inc. in price and size while putting its headset line on track for profitability. The new device, called Meta VR Glasses, represents a significant shift in headset design. Its processor, cooling fan, battery and other computing components are housed in an external pack that can clip to a user’s pocket or rest on a table. Meta’s previous models incorporated those components into the headset itself, while Apple’s Vision Pro leaves only the battery to an external pack.

The device has many of the same features as the Vision Pro and Meta’s existing Quest headsets, including immersive video playback, office apps, the ability to serve as an external monitor for a Mac or PC, web browsing, gaming, videoconferencing and a voice assistant. But moving the computing hardware outside the headset dramatically reduces its weight. At about 100 grams, Meta VR Glasses weigh roughly one-sixth as much as Apple’s device and one-fifth as much as the Quest 3S. That lighter design made the headset comfortable during an extended demonstration that included watching immersive highlights from an NBA game, navigating Meta’s redesigned operating system and working on a PC.

This looks awesome, and like a thorough rebuke of Apple’s approach to the Vision Pro hardware; I’m going to save further comment until I get a chance to try them. Next ( https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-23/meta-debuts-349-camera-free-ray-bans-and-brings-muse-to-glasses ) :

Meta Platforms Inc. is expanding its smart-glasses lineup with camera-free models, support for the popular new Muse assistant and a hearing-aid mode. At its Connect conference Wednesday, the social media giant unveiled the Ray-Ban Meta Audio, its first pair of glasses without cameras. The company also introduced a third-generation model of its camera-equipped Ray-Ban spectacles and new versions of its more affordable Meta-branded frames.

The new audio-focused glasses give Meta a way to revisit a product category that has sparked outcry over privacy issues. Meta and the broader smart-glasses industry have faced concerns that the devices’ cameras can record people without their knowledge or consent.

The privacy concerns are totally legitimate, but there are other advantages to these as well, including increased battery life. Finally ( https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-23/meta-debuts-a-dedicated-palm-sized-muse-charm-device-to-use-ai-on-the-go ) :

Meta Platforms Inc. unveiled a palm-sized, dedicated gadget for using Muse, the company’s popular new artificial intelligence assistant, pushing deeper into the AI devices market with a surprise announcement. The product, unveiled on stage by Meta Chief Executive Officer Mark Zuckerberg, was born out of the company’s new design lab led by former Apple Inc. interface design chief Alan Dye and its Superintelligence AI group. The device, called the Muse Charm, looks like a Tamagotchi — the digital pet from the ’90s. It has a roughly 2-inch display and runs a new operating system designed to bring Muse to life on its own hardware.

This device was emblematic of the Connect keynote as a whole, which was completely live and landed in a tight 50 minutes. Everything just made sense, because Meta Connect Labs suddenly has a north star: this isn’t a VR division, or an AR division, or even an AI glasses division. Rather, Meta devices are Muse delivery vehicles, and nothing exemplifies that more than the Muse Charm. It might end up being a nothingburger, but it’s cute, just like Muse, and it only makes sense if Muse is a hit.

Indeed, this is the most powerful affirmation of how important Muse is, and what its potential might be: it can singlehandedly justify the more-than-a-decade twelve-figure losses that Reality Labs has incurred. Meta is well-placed to win the agent wars in consumer not just because Muse is tremendously well done and is riding on Meta’s distribution; they also have hardware ready to go to deliver Muse to you on your terms, both on your smartphone but also on independent devices.

Meta Enterprise Platform
------------------------

From the Wall Street Journal ( https://www.wsj.com/tech/ai/meta-seeks-payoff-from-ai-spending-with-new-push-for-business-customers-8b9ca5bc ) :

Meta Platforms is starting a new business focused on selling artificial-intelligence tools to enterprise customers, aiming to build on the momentum of its viral Muse personal assistant and deliver a return on its hefty spending. The move will bring together sales of several of Meta’s existing AI tools including its Muse personal productivity agent, a coding tool, access to its top models and agents for businesses, the company said. Meta hired Chirantan “CJ” Desai, previously chief executive of document database company MongoDB, to run the new business line, reporting to CEO Mark Zuckerberg.

The Facebook and Instagram parent is in the throes of an evolution from a consumer-oriented business with a core focus on selling ads to one that also offers subscriptions and services to enterprise customers. It is spending more than $100 billion on its AI infrastructure just this year, and investors are eager to see that investment bear fruit.

Zuckerberg said on X ( https://x.com/finkd/status/2104550609403695581 ) :

We believe superintelligence will create significant new opportunities for all people and businesses. Meta already serves billions of people at scale and helps hundreds of millions of businesses reach customers. Today we are starting the next major pillar of our business, Meta Enterprise Platform, to help businesses use AI to grow and transform in new ways as well.

Meta Enterprise Platform will use our strengths that few other companies have: advanced models, leading agents, large-scale infrastructure, and years of working closely with many businesses. Initially, we will focus on bringing our full technology stack, including the Muse agent, Meta Business Agent, Muse API, Muse Code, and more to businesses and developers to help them grow.

Insert sad trombone sound.

I don’t think that most readers will be surprised, but I hate this, and it’s a real sour note after the last month. Serving enterprise customers is completely different than serving consumers, and there are very few companies — if any! — who are good at both. Enterprises require go-to-market skills that consumer companies lack; consumer companies can push the envelope on experiences and data usage that are verboten for enterprise companies. The underlying tech may be similar, but everything else is not just different, but diametrically opposed.

People will, of course, point at Google, but I’m not sure that’s a great example. First, it took Google multiple restarts and over a decade of leadership from Thomas Kurian to build a cloud business that is still third place in its market. Moreover, you could make the case that it isn’t an accident that Google hasn’t really done anything of note in the consumer market in those ten years, other than milk properties that preceded its enterprise expansion. Indeed, there is an argument that prioritizing Google Cloud has been a factor in Google’s lackluster AI offerings.

Meta right now, at this moment, sits in a very different position: Zuckerberg’s AI pivot has the potential to pay off, and all of the company’s focus and resources — including compute — should be focused on owning the consumer space in a way that hasn’t really been possible before. Meta is already dominant in terms of entertainment, but Muse gives them an opportunity to be the entry point for every other aspect of a user’s life.

That is, I suppose, the steelman argument here: a user’s life includes work, so why not bring Meta there as well? I think, however, that this harkens back to what I think was one of Zuckerberg’s most mistaken views; from The Facebook Effect ( https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/The-Facebook-Effect/David-Kirkpatrick/9781439102121 ) :

How much of ourselves should we show the world? It’s an important question Facebook forces us to confront. Do I want you to know that I am a longtime Fortune magazine journalist who covers technology and is now writing a book about Facebook? Or should I tell you I am a fifty-seven-year-old husband of an artist, father of a teenage girl, sometime poet, and former union activist? Up to now, depending on the social context, I would most likely have presented one or the other of these identities to you. On my single Facebook profile, pretty much all is revealed.

That is no accident. Zuckerberg designed Facebook that way. “You have one identity,” he says emphatically three times in a single minute during a 2009 interview. He recalls that in Facebook’s early days some argued the service ought to offer adult users both a work profile and a “fun social profile.” Zuckerberg was always opposed to that. “The days of you having a different image for your work friends or co-workers and for the other people you know are probably coming to an end pretty quickly,” he says.

He makes several arguments. “Having two identities for yourself is an example of a lack of integrity,” Zuckerberg says moralistically. But he also makes a case he sees as pragmatic—that “the level of transparency the world has now won’t support having two identities for a person.” In other words, even if you want to segregate your personal from your professional information you won’t be able to, as information about you proliferates on the Internet and elsewhere. He would say the same about any images one individual seeks to project—for example, a teenager who acts docile at home but is a drug-using reprobate with his friends.

Zuckerberg, along with a key group of his colleagues, also believes that by openly acknowledging who we are and behaving consistently among all our friends, we will help create a healthier society. In a more “open and transparent” world, people will be held to the consequences of their actions and be more likely to behave responsibly. “To get people to this point where there’s more openness—that’s a big challenge,” says Zuckerberg. “But I think we’ll do it. I just think it will take time. The concept that the world will be better if you share more is something that’s pretty foreign to a lot
of people and it runs into all these privacy concerns.”

I just flat out disagree with this; I laid out my argument in Social Networking 2.0 ( https://stratechery.com/2020/social-networking-2-0/?access_token=eyJhbGciOiJSUzI1NiIsImtpZCI6InN0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LnBhc3Nwb3J0Lm9ubGluZSIsInR5cCI6IkpXVCJ9.eyJhdWQiOiJzdHJhdGVjaGVyeS5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUiLCJhenAiOiJIS0xjUzREd1Nod1AyWURLYmZQV00xIiwiZW50Ijp7InVyaSI6WyJodHRwczovL3N0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LmNvbS8yMDIwL3NvY2lhbC1uZXR3b3JraW5nLTItMC8iXX0sImV4cCI6MTc5MzI2ODE2MSwiaWF0IjoxNzkwNjc2MTYxLCJpc3MiOiJodHRwczovL2FwcC5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUvb2F1dGgiLCJzY29wZSI6ImZlZWQ6cmVhZCBhcnRpY2xlOnJlYWQgYXNzZXQ6cmVhZCBjYXRlZ29yeTpyZWFkIGVudGl0bGVtZW50cyIsInN1YiI6IjAxOTUxNmYxLWEzNDYtNzYxNC05ZTk2LWI2M2JkMTFmZTg1NiIsInVzZSI6ImFjY2VzcyJ9.EZ1lQX9KLY61_WYWakJYOdHq5k9XzRmyBawwkgT_YiPElUwuzkBjRnzQHAGPDo0Sg7Yr6qC81BiX86uuPQIq6i6cZ9tUUpMopgh8AMs3_PEO-CXiIhQbqCjQ_uqX1rE_G1XQs-gLeBL_TQIRwVbO_3OJB-4l--u66kAujVdJNS-Nl6G3hRjwmdrcDqdKw5R-u59PzL5V7jq4BTAwjgTpoKvkk9-RDAcetNNOFA0qWzCtYOcyz0TnTTBBGR00hQBYqJAU9z3BmhA1Jv8USR_pTPfUA1oX0wZh4RA66loUD0sX-ulo6S2wumLMBWYFK-FUuvMBhnvjfJlXHOyCy9J0wQ ) :

What I increasingly realize, though, is that separating my identities on Twitter does not mean a lesser experience, but a far superior one; social interaction in any medium is always a balance between self-expression and the accommodation of others, which means that in the analog world it is a constant struggle to strike a balance between being myself and annoying everyone around me at some point or another. The magic of the Internet, though, is that you can be whatever you want to be…

This was tied to my enthusiasm for messaging and group chats:

This is where messaging is a much more natural fit, and, as far as the depth of your network is concerned, messaging services are just as much a threat to v1 social networks connectivity as TikTok is to Facebook’s hold on attention: I can simultaneously be a Bucks fan with the Fiefdom, be a tech enthusiast with my Slack group, explore ideas with my WhatsApp group, and talk politics with my trusted friends. The fact that I am not my whole self in any of these groups is a feature, not a bug, and one that is uniquely made possible by digital…I am in private groups with plenty of folks that I disagree with about a whole host of things, but because we share a common interest, and are ok being trusted friends on that vector, I have learned a lot about why they believe what they believe about a bunch of issues. I think it helps my analysis, and I think it makes me a better citizen. That certainly isn’t our expectation of social media today, but that is because we are stuck on v1.

There is certainly room to debate whether or not multiple identities make sense in your private life (although I highly doubt I’m going to come off of my position here); what I think is even less debatable is that having separate identities for your private life and work life is not just healthy, but also a vastly superior product experience. We just saw Microsoft realize that Copilot for both professional and personal lives was a mistake, and double down on the former; that Meta is risking the possibility of owning the latter to pursue the former, with literally no reason to think they might succeed other than what I believe to be a mistaken understanding of human nature, is crazy to me. Muse is cute and fun; why would you want to associate that with work, and intermingle what you have to do with what you want to do?

The other steelman argument is that I am underestimating just how much all of this costs, both compute and also the R&D that undergirds Meta Superintelligence Labs. We know that you can make money in enterprise selling AI; maybe Zuckerberg feels that it would be fiscally irresponsible to not chase those dollars given how much he is spending. I can see the logic in that, but the unfortunate reality is that initiatives based on solipsistic justifications like rationalizing spending are unlikely to end well, especially when they are a bad idea.

All of that noted, I am somewhat more encouraged by this morning’s announcement of Muse for Small Business; that is a much more natural extension of Meta’s actual advantages, and as a small business owner, I can certainly see the extent to which private and professional life can intermingle. Start there, though, and see if growing up makes sense; going for enterprise now is a mistake.
