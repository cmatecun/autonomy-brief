** AI questions
------------------------------------------------------------

In tech, the point that we understand everything is generally the point to move on to the next thing: we understand app stores and PCs, but we understand almost nothing about how AI will work, so far. It’s all very 1997. But after almost four years (ChatGPT went live in November 2022, remember), we might be getting a bit more clarity about the questions.

First, there’s a cluster of questions that everyone knows, that have been foundational since 2022. At the root of everything, how far do the models keep scaling, at what cost? Then, do models continue to commoditise, do open models match the frontier and overtake it, and when do Chinese models join the frontier? These are big questions, but they’re also kind of boring - there’s no tension here. We can’t answerthese, just wait to see.

** The Hugging Face hack fall-out
------------------------------------------------------------

OpenAI finally published its own post mortem of the Hugging Face hack. The more details that come out, the more impressive the sophistication and complexity of the attack becomes: anyone who looks at the details of what these systems did and still claims they’re ‘stochastic parrots’ and hype, and that this tech isn’t a very big deal, is in deep denial (or just a con-artist). This particular example clearly changes the landscape for IT security. But how much more should we be worried? ** Nvidia buys Hugging Face (?)
------------------------------------------------------------

Last week Stripe bought OpenRouter for (reportedly) $7.5bn; this week Nvidia has (reportedly) agreed to buy Hugging Face for $12.9bn. Both companies are plays on the thesis that LLMs will be commodities; if companies switch models all the time, they’ll need orchestration layers of some kind to manage that.

It’s fascinating to watch Nvidia use its balance sheet ($127bn of FCF in the last 12 months) to create as much competition and fragmentation as possible amongst its competitors and suppliers: this is all boosting demand, but it’s also reinforcing Nvidia’s own de facto monopoly at its own layer in the stack. Credit guarantees, power guarantees, direct funding and subsidies for open source models all have the effect of promoting more customers for Nvidia, each with less bargaining power, while weakening the market for alternative chips (TPUs etc). LINK (https://us.list-manage.com/IBrXw61sLWI?e=bc4ee99da9&c2id=e2956131aa985567d31359cb60722597)


** Model neutrality is the new net neutrality
------------------------------------------------------------

OpenAI shows the other side of this - following SpaceX’s purchase of Cursor, it announced that it would no longer let Cursor use OpenAI models. The reasoning is that it can’t trust Elon Musk to follow the ToS, since he’s already admitted breaking them. Cursor responded that OpenAI models are only 5% of use on its platform and Claude plus its own Grok are better, which misses the point - they may be better now, but what about tomorrow? More broadly, in every platform war, companies weaponise compatibility, APIs and ToS: remember Microsoft in the 1990s or iOS and Android in the 2010s. The reasons they give are often even true, but they’re still weapons. ** Musk musks
------------------------------------------------------------

The Information does a good job of pulling together all of Elon Musk’s recent moves into making his own gas turbines to power data centres. As a bunch of people have pointed out (see the Bloomberg report I linked last week), there are more chips being bought and data centres being built in the US than there is grid power to light them all up, with supply backlogged for years, and if you want to build your own on-site generation then gas turbines are the best option, except that there are only a handful of suppliers and they’re backlogged for years as well. Further along the supply chain, the turbine blades themselves are the critical components and those again have a handful of backlogged suppliers. So, in parallel to SpaceX’s plan to create a trillion dollar chip foundry, Musk now plans to start making his own turbine blades. The whole thing looks more and more like Henry Ford (with Twitter standing in for the Dearborn Independent). I know enough about cellular to know that SpaceX cannot
compete with it directly, but I don’t know enough about gas turbines to know if this makes any sense. ** Meta settlement
------------------------------------------------------------

The obvious but perhaps not so important story this week: Meta settled with a group of US states over teenager use of its products, paying a fine (spread over a decade) and agreeing to a range of policy changes and usage restrictions. The interesting part is that quite a lot of the settlement is linked to whether Snap, YouTube and TikTok agree to the same terms: Meta will pay $12bn and make some products changes if it's alone in the deal, but if the others join the settlement then Meta will pay a further $5bn and make further product changes. Meta is trying to spin this as industry leadership to solve the problem, and trying to bounce its competitors into following.

Stepping back, even $17bn, spread over a decade, is pretty small given that Meta had $19bn of operating profits just in the last quarter (let alone the ongoing losses in ‘Reality labs’), and on the accompanying analyst call Meta said that teenagers are only 1% of revenue. Meanwhile, I pointed out last week that while plenty of people will say that they ‘know’ this is all bad for kids, the actual evidence for harm is thin and contradictory - this isn’t tobacco. And finally, like Apple’s EU recent app store settlement, this is an argument from the past, a decade after these decisions were taken, just as AI is about to change everything again.
