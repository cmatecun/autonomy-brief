# Europe's First Fully Driverless Robotaxi Rides & Spain's First L4 Permit

Across the Atlantic, Waymo went live on the Lyft app in Nashville, in a structure that has not existed anywhere before.

Three European wins this week; two Chinese stacks delivered them
Three driverless firsts landed on European roads. A Chinese stack took the safety person out of an airport shuttle in Switzerland. A Chinese stack took Spain’s first national Level 4 permit. A Chinese stack ran Europe’s first fully driverless passenger rides in Croatia.
Europe supplied the roads, the regulators and the paperwork. It did not supply a single one of the stacks.
And the thing it still does not have is not only technology. It is a service an ordinary member of the public can open an app, book and pay for, with no human driver on board.
WeRide took the human out of the bus at Zurich Airport
Zurich Airport’s automated shuttle buses are now running without a safety person on board, monitored from a central control room. The first fully automated runs took place in early September.
Source: LinkedIn/WeRide
The programme has been going since March 2025 with WeRide as technology partner. Two shuttles, used as employee transport, running a defined route. In the last eighteen months they have covered more than 15,000 kilometres.
These are employee shuttles on a fixed route, and the route does not cross any aircraft taxiways. The first Level 4 runs are again being conducted without passengers, and the airport expects employees to be allowed on board after roughly four weeks of test operation. The whole programme is currently funded and planned only to the end of 2026, with continuation “being examined” with the project partners. That is a small, well-bounded operational design domain, and saying so makes the achievement more credible rather than less.
Staff from partner firms Swissport and Krummen Kerzers watch the operation from a remote cockpit and can intervene if needed. Those are the former safety drivers. They were trained for the new role and now accompany the operation from a distance.
Raphaël Glaesener, Senior Innovation Manager at Flughafen Zürich AG, put it more plainly:
“Automated driving does not mean that people disappear. But the tasks change. Operations also become more efficient, because in future one employee can monitor several buses.”
That second sentence is the entire economic argument of this issue, stated by a Swiss airport rather than by a vendor. The job survives the transition. The ratio is what makes the business work, and the ratio is designed to keep climbing.
Coralie Klaus Boecker, head of the airport’s innovation hub, gave the demand-side reason: stepwise automation can make a real contribution for airports facing an emerging skilled-labour shortage.
But WeRide was not finished.
WeRide, Uber and AVOMO received the first Spanish permit for L4 passenger vehicles
Two days later, WeRide, Uber and AVOMO received Spain’s first national permit for Level 4 autonomous passenger vehicles on public roads, issued by Spain’s Directorate General of Traffic under the ES-AV framework, in collaboration with the Regional Government of Madrid.
The specifics, because they are what make this gradeable:
Twenty vehicles. Deployed across high-demand areas of the Greater Madrid region. Operating under the supervision of an in-car vehicle specialist. The permit covers mapping, route validation and operational readiness testing, with commercial operations expected before the end of 2026 once the remaining operational and regulatory milestones are met. AVOMO, the autonomous vehicle division of Moove Cars Group, manages the fleet, drawing on roughly 400 vehicles it already runs in Austin and Atlanta.
Two details are doing the real work. This is the first national approval for WeRide’s GXR in the EU, which the companies correctly describe as a certification benchmark for the region. And it is WeRide’s ninth autonomous driving permit worldwide, and the fourth of the fifteen cities in the WeRide and Uber partnership, with another eleven to come by 2030.
In CW34 we set a standard for this kind of announcement: an international expansion is an ambition until the cities, the regulators and the local fleet partners are disclosed. Madrid clears it cleanly. Named regulator, named framework, named vehicle platform, named vehicle count, named operator with a track record, named scope of activity and a named commercial target. 
What it is not is a driverless service. “Spain’s first Level 4 permit” will be read by a good portion of the market as robotaxis on Madrid streets, and what has actually been authorised is twenty test vehicles with a specialist in the car. The asset here is the national permit in an EU member state, not the deployment.
Jennifer Li, WeRide’s CFO and Head of International, called Madrid “a key gateway for our European expansion.” Uber’s Sarfraz Maredia called it the move “from planning into real-world deployment.” Manuel Puga of Moove Cars Group gave the line closest to this newsletter’s standing position:
“Every successful autonomous vehicle deployment begins long before the first commercial ride.”
The other big European news this week came from Pony.ai.
Pony.ai goes driver-out in Zagreb
On 10 September, Pony.ai and Verne began fully driverless robotaxi test rides with passengers on public roads in Zagreb. Europe’s first.
The route is 22 kilometres, linking Verne’s headquarters and one of Zagreb’s main business districts with Franjo Tuđman Airport. The companies plan to expand the driverless routes over the coming months with the aim of covering the whole Zagreb operating area.
Since the commercial service launched in early April, the fleet has logged more than 200,000 kilometres, completed several thousand customer rides and holds an average passenger rating of 4.7 out of 5. The vehicle is Pony’s seventh-generation robotaxi, running a Pony-developed Level 4 domain controller built on NVIDIA DRIVE AGX and the safety-certified DriveOS operating system, with 360-degree sensor coverage, multiple redundancy layers and fail-operational capability. 
Marko Pejković, Verne’s CEO, marked the distance travelled: “Five months ago, we launched Europe’s first commercial Robotaxi service. We are now taking the next step, moving from autonomous driving with an onboard AV operator toward fully driverless operations.”
I should mark our own call here too. In CW35 we covered Zagreb becoming Europe’s first Uber-bookable AV service and described it as “a distribution milestone rather than an autonomy one,” because a licensed operator was still sitting behind the wheel. Weeks later, the seat is empty. The call was right and it has now been overtaken, which is the best outcome a dated call can have.
The qualifiers that still apply: these rides are invited, they are free, and they are not bookable through either the Verne app or Uber. The companies have not said when fully driverless rides will open to the general public.
Which leaves Europe with two things
Put the continent’s position together and it looks like this.
Europe has public, paying robotaxi rides that are supervised. Verne in Zagreb since April, and Wayve with Uber in London since 3 September. Real riders, real fares, and a trained human in the driver’s seat of every vehicle.
Europe has driverless passenger rides that are not public. Zagreb as of this week. Nobody on board, nobody driving remotely, and no way for an ordinary person to book one.
What Europe does not have is a single service that is driverless, open to the public and charging a fare at the same time. That is the thing Waymo has been running in the United States for years, and it is the rung nobody on this continent has reached.
I think we get there, and I do not think it takes as long as the last two years would suggest. The reason is that the two halves now exist separately, in the same city, run by the same partners. Verne is operating a commercial service with paying customers and simultaneously running driverless vehicles on a 22 kilometre corridor through the same city. Joining those two things is a regulatory step and an operational expansion, not a technical breakthrough. That is a much shorter distance than the one Europe has already covered.
I am genuinely looking forward to it. And I will note, because it is the honest thing to note, that the constraint on the date is paperwork rather than autonomy, which means the timing is in the hands of Croatian, Spanish and British regulators rather than the companies.
In other parts of the world, Pony is also pulling the driver out.
Pony’s fully autonomous rides in Doha
The second Autonomous e-Mobility Forum, hosted by Qatar’s Ministry of Transport, ran in Doha from 7 to 9 September. Pony.ai and Mowasalat used it to show Doha’s first fully driverless robotaxi rides, the first autonomous vehicle experience in the city without an onboard safety operator.
The programme’s history: Pony and Mowasalat began public-road testing in Doha in August 2025 and moved from testing to commercial operations within four months. Passengers book through Mowasalat’s Karwa app. The service covers Doha’s historic district, the central business areas and tourist attractions, plus connections to Hamad International Airport, serving daily commuting, business travel and tourism. Pony describes it as its first autonomous driving project to reach commercial deployment at scale outside China.
Mowasalat is not a startup partner. It operates Qatar’s largest network of taxis, buses and business transport, and sits at the centre of the country’s transport strategy. The project runs under Ministry of Transport supervision, tied to the sustainable mobility objectives of Qatar National Vision 2030. In an official update in July, the ministry said the programme had exceeded expectations on passenger satisfaction and safety, and that the technology and operating system were ready for wider deployment.
Keep the two things separate, because the press coverage does not. The commercial service on the Karwa app exists today and carries a safety operator. The driverless rides were a demonstration during a three-day forum. The ministry describes fully driverless commercial operations in Doha as the next stage, not the current one. The vehicle is Pony’s Gen 7, built for international markets with an explicit focus on lower cost and better operating efficiency ahead of broader deployment.
Pony now has operations across nine countries in the Middle East, Asia and Europe, with public passenger services running in Croatia, Singapore and Qatar. And here are the three forward numbers, which belong together rather than one at a time: an overseas deployment pipeline of more than 4,000 robotaxis, plans with Uber for more than 2,000 vehicles across Europe, and a target of more than 3,500 robotaxis globally by the end of 2026 across more than 20 cities.
And then Beijing put its own ambitions on paper
The pattern across Zurich, Madrid, Zagreb and Doha is not subtle. Chinese developers are treating autonomy as an export product, and their management says so on the record. This week the state said it too.
On Friday, China’s Ministry of Industry and Information Technology published the 15th five-year development plan for the intelligent connected new energy vehicle industry, covering 2026 to 2030. The document is dated 9 September and was jointly issued by nine government departments, including MIIT, the National Development and Reform Commission and the Ministry of Transport.
The headline targets are about electrification. New energy vehicles are to reach 70% of domestic new passenger-car sales and 40% of new commercial-vehicle sales by 2030, alongside large-scale deployment of vehicles with autonomous driving capability.
On autonomy specifically, the plan commits to highly automated driving on highways, urban expressways and some city roads by 2030, and to vehicles with autonomous driving systems substantially outperforming human drivers on safety, with mechanisms to assess the technology’s maturity. It calls for commercialisation demonstrations across autonomous passenger cars, buses, long-haul logistics and urban delivery, with an “orderly approach” to vehicle approvals and road access. 
National Level 3 and Level 4 standards are due to take effect from mid-2027. Rules tighten in parallel across vehicle production, road safety, self-driving systems and over-the-air software upgrades, with special approval pathways to be explored for key components and low-volume vehicles. Supporting infrastructure gets faster digital and connectivity upgrades in key first and second-tier cities and on selected national highways, to enable vehicle-road-cloud integration.
Autonomy is not the main subject of this plan, but it is unmistakably one of the priorities inside it. And it is treated as inseparable from everything around it: the driving standards, the chip supply, the battery chemistry, the charging build and the export policy all sit in the same document.
The second half of the document is something else entirely. It is a correction of the domestic market. Beijing will tighten oversight of vehicle and battery production capacity, impose strict conditions on projects establishing new standalone NEV manufacturers, and step up mergers, restructuring and cross-regional consolidation to phase out inefficient capacity. It calls for stronger antitrust, unfair-competition and pricing enforcement, and for curbs on local governments attracting investment through unauthorised subsidies, tax breaks and preferential land policies. It also requires equal treatment of domestic and foreign companies in government procurement, encourages Chinese and foreign firms to deepen cooperation on vehicle and component research, and opens pilot cooperation on cross-border data flows.
The timing tells you why that half of the document exists. The plan arrived in the eleventh consecutive month of decline in China’s domestic passenger car market, which fell 23.7% in August to 1.55 million vehicles while passenger vehicle exports rose 77.5% to a record 894,000 units. 
Read those together and the export drive is not only an offensive, it is also the pressure relief for a domestic market contracting fast, and the guidelines Beijing issued last week warning automakers against steep price cuts abroad suggest it knows what else could escape along with the volume.
The stated destination is explicit: several Chinese automakers among the global top ten by sales, suppliers among the world’s top hundred, greater international appeal for Chinese brands, and a louder Chinese voice inside international automotive standards bodies.
That last one is the quiet part. Vehicles are the visible export. Standards are the durable one.
One more thing happened this week, and it did not come from Beijing.
Roberto Vavassori, president of the Italian auto supplier lobby Anfia, asked Brussels for a wall: Chinese imports tariff-free up to 8% of annual European vehicle registrations, and 80% above that, covering components as well as finished vehicles. He is a lobbyist with a direct commercial interest in the answer, and the framing is still worth hearing. “We have maximum respect for what the Chinese industry has achieved. But that respect has now turned into fear.”
Now set that against the rest of this section. Spain’s traffic authority handed a Chinese stack the country’s first Level 4 permit. Croatia let one carry passengers with nobody driving. Zurich let one take the human out of the bus. And Italy’s supplier industry asked for an 80% tariff on Chinese vehicles and the parts inside them.
Europe opened its roads to Chinese autonomy and asked for protection from Chinese cars, in the same seven days. Both positions are coherent on their own terms. They are not answering the same question, and nobody in Brussels has yet been required to answer both at once. 
This newsletter has the other side on record too: asked on EP21 of Autonomy Insiders whether Europe should ban Chinese AVs, PAVE Europe’s Guido Di Pasquale gave the shortest possible answer, “short answer, no.” and his reasoning was practical rather than ideological. Europe does not yet have its own ADS stack at scale, so it needs Chinese and American technology on the road simply to make progress. Every deployed vehicle is also a learning opportunity for the city hosting it, and a start on a driver shortage he calls a real issue rather than a future one.
Leuven is his worked example: the Belgian deployment runs a Chinese provider because no alternative was available, and what the city gets is knowledge of how the vehicles behave, how to place them in its streets, how other road users react and how to train supervising staff.
Di Pasquale’s conclusion is the direct answer to what Anfia is asking for, and it goes the other way. The lever should be standards, not walls. Set the European rules, on data sovereignty and on treating these vehicles as critical infrastructure, and then enforce them: “these are our rules. If you want to deploy in Europe, you have to comply.”
So the two of them are closer on the goal than they sound and completely apart on the method. Both want Europe holding leverage over who drives on its roads. Vavassori wants that leverage at the border, as a price. Di Pasquale wants it as a condition of entry that binds everyone equally, including European companies, which is why he insists on data transparency “even if the technology is coming from China or elsewhere.”
Which is the same instrument China is using on the way out. Standards travel. Tariffs only work at your own border.
What this means and what to watch
First, Europe’s binding constraint is now demonstrably regulatory rather than technical, and the evidence arrived this week. A Chinese stack ran driverless in Croatia and took Spain’s first national Level 4 permit. Neither of those was gated on whether the software could drive. Both were gated on whether an authority had written the permission. Any forecast of European deployment that models technology readiness rather than permit throughput is modelling the wrong variable.
Second, watch Zagreb for the rung nobody has reached. The specific thing to watch is not route expansion, it is whether Pony and Verne open fully driverless rides to riders who book and pay through the Verne or Uber app. That single step would give Europe its first driverless, public, fare-charging service. 
Third, watch Madrid’s step from testing permit to commercial authorisation, and watch the GXR. Commercial operations are targeted before the end of 2026, which is less than four months away and entirely checkable. The vehicle approval is the more transferable asset: the GXR now has EU national approval in one member state, and how quickly that converts into approvals elsewhere is one to watch.
Fourth, on Doha, hold the distinction between demonstration and service. The commercial Karwa service is supervised. The driverless rides were a forum demonstration. The checkpoint is Qatar’s Ministry of Transport authorising fully driverless commercial operations, which it has described as the next stage without attaching a date. That date is the milestone, not the forum.

Waymo Lands on Lyft, and the Depot Tells the Story
Waymo going live on a second ride-hail platform is the headline. The depot underneath it is more interesting, and it arrives in the same week that Minneapolis put the jobs argument to a vote.
Waymo lands on Lyft: What actually launched
Riders in Nashville who open the Lyft app can be matched with a fully autonomous Waymo vehicle.
The mechanics are worth having precisely. Matching applies to Standard, Priority Pickup, Wait & Save and Extra Comfort rides, at no additional cost. The route has to start and end inside the Waymo service area on Lyft, which covers Downtown and Broadway, North Nashville and Germantown, East Nashville, Midtown and South Nashville. Riders can decline an autonomous ride or opt out of them entirely, and human drivers continue to serve the same area. Waymo has been running in Nashville on its own app since April, with more than a hundred vehicles across roughly 60 square miles.
Here is the part that is genuinely new. Nashville is the first market anywhere where Waymo vehicles are available on both the Waymo app and a partner app, and the two companies say they are “dispatching vehicles from a single fleet dynamically across both networks.”
Not a Waymo pool and a Lyft pool. One pool, two front doors.
Bloomberg framed the launch as Lyft “playing catch-up with rival Uber.” I think that reads the wrong variable. Uber has more than thirty autonomy partners and has committed over $10 billion to the category. Lyft has one Waymo city, one May Mobility city in Atlanta, a Baidu project in London and FreeNow in Europe. On partner count and capital deployed, Uber is ahead and it is not close.
But those are different bets, not the same bet run at different speeds. Uber deliberately spread its fleet operations, specifically so that no single operator could become a chokepoint. Lyft did the opposite. It bought the chokepoint in 2020 and has been building inside it ever since.
That subsidiary is Flexdrive, and it is the actual subject of this story.
The depot Lyft has been building for a year
Flexdrive is providing fleet management for Waymo in Nashville. It runs a provisional operations depot today, where staff clean, prep, repair and position the vehicles, and it will open a purpose-built 80,000 square foot depot in October.
We know an unusual amount about that building, because John Parks, Flexdrive’s CEO, described it on Autonomy Insiders in May [ https://substack.com/redirect/34805aea-e855-4e3c-94f8-e898f9f198ef?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]. Roughly 80,000 square feet. More than 40 charging ports. Office space, service bays, and some of Flexdrive’s central functions sitting on site so the people who manage the fleet remotely can watch what actually happens to it. Around a year to build, “millions of dollars,” and designed, in his words, for hundreds of vehicles rather than fifties.
Parks also gave us the taxonomy that explains why Lyft can do this at all. Lyft runs three models with AV partners:
One. The AV company owns the vehicles and keeps running the autonomous driving system, Flexdrive provides fleet management, and Lyft provides demand. That is Nashville with Waymo.
Two. Lyft, a Lyft subsidiary or a strategic partner owns the vehicle, the AV partner provides ongoing service, Flexdrive manages the fleet and Lyft provides demand. The most vertically integrated version. That is the Baidu arrangement in London, and it is also the shape of the Dallas deal we covered back in CW8, where Mobileye supplies the driver, the Japanese trading house Marubeni finances and owns the fleet, and Flexdrive operates it.
Three. The AV partner does everything, and Lyft is purely the demand layer. That is May Mobility in Atlanta.
His reasoning for keeping all three open: “everybody’s at different phases of their development of their AV technology. There may be some things they always will want to keep in house and there’s some things that they may never want to have in house.”
That flexibility is the product. It is also why Flexdrive is structurally different from Avis, Moove, Transdev or Element, all of which are genuinely excellent at fleet operations and none of which own a demand platform.
Now the number everyone is going to quote
The Nashville operation employs 72 people, and the purpose-built depot has not even opened yet. Roughly half of them are former Lyft drivers. Across Flexdrive as a whole, currently operating in 30 US cities, former drivers make up 35% of the workforce.
The individual outcomes look genuinely good. Jonathan Baines started driving for Lyft in 2016 to support himself as a singer-songwriter. He now runs fleet operations at the Nashville depot, overseeing the teams that prepare the vehicles. He told WIRED it is the best operations job he has ever had, and “more fun than retail.”
Lyft describes the roles as skilled technicians, fleet operations leads and coordinators, and service coordinators. Parks called them high-tech jobs with transferable skills. On that evidence I think these are probably better jobs than the driving they replaced. There is no wage data anywhere in this. Not from Lyft, not from Waymo, not in any of the coverage. What we can say with confidence is that the shape of the earnings changed, from independent-contractor gig income to salaried employment. 
Why the former drivers keep showing up, and why it is a moat
The question is why Lyft is filling the jobs with former drivers.
Parks answered this in May, and his answer is the strategic point hiding inside the human-interest story:
“If you really want to understand your customers and understand their pain points and their needs, no better way to understand that than to go hire those people to come and work for you. And so they understood the value of customer service, what it means to have a rider in the back of your car.”
And then the mechanism:
“FlexDrive is the best kept secret at Lyft. We didn’t advertise a lot. The way you find FlexDrive is you want to drive on the platform.”
That is a hiring funnel, not a retraining programme. Former drivers apply in volume because Flexdrive recruits inside an ecosystem it already owns, and it has been doing that since long before autonomy showed up. The 35% company-wide figure predates the AV business entirely.
Staffing an AV depot requires locally resident, pre-screened people who already understand rider service and high-mileage vehicle operations, in a city you may have entered six months ago. Lyft can source that from an existing relationship with its own driver base. 
Parks made the connection explicit, and it is the best articulation of the operations thesis this newsletter archive holds:
“We’re not just cleaning cars. We’re preparing cars to have a rider in the back seat of it and have this just unbelievable experience of riding in a car with no one driving it. It’s the same thing they tried to deliver as drivers every day when a passenger got in the back seat of their car.”
Why one fleet and two apps actually works, and what it costs Lyft
The clearest explanation of why this structure makes sense came from Harry Campbell  of The Driverless Digest, who joined me on EP11 of Autonomy Insiders to argue that the Uber and Waymo relationship was deteriorating.
His read is that Waymo gets the best of both channels. It keeps serving the riders who specifically want a Waymo and will wait longer or pay more for it, while tapping Lyft’s much larger customer base during lower-demand periods. Premium demand on its own app, utilisation from somebody else’s. That is the economic logic behind a single dynamically dispatched fleet, and it is why this arrangement is cleaner than the one Waymo has with Uber.
Then the cost, which Campbell calls the Trojan horse problem. A Lyft rider gets matched with a Waymo, has a very good experience, and starts booking through the Waymo app directly. Lyft has effectively paid to introduce its own customers to a competitor’s front door.
He thinks both platforms simply have to live with it, and his conclusion is the one worth carrying:
“It’s tough for them to compete with Waymo on the actual ride experience, so their advantage needs to come from lower prices, shorter ETAs and better reliability.”
Two answers to one problem, and Uber is giving the other one
Set Lyft’s approach next to Uber’s, because they are responding to the same pressure in opposite directions.
Lyft’s answer is operational: absorb the transition into the business, retrain drivers into the depot, run driver forums in Nashville, keep the hybrid network running.
Uber’s answer is regulatory. As we covered in CW30, Uber has been lobbying in Washington DC and New Jersey for a hybrid-network mandate, including circulated language that would require human drivers to handle 85% of rides during a three-year pilot period. That would effectively bar Waymo, Zoox and Tesla from operating their own apps at scale in those jurisdictions. Uber’s own May policy paper concedes that driver utilisation and earnings are already declining in San Francisco and Los Angeles, and Uber COO Andrew Macdonald described the company’s position as “ironic” on the record.
One company is absorbing the transition. The other is trying to legislate a delay in it. Both are rational responses to the same problem, and the difference is that Lyft owns an operations business that benefits from the transition happening and Uber does not.
Which brings us to the place where somebody else is deciding.
Minneapolis wants a paid human “safety monitor” in the driver’s seat
Four Minneapolis city council members have introduced an ordinance that would require every autonomous vehicle operating in the city to carry a paid human “safety monitor” in the driver’s seat, and would require AV companies to obtain a city licence to operate starting next autumn.
The authors are Jason Chavez, Aurin Chowdhury, Robin Wonsley and Jamal Osman. The measure, first reported by the Star Tribune, gets its first public hearing this month.
Wonsley’s framing of the motive is unusually direct. The authors are focused, she said in a statement, on “public safety and protecting immigrant workers from losing their jobs to automation.”
“Without it, Minneapolis workers and residents are vulnerable to all the negative impacts of robotaxis without any regulations or accountability mechanisms in place.”
Waymo, which began testing in the Twin Cities last November, called the proposal “a de facto ban on autonomous vehicles.”  Adam Lane, Waymo’s state and local public policy manager, added that the company “remains committed to working with officials on a path forward that offers Minnesotans the option to choose fully autonomous transportation.”
An old acquaintance returns
The third thread this week is a name most of this readership has not had to think about in a while.
The Financial Times reports that Travis Kalanick is preparing a return to ride-hailing by developing robotaxi technology at Atoms, his new venture, and reuniting with former members of Uber’s autonomous vehicle team to do it.
The confirmed facts are these. Atoms raised $1.7 billion in July, led by Andreessen Horowitz. Uber has invested $100 million in the company, a figure TechCrunch has separately confirmed. In March, Atoms acquired Pronto, the heavy-industry and mining automation startup founded by Anthony Levandowski. Atoms itself is a rebadging of City Storage Systems, Kalanick’s holding company, which also contains CloudKitchens. It has more than 2,000 employees across its arms, most of them in the food business, with a growing number working on autonomous vehicles.
The reported parts, sourced to people familiar with the plans, go further. A hiring spree, including potential acquisitions, aimed at autonomous engineering talent. Preliminary talks with Uber about using Atoms technology for robotaxis on the Uber network. Levandowski brought in to lead the autonomous vehicle work, including robotaxi development. Several dozen former Uber employees hired, plus recruits from Zoox, Tesla and Waymo. Former Uber CFO Gautam Gupta is now Atoms CFO. Eric Meyhofer, who succeeded Levandowski as Uber’s robotaxi development chief, runs Lab37, the kitchen automation arm.
And then there is what the company says on the record, which is a flat denial:
“We have no plans to enter the saturated robotaxi market. Uber is a partner of Atoms and can use our technology for its ridesharing business if it is helpful.”
Atoms describes itself as “an industrial software company focused on industrial applications.” Uber declined to comment.
Both things can be partially true. Building an autonomy stack for industrial and mining applications and licensing it to a ride-hail platform is not the same as entering the robotaxi market, and the FT’s own sources stress that robotaxis are not the whole of the plan. The model being described, build the technology and not the vehicle, is the Wayve and Nuro model, and neither of those companies operates a robotaxi service either.
The detail I find most interesting is not Kalanick and it is not Levandowski. It is that Dara Khosrowshahi and Kalanick remain in regular contact, and that the two were in talks in 2025 about a deal involving the US arm of Pony.ai. Those talks fizzled. But it tells you that Uber’s supply strategy has been ranging considerably wider than its published partner list suggests.
The rest is colour. Saudi Arabia’s Public Investment Fund is among the backers, alongside Bain Capital Ventures, Fifth Wall and SV Angel, with debt from JPMorgan and Goldman Sachs. Ben Horowitz called it “certainly the biggest cheque I’ve ever written.” Kalanick’s memo announcing the venture was titled “Unfinished business,” which is not the title you choose if you have stopped thinking about the company you founded.
What this means and what to watch
Watch whether one fleet and two apps gets repeated anywhere else. Nashville is the only market where Waymo vehicles are dispatched dynamically across its own app and a partner’s, from a single pool. If that structure shows up in a second city, it is a template and Lyft has something durable. If it stays a Nashville arrangement, it was a local deal that suited one market. The next launch announcement tells you which.
Second, watch who fills the seats that are currently empty. When Waymo opened Denver, San Diego and Tampa on 1 September, it disclosed a fleet operator for one of them. San Diego went to Element. Denver and Tampa were announced without one. Those open slots are the cleanest test of whether Nashville was a template or a one-off. If Flexdrive takes one and the vehicles also show up on the Lyft app, the integrated position is real and it travels. 
On Atoms, hold the denial and watch the hiring. A company genuinely uninterested in robotaxis does not put the former head of Uber’s self-driving programme in charge of robotaxi development. Watch for an acquisition of an AV team, and watch whether Uber’s $100 million turns into a commercial agreement. If it does, Uber will have a supplier founded by its own founder.

Einride announced a partnership with UNIQAI Systems to bring AI-driven mission planning and operational decision-making to its autonomous logistics platform for defense and national security customers. The structure is the part worth noting. Einride is treating its own driving stack as the base and buying specialised layers on top of it, situational protection from one partner and mission-level intelligence from another, rather than building either itself. Ytterhälla was founded in 1991 in Västerås, employs 36 people, and grew revenue from SEK 52.05 million to SEK 63.55 million with EBIT of SEK 5.63 million and an 8.9% operating margin.
