Intel Earnings
--------------

I’m a bit late to this, but it’s been a busy few weeks! From Bloomberg ( https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-29/intel-outlook-hurt-by-weak-market-for-personal-computer-chips ) two weeks ago:

Intel Corp. suffered its worst stock rout in more than four decades after the PC industry slump and supply-chain snags hurt its forecast, renewing fears about the chipmaker’s turnaround bid. Sales in the fourth quarter will be $15 billion to $16 billion, the chipmaker said in a statement on Thursday. Analysts on average estimated $17 billion, and some projections topped $18 billion, according to data compiled by Bloomberg. Profit will be 25 cents a share in the quarter, excluding certain items. Wall Street was projecting adjusted earnings of 43 cents a share.

Intel is contending with a severe slump in the personal computer market. Makers of laptops and desktops — key customers — are struggling with shortages of memory semiconductors and surging component prices. That’s cut into PC shipments and hurt demand from users. In addition, Chief Executive Officer Lip-Bu Tan said his company’s data center unit would have done even better if it hadn’t suffered from shortages of some raw materials and a shutdown of a factory in Israel — supply snags that the company is working hard to fix.

CEO Lip-Bu Tan said in his opening remarks on the earnings call ( https://seekingalpha.com/article/4959834-intel-corporation-intc-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript ) :

I also want to acknowledge headwinds limiting our progress to meet this extremely high AI-driven demand. First, Intel 3 yield and cycle time continue to improve, but I am demanding significant execution from the team. Second, even as our Intel 3 wafer output ramps quickly, an industry-wide shortage of advanced organic substrates for our EMIB packaging is also impacting our ability to scale output. Finally, early this month, an unfortunate and tragic accident led to an extended shutdown in our Israel factory. While the teams have worked quickly to address the situation, it will impact our Q4 10-nanometer server shipments…

Turning to our client business. In recent weeks, our customers began signaling a PC slowdown based on the memory prices and availability. Year-to-date system endpoint prices have increased significantly placing additional pressure on already limited IT budgets. The pace of this PC correction has accelerated over the last 2 months, as our OEM partners are aggressively managing inventory to match the softer sell-through. We expect the current environment to persist until memory availability and prices begin to normalize.

While the next few quarters will be challenging for the PC market, our road map continues to strengthen and our long-term outlook remains positive. We have successfully ramped Panther Lake on Intel 18A and demand for the Wildcat Lake is outstripping supply. Nova Lake, our first Intel 18A-P client product, is tracking ahead of schedule, with design wins expected to be twice those of Panther Lake at launch. We look forward to sharing more at CES in January. As the market normalizes, we expect this portfolio drives meaningful share gains.

Intel’s results made the market very concerned about AMD specifically, and the AI trade generally: where was the promised agentic-driven CPU demand?

AMD Earnings
------------

Again from Bloomberg ( https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-03/amd-sales-forecast-tops-estimates-on-soaring-ai-chip-demand ) , this time last week:

Advanced Micro Devices Inc., a key challenger to Nvidia Corp. in artificial intelligence chips, gave a sales forecast that soared past analysts’ projections, a sign of buoyant demand for data center processors. Fourth-quarter revenue will be $17 billion, plus or minus 3%, the chipmaker said in a statement Tuesday. That easily topped the average analyst estimate of $16.3 billion, helping send the shares up about 7% in late trading. The outlook provided fresh evidence that AI spending is sustainable, soothing jittery investors. Shares of chipmakers and other tech companies have been battered in recent weeks over concerns about a bubble in the AI infrastructure industry.

CEO Lisa Su said in her opening remarks on the earnings call ( https://seekingalpha.com/article/4961857-advanced-micro-devices-inc-amd-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript ) :

Data center revenue grew 97% year-over-year to a record $8.5 billion. In server, both cloud and enterprise sales more than doubled year-over-year. We significantly exceeded our previous outlook of more than 80% growth as we gained revenue share…Cloud providers also added significant EPYC capacity in the quarter for personal and enterprise agents, which are quickly becoming the next killer app for CPUs in the cloud. Meta’s Muse and OpenAI’s Dots illustrate how quickly personal agents are growing with more than 10 million people activating Muse agents in the first 6 weeks. Enterprises are moving fast as well, putting agents to work across software development, supply chain and IT operations. These agents work continuously and in parallel across applications and enterprise systems, adding to accelerator demand while creating a substantial increase in CPU demand.

Ah, there it is. As for consumer:

Looking at the market, PC sell-through slowed late in the quarter as higher memory and component costs weighed on demand. We are managing the business accordingly, aligning sell-in with sell-through to keep customer and channel inventory healthy. In 2027, we are planning for PC market unit shipments to decline by a double-digit percentage. In this environment, we expect to outperform the market as we deliver our Ryzen road map and grow our commercial footprint.

Note the difference in how Su talks about inventory: Intel is at the mercy of its customers declining to stock more PCs until the ones they have are sold; AMD is aligning their supply with customers’ demand. That difference is more profound than it seems.

Intel and AMD’s Divergence
--------------------------

The simple story behind these earnings is that Intel has a lot of consumer chips and not enough server chips; the consumer market is crashing and the server market is surging, and Intel is missing out. Why, though, is that good for AMD? How are they managing the consumer slowdown and seamlessly selling to the server market? The answer, as is so often the case with chips, goes back many years.

Intel was, famously, an integrated device manufacturer, or IDM; this meant that they both designed the chip and manufactured it. That is still by-and-large the case today, but Intel is trying to peel those pieces apart: today some Intel designed chips are made by TSMC, and they would desperately like to make chips for other designers.

AMD used to be an integrated device manufacturer, which is no surprise given the company was founded in 1969, when that was the only way to be in the chip business (and, as founder Jerry Sanders famously said, “Real men have fabs”). That story started to change in 2008 when AMD spun out its foundry business as GlobalFoundries; it truly ended when AMD’s leading edge chip fabrication moved from GlobalFoundries to TSMC in 2018.

Throughout this time period — right up until 2018, actually, when TSMC launched its 7nm node — Intel had the best manufacturing, and its design arm leaned heavily on that advantage, as I explained in 2018’s Intel and the Danger of Integration ( https://stratechery.com/2018/intel-and-the-danger-of-integration/?access_token=eyJhbGciOiJFUzI1NiIsImtpZCI6IkxzanFRcEhoMUJUdmU0djJQUUVuejIiLCJ0eXAiOiJKV1QifQ.eyJhdWQiOiJzdHJhdGVjaGVyeS5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUiLCJhenAiOiJIS0xjUzREd1Nod1AyWURLYmZQV00xIiwiZW50Ijp7InVyaSI6WyJodHRwczovL3N0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LmNvbS8yMDE4L2ludGVsLWFuZC10aGUtZGFuZ2VyLW9mLWludGVncmF0aW9uLyJdfSwiZXhwIjoxNzk2OTg2ODg1LCJpYXQiOjE3OTQzOTQ4ODUsImlzcyI6Imh0dHBzOi8vYXBwLnBhc3Nwb3J0Lm9ubGluZS9vYXV0aCIsInNjb3BlIjoiZmVlZDpyZWFkIGFydGljbGU6cmVhZCBhc3NldDpyZWFkIGNhdGVnb3J5OnJlYWQgZW50aXRsZW1lbnRzIiwic3ViIjoiMDE5NTE2ZjEtYTM0Ni03NjE0LTllOTYtYjYzYmQxMWZlODU2IiwidXNlIjoiYWNjZXNzIn0.1K6tBZw5HhreDWnDak4u-ArQIj3wR0h3Us50j0aakGYoqyjoH31g0gdeE-eoCb2yBKj46B8dmIYooolxDeziaA ). Intel was integrated in another way: the company’s design approach was monolithic, which meant that all of the major processor components were fabricated on a single piece of silicon. This meant that when Intel designed different chips for servers and consumers — which obviously they had to — the entire chip was different.

AMD, meanwhile, had to approach the problem in a modular fashion, because they didn’t control their own manufacturing. It turned out, however, that a modular approach could be extended beyond separating design from manufacturing: you could actually make the chip itself modular. The two primary components of a multi-core chip are compute and I/O; AMD’s 2019 Zen 2 (consumer) and EPYC Rome (server) used TSMC for the compute chips and GlobalFoundries for the I/O chip (a processor has multiple compute chips but usually only one I/O chip). There is a real trade-off here: a chiplet approach like AMD took has additional latency and energy overhead; AMD made up for it by focusing on making one super awesome processor design and using it for both the consumer and server markets. The I/O chip provided the real differentiation, like number of memory controllers, PCIe interfaces, etc.

Right there you can see the seeds of what bloomed into last quarter’s contrasting results: AMD can repurpose consumer compute chiplets (from their desktop processors, anyways) for servers, because all of their chiplets are mostly the same — the server chips just use a lot more of them!

Intel, on the other hand, has moved to chiplets, but like so many other aspects of the company, cannot escape the overhang of their past success. Their consumer compute dies are different from their enterprise ones (although they share the same microarchitectures). This is precisely the problem, because Intel can’t shift chiplets from the plummeting consumer market to the thriving server market. This came up on the earnings call:

*Does the PC weakness give you the opportunity to reallocate wafers and sort of address some of this and accelerate that transition?*

*CFO David Zinsner:* The answer is yes. What we’re taking offline to put Intel 3 into Oregon is 18A wafers, which we’re generally going to build Panther Lake products. So we see the opportunity with some of that capacity freed up to go produce more data center wafers.

Notice the difference: AMD can just reallocate already-made chiplets from consumer to server; Intel needs to overhaul an entire line.

The EMIB issue is a variation on this theme. EMIB is a packaging technology that much more tightly integrates the compute chips with the I/O via very dense high-bandwidth connections that flow through silicon bridges in an organic substrate; AMD uses much more conventional packaging technology that lays the compute chips around a centralized I/O chip. This is simpler and more scalable, but it has costs in terms of latency and energy overhead. In other words, as is ever the case ( https://stratechery.com/2022/an-interview-with-father-of-the-ipod-tony-fadell/?access_token=eyJhbGciOiJFUzI1NiIsImtpZCI6IkxzanFRcEhoMUJUdmU0djJQUUVuejIiLCJ0eXAiOiJKV1QifQ.eyJhdWQiOiJzdHJhdGVjaGVyeS5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUiLCJhenAiOiJIS0xjUzREd1Nod1AyWURLYmZQV00xIiwiZW50Ijp7InVyaSI6WyJodHRwczovL3N0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LmNvbS8yMDIyL2FuLWludGVydmlldy13aXRoLWZhdGhlci1vZi10aGUtaXBvZC10b255LWZhZGVsbC8iXX0sImV4cCI6MTc5Njk4Njg4NSwiaWF0IjoxNzk0Mzk0ODg1LCJpc3MiOiJodHRwczovL2FwcC5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUvb2F1dGgiLCJzY29wZSI6ImZlZWQ6cmVhZCBhcnRpY2xlOnJlYWQgYXNzZXQ6cmVhZCBjYXRlZ29yeTpyZWFkIGVudGl0bGVtZW50cyIsInN1YiI6IjAxOTUxNmYxLWEzNDYtNzYxNC05ZTk2LWI2M2JkMTFmZTg1NiIsInVzZSI6ImFjY2VzcyJ9.BlRdMcMCI7Xpw9b4y1W4Mbj-i6B5eRcVBt-sf52WntbS1i31bBljJwZy0zaj9nBoHCqeab2VKfqeOTstMI8hCA ) , Intel prioritized performance and assumed its manufacturing could make it happen (Intel also, in the case of Granite Rapids, put memory controllers on the compute tiles; AMD puts them on the I/O chip); AMD, by necessity, prioritized modularity and established processes. And, once again, it’s the AMD approach that is working out. Intel’s server chip problems are exacerbated by EMIB issues, while AMD happily fills in the gap with standard interconnect technology.

It is always fun to take a trip down memory lane; the worry for Intel, however, is that these trips usually function as a reminder that no matter how much you might change strategy, or even culture, it’s dramatically harder to change your nature ( https://stratechery.com/2017/microsofts-monopoly-hangover/?access_token=eyJhbGciOiJFUzI1NiIsImtpZCI6IkxzanFRcEhoMUJUdmU0djJQUUVuejIiLCJ0eXAiOiJKV1QifQ.eyJhdWQiOiJzdHJhdGVjaGVyeS5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUiLCJhenAiOiJIS0xjUzREd1Nod1AyWURLYmZQV00xIiwiZW50Ijp7InVyaSI6WyJodHRwczovL3N0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LmNvbS8yMDE3L21pY3Jvc29mdHMtbW9ub3BvbHktaGFuZ292ZXIvIl19LCJleHAiOjE3OTY5ODY4ODUsImlhdCI6MTc5NDM5NDg4NSwiaXNzIjoiaHR0cHM6Ly9hcHAucGFzc3BvcnQub25saW5lL29hdXRoIiwic2NvcGUiOiJmZWVkOnJlYWQgYXJ0aWNsZTpyZWFkIGFzc2V0OnJlYWQgY2F0ZWdvcnk6cmVhZCBlbnRpdGxlbWVudHMiLCJzdWIiOiIwMTk1MTZmMS1hMzQ2LTc2MTQtOWU5Ni1iNjNiZDExZmU4NTYiLCJ1c2UiOiJhY2Nlc3MifQ.C-xIT6uPbCiq9CCriJrLUwtTM2EoNEkwndNKXfUJpcCyjz7C5T27exO29ufclvYp79rVnA_OeFc6sQ5qmMJLKA ). To that end, one obvious conclusion is that Intel needs to be more like AMD.

I increasingly wonder, however, if Intel actually needs to be more like Intel, which is to say an IDM — specifically an IDM obsessed with performance. The foundry idea makes tons of sense strategically; at what point, however, is it viewed as not just a failure for its inability to win customers, but also as a distraction that is actually making everything worse? The real issue isn’t that Intel can’t repurpose consumer chips — again, their approach has merit — but that their server lines aren’t producing enough volume. Separating design and manufacturing assumes that both can stand alone; Intel’s best chance may be in realizing they can’t. Or, to put it another way, AMD was for many years defined as not-Intel; one wonders if Intel’s ultimate salvation may come in realizing they can’t be AMD — or TSMC.
