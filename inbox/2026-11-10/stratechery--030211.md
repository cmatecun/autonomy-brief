The Jensen Huang Story Gets Worse
---------------------------------

The Jensen Huang story got considerably worse for him and Nvidia on Monday. From the New York Times ( https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/09/business/economy/nvidia-megaspeed-investigation-huang.html ) :

Investigators at the Commerce Department were deep into a monthslong investigation of a Singaporean company that they suspected of smuggling Nvidia’s artificial intelligence chips into China when their work came to a sudden halt late last year. The investigation centered on Megaspeed, a data center company led by an executive who U.S. officials now believe to be a Chinese spy who had a relationship with Nvidia’s chief executive, Jensen Huang. But the case was abruptly transferred to the Justice Department last year. The shift came after Commerce Department investigators working on the case received an email from Nvidia’s general counsel seeking information about the inquiry into Megaspeed.

From Bloomberg ( https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-09/nvidia-intervened-in-us-probe-of-megaspeed-chip-smuggling ) :

In late summer 2025, Nvidia’s Tim Teter called Commerce Under Secretary Jeffrey Kessler, who oversees US chip export curbs, to ask about the Megaspeed investigation, according to people familiar with the matter. Specifically, Teter wanted to know whether Kessler’s Bureau of Industry and Security was involved in the Singapore government’s recent detention of Alice Huang, a Megaspeed executive, according to the people. Singapore police had brought Alice in for questioning and restricted her from leaving the country. BIS was not involved in that action, said the people, who spoke on condition of anonymity owing to the sensitivity of the matter.

It’s unclear whether Teter made any specific requests of Kessler related to his agency’s investigation into Megaspeed, which was separate from Singapore’s. But in the weeks that followed, BIS staff were muzzled in their ability to look into the company, including a mandate that they route all requests of Nvidia through Kessler or seek his approval for outreach — a highly unusual step that remains unique to the chipmaker, the people said. They were also told not to mention Megaspeed or Speedmatrix, the company’s Malaysian subsidiary, in external communications.

From the Financial Times ( https://www.ft.com/content/cbabf2da-2079-4b69-88e3-0ba84cf8d6fd?syn-25a6b1a6=1 ) :

Donald Trump said the FBI was “looking into” Jensen Huang after the Nvidia chief executive admitted to an affair with a woman the US believes is a Chinese spy…Nvidia’s board also announced it had opened an investigation. On Capitol Hill, lawmakers have also called for investigations into the matter. Hakeem Jeffries, the Democratic leader in the House, told reporters on Monday: “There are real concerns that need to be addressed with respect to the Nvidia situation.”

One question lawmakers are expected to examine is whether other members of the company knew that Huang had carried on an affair with the Megaspeed executive. As recently as last week, Tim Teter, Nvidia’s general counsel, insisted inside the company that claims of an affair were inaccurate, according to people familiar with the situation.

From Wired ( https://www.wired.com/story/nvidia-ceo-jensen-huang-hired-a-pr-firm-known-for-handling-sex-scandals-last-year/ ) :

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang began working with a crisis communications firm known for handling public scandals a year before he admitted to having an extramarital affair with a woman suspected by US intelligence of being a Chinese spy. The firm, Heller, has handled Huang’s behind-the-scenes communications since at least October 2025, according to two sources familiar with the matter…

In 2024, Huang began appearing in public with a woman who goes by Alice Huang, a former executive at the Singapore-based chip buyer Megaspeed, according ( https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/08/technology/nvidia-investigation-jensen-huang-china.html ) to The New York Times. Megaspeed was created when it split off from a Chinese gaming and cloud computing firm in 2023, a few months after the US government began restricting ( https://www.wired.com/story/us-chip-sanctions-kneecap-chinas-tech-industry/ ) exports of advanced semiconductors to China, including those developed by Nvidia. A subsidiary of Megaspeed later became a major buyer of Nvidia chips.

In early October 2025, Jensen Huang’s business dealings with Megaspeed and his connection to Alice became public in a lengthy New York Times report ( https://www.nytimes.com/2025/10/09/technology/nvidia-chips-china-megaspeed.html ) , which alleged that the US Commerce Department was investigating whether the company was helping Chinese firms evade American export controls through its subsidiaries. The report caused tensions to rise within Nvidia’s top ranks, and Jensen was rankled by the story, according to sources. By the end of that month, Nvidia’s internal communications teams had been instructed to include Heller’s firm on any press inquiries about Huang, whether personal or professional, according to people familiar with the matter.

The order of these stories matters — and the extent to which it matters roughly depends on what city you are in (spiritually, if not physically).

Washington vs. New York
-----------------------

The order I listed these four stories — all of which were published yesterday — is ranked by what I perceive to be the most scandalous by Washington D.C. The first suggests that Nvidia was interfering with a Commerce Department investigation of Megaspeed, and the second suggests that Nvidia was doing favors for Megaspeed for an issue they — Alice Huang specifically, in fact — were having in Singapore. Was Jensen Huang calling in favors for his girlfriend? Oh, and per that second story, why did Nvidia severely underreport how many GPUs Megaspeed had acquired?

In the months after Teter’s initial call with Kessler, Nvidia gave BIS an incomplete account of its business with Megaspeed. After several visits to Megaspeed’s data centers — 12 facilities owned by eight companies across three countries in Southeast Asia — Nvidia had sent inventory checks to the agency and declared to Bloomberg News in mid-November 2025 that everything was where it should be. The company also said at the time that it had asked the US government whether it needed to cut off business with Megaspeed, and was told no.

But Nvidia’s tabulations, as relayed to the US government, undercounted the number of chips it had sold to Megaspeed via hardware partners by 50,000 units, according to trade records analyzed by Bloomberg. That’s worth billions of dollars. After being pressed about the gap, Nvidia told Bloomberg in December 2025 that it had checked additional Megaspeed warehouses that contained the missing hardware, while declining to state exactly how many chips it found in those facilities or provide any further details.

These stories look pretty bad, make no mistake, but I’m not sure they will, in-and-of-themselves, lead to Huang losing his job. The fact of the matter is that he is, as I wrote yesterday ( https://stratechery.com/2026/jensen-huangs-affair-the-culper-report-huangs-righteousness/?access_token=eyJhbGciOiJFUzI1NiIsImtpZCI6IkxzanFRcEhoMUJUdmU0djJQUUVuejIiLCJ0eXAiOiJKV1QifQ.eyJhdWQiOiJzdHJhdGVjaGVyeS5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUiLCJhenAiOiJIS0xjUzREd1Nod1AyWURLYmZQV00xIiwiZW50Ijp7InVyaSI6WyJodHRwczovL3N0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LmNvbS8yMDI2L2plbnNlbi1odWFuZ3MtYWZmYWlyLXRoZS1jdWxwZXItcmVwb3J0LWh1YW5ncy1yaWdodGVvdXNuZXNzLyJdfSwiZXhwIjoxNzk2OTAwNTMxLCJpYXQiOjE3OTQzMDg1MzEsImlzcyI6Imh0dHBzOi8vYXBwLnBhc3Nwb3J0Lm9ubGluZS9vYXV0aCIsInNjb3BlIjoiZmVlZDpyZWFkIGFydGljbGU6cmVhZCBhc3NldDpyZWFkIGNhdGVnb3J5OnJlYWQgZW50aXRsZW1lbnRzIiwic3ViIjoiMDE5NTE2ZjEtYTM0Ni03NjE0LTllOTYtYjYzYmQxMWZlODU2IiwidXNlIjoiYWNjZXNzIn0.k0xiKYT42A9KePAEoqf29BTcD0peMKcgO8poukI4pmxntVo3XujkvhS1Cjh1IYVaH10-M36Vk9OByOIed8nrrQ ) , essential to Nvidia, essential to the broader AI build-out, and losing him would be a massive blow to both. Moreover, Nvidia’s board almost certainly has no interest in firing Huang: the entire company is built around him. That, however, is where the New York perspective comes in — and where the Wired PR agency story actually looms as the most important. Jerry Capital put it very well on X ( https://x.com/jerrycap/status/2119968001256542508?s=46 ) :

If Jensen knew the relationship created a serious conflict, hired outside crisis advisers, allowed NVIDIA to publicly characterize the relationship as routine business, and did not disclose the romantic relationship to the board, then this stops being primarily an affair and becomes a corporate-governance/deception issue. The board's current investigation is explicitly empowered to examine all available information, even though it presently says it has no reason to believe company secrets or national security were compromised ( https://x.com/jerrycap/status/2119968001256542508?s=46 )

When I say “New York” I mean investors; when I hear “corporate-governance/deception issue” I envision shareholder lawsuits ( one has already been filed ( https://www.reuters.com/world/nvidia-ceo-huang-sued-over-disclosures-related-suspected-chinese-spy-2026-11-09/ ) ) and, critically, potential liability for the board of directors themselves. Washington D.C. may be convinced that a crime occurred; what seems more likely to sink Huang is the cover-up. It’s pretty stunning in retrospect that Nvidia’s chief counsel and spokesman ( https://x.com/lulumeservey/status/2119854872019497174 ) were either lying or lied to as late as last week; who else knew the truth, or should have? And when did they — particularly the board — know it?

Keep in mind Huang started Nvidia in 1993, long before the idea of founder control and dual-class shares were a thing in Silicon Valley. He owns 3.58% of the company, which certainly makes him very rich; it also makes him very fireable. And, if the Board of Directors is suddenly cast in a position of it being Huang or them, are they really going to choose Huang — even if, you could argue, that is actually in the fiduciary interest of shareholders?

Silicon Valley
--------------

The last city is San Francisco, or, more accurately, Silicon Valley broadly, and I expressed my sentiment yesterday: I think it is critical for the industry and the U.S. that Huang stay at Nvidia. My view is, by-and-large, is shared by the startup ecosystem, for a few reasons:

* First, I think the VC world generally is much more biased towards the importance of singular individuals, and there are few individuals in the history of Silicon Valley more singular than Huang.
* Second, to the extent startups access GPUs directly, they are mostly going to prefer Nvidia chips, because of CUDA.
* Third, startups are very threatened by the frontier labs, both in terms of encroaching on their markets and also in terms of eating their margins; that means it is the startup ecosystem that cares the most about there being open weight models, and Huang is the biggest advocate of open weight models — and for largely the same reasons! Nvidia doesn’t want to be a mere supplier to hyperscalers or the frontier labs either.

The neolabs, meanwhile, are entirely dependent on Nvidia, not only for chips, but also for financing and cheaper credit. Any slowdown in Nvidia innovation will hurt their value proposition; any break in Nvidia’s Risky Business ( https://stratechery.com/2026/nvidias-risky-business/?access_token=eyJhbGciOiJFUzI1NiIsImtpZCI6IkxzanFRcEhoMUJUdmU0djJQUUVuejIiLCJ0eXAiOiJKV1QifQ.eyJhdWQiOiJzdHJhdGVjaGVyeS5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUiLCJhenAiOiJIS0xjUzREd1Nod1AyWURLYmZQV00xIiwiZW50Ijp7InVyaSI6WyJodHRwczovL3N0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LmNvbS8yMDI2L252aWRpYXMtcmlza3ktYnVzaW5lc3MvIl19LCJleHAiOjE3OTY5MDA1MzEsImlhdCI6MTc5NDMwODUzMSwiaXNzIjoiaHR0cHM6Ly9hcHAucGFzc3BvcnQub25saW5lL29hdXRoIiwic2NvcGUiOiJmZWVkOnJlYWQgYXJ0aWNsZTpyZWFkIGFzc2V0OnJlYWQgY2F0ZWdvcnk6cmVhZCBlbnRpdGxlbWVudHMiLCJzdWIiOiIwMTk1MTZmMS1hMzQ2LTc2MTQtOWU5Ni1iNjNiZDExZmU4NTYiLCJ1c2UiOiJhY2Nlc3MifQ.FgBfB9UkXkYvYofa1g5cP5pBCObaGyHpEbDVIuZAakiTawROMFhpeSU3pDPyKAD7OhToXX4FNKQLHY5iCM1bCg ) might very well put them out of business completely.

Hyperscalers will be less bothered by a world without Huang. Yes, they favor open weight models, and yes, they have a lot of customers who want Nvidia chips. At the same time, they are all building their own chips, they hate paying Nvidia’s margins, and they especially hate the way that Huang plays hardball with chip allocation. They would also love a world where neolabs lose their patron, and potentially have to sell off chips and sites for a song.

It’s the frontier labs that are the most interesting. OpenAI is the most allied with Nvidia, from the first DGX supercomputer on ( https://www.tomshardware.com/news/openai-nvidia-dgx-1-ai-supercomputer,32476.html ) :

( https://www.tomshardware.com/news/openai-nvidia-dgx-1-ai-supercomputer,32476.html )

OpenAI has mostly run on Nvidia chips, and Nvidia has invested substantially in the company and helped backstop its data center projects. OpenAI is pushing for alternatives, including its own chip, but they would be harmed by Huang’s exit.

Anthropic, on the other hand, is something else entirely. Huang has characterized Anthropic as one of his biggest mistakes; from an interview earlier this year with Dwarkesh Patel ( https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/jensen-huang ) :

So, you asked the question why [Anthropic uses other accelerators]. A long time ago, we just didn’t have the ability to do it. At the time, I didn’t deeply internalize how difficult it would be to build a foundation AI lab like OpenAI and Anthropic, and the fact that they needed huge investments from the supplier themselves. We just weren’t in a position to make the multi-billion dollar investment into Anthropic so that they could use our compute. But Google and AWS were. They put in huge investments in the beginning so that Anthropic, in return, used their compute. We just weren’t in a position to do that at the time.

I would say my mistake is I didn’t deeply internalize that they really had no other options, that a VC would never put in $5-10 billion of investment into an AI lab with the hopes of it turning out to be Anthropic. So that was my miss. But even if I understood it, I don’t think we would’ve been in a position to do that at the time. But I’m not going to make that same mistake again.

I’m delighted to invest in OpenAI, and I’m delighted to help them scale, and I believe it’s essential to do so. And then, when I was able to, when Anthropic came to us, I’m delighted to be an investor, delighted to help them scale. We just weren’t, at the time, able to do it. If I could rewind everything—and Nvidia could have been as big back then as we are now—I would’ve been more than happy to do it.

Thanks to Amazon and Google, Anthropic doesn’t need Nvidia — or rather, they need Nvidia the least of anyone in AI. Moreover, it is Anthropic who are the fiercest proponents of the China chip ban, and who have made the most penetration into the national security apparatus (particularly during the Biden presidency). Yes, they have taken investment from Nvidia and now run on Nvidia chips, but they are the company most ideologically opposed to Huang.

Anthropic is also poised to benefit for pragmatic reasons. Huang’s downfall — or even his hobbling — significantly increases the likelihood of stricter controls on chips and compute for China, increases the likelihood of a crackdown on open weight models, and increases the likelihood of there being an AI compute slowdown, all of which are very good for Anthropic’s business prospects. It certainly is good fortune that the prospects of all of these are increasing just before the company’s attempted IPO.

Also, it’s only Tuesday.
