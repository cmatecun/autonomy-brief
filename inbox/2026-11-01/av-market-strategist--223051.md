👋 Welcome back everyone,
Waymo finally told us what its Toyota deal really is, and a familiar car turned up in a new role. It also made its existing cities bigger, while Tesla redrew its Houston map and Elon Musk declared the robotaxi race over. Tesla’s driver assistance picked up another European country, and in Munich a player I did not expect joined the robotaxi race. In China, regulators gave Volkswagen and XPeng the green light for road trials, and WeRide went looking for insurance at scale.
Let’s go!

Everyone’s watching the AV rideshare race through four layers: tech, apps, fleets, and ops. But there’s a fifth layer hiding in plain sight, and it’s worth $200B: charging infrastructure. Without power, an AV can’t serve a single ride. Without a network, it can’t serve a city.
Terawatt breaks down why infrastructure demands its own specialized capital and expertise, why fleet operators can’t easily build it themselves, and what it’ll take to keep pace as AVs scale 200x by 2035.
Read the full breakdown of the industry’s missing piece.
Tesla declares victory over Waymo, Waymo gets its Toyota, and Munich’s taxis want in
The biggest deal of the week solves Waymo’s problem for the end of the decade. The loudest claim of the week rests on one city and one week. And the most interesting line in Munich is about who owns the rider.
Waymo’s next robotaxi is a Toyota
Big news from Waymo: its next robotaxi will be a Toyota bZ4X.
Toyota and Waymo announced the next phase of their partnership on Friday. Here is what they said:
The start. Initial operations to test and validate an autonomous bZ4X fleet begin in Japan in 2027.
The markets. Deployment to the UK and “other Waymo-serviced markets” is planned for “the following years.” TechCrunch reports that the intent is to use the car in right-hand-drive countries like the UK.
The factory. Toyota builds the vehicles at its Takaoka plant in Toyota City, Aichi Prefecture. “The entire process will take place under one roof, from assembling the base vehicle to integrating Waymo’s AV hardware.”
The factory is the part I would look at first. A line-fit vehicle gets its autonomy hardware where the car itself is built, with no second stop. Compare that with the Ojai, which Zeekr builds in China and which gets fitted with the Waymo Driver in Mesa, Arizona. For the IONIQ 5, we still don’t know. As we noted in CW39, the Georgia line-fit we once assumed was our own inference, and Hyundai declined to confirm it when asked. So Toyota is the first time Waymo has a publicly disclosed integration inside the vehicle maker’s own plant. As Walt Piecyk said on Road to Autonomy, you don’t set that up unless you’re talking about material volumes.
This also closes a long loop. We first covered the preliminary agreement in CW19 last year, back when it was mostly a statement of intent. Eighteen months later, there is a plant, a model and a year.
It also makes Toyota the fourth platform in Waymo’s lineup, after the Jaguar I-PACE, the Zeekr-built Ojai and the Hyundai IONIQ 5. 
Over time the Jaguars leave the picture, which leaves Toyota, Hyundai and Zeekr, although the I-PACE is still the car that opens new markets like Madrid and Toronto.
There are two ways to read this. TechCrunch’s Kirsten Korosec sees “a conventional vehicle supplier deal” that fills specific regional needs. Road to Autonomy sees something much bigger, with Waymo “preparing for global dominance.” I sit somewhere between the two, and timing is why. There is no volume in the announcement, no price and no contract value, and testing only starts in 2027. So this car does nothing for the numbers we discussed last week: 8,000 vehicles by the end of this year and 20,000 by the end of 2027, if Semafor is right. Those still depend on the Ojai and the IONIQ 5.
So I think of it this way: Toyota solves Waymo’s problem for 2028-2030. It does not solve the one for 2027.
Over the longer run, though, the world’s largest carmaker building your robotaxi in its own plant matters a lot. And the right-hand-drive angle also matters, because Waymo’s Tokyo and London plans need a car for those roads.
One thing has not moved. On personally owned vehicles, the two companies say they will “continue to explore” the future of autonomous technology for Toyota’s cars. They were exploring that eighteen months ago, too.
The same car, a different driver
If the bZ4X sounds familiar, it should. Waymo isn’t the first to turn this car into a robotaxi.
As we reported in CW8, Pony.ai and Toyota started producing a bZ4X robotaxi earlier this year, and it is one of the three vehicles behind Pony’s seventh-generation fleet, alongside the GAC Aion V and the BAIC Arcfox Alpha T5.
The difference is where it comes from. Pony’s version rolls off the line at GAC Toyota, Toyota’s joint venture in China. Waymo’s comes from Takaoka in Japan.
Same model name, two autonomous drivers, two factories, two supply chains. 
So Toyota now has a robotaxi line with one of the leading American drivers and one of the leading Chinese drivers. That is a comfortable place to be. Toyota doesn’t have to decide who wins, and it gets to sell the car either way.
And Waymo needs cars, because it keeps growing. This week, though, with fewer new cities and more room in existing ones.
Waymo adds nearly 250 square miles in five cities
Waymo is growing its driverless service area by nearly 250 square miles, across five metros where it already runs:
Los Angeles: down the coast into the South Bay, through Manhattan Beach and Redondo Beach to Torrance.
Nashville: further south, and riders can now get to the zoo.
Orlando: coverage on the east side nearly doubles, with more service downtown.
San Diego: north to La Jolla, taking in University City and Clairemont along the way.
San Francisco Bay Area: further into the South Bay, including a connection to BART at Berryessa station in San Jose. The service area now runs from San Francisco down the Peninsula to San Jose and Campbell.
Source: Electrek
Waymo didn’t break out the square miles by city or say how many vehicles it is adding. Nashville opened to the public in April with about 60 square miles. San Diego only went public on 1 September, with roughly 50. 
Waymo also became the Official Ride-Hailing Partner of the Denver Broncos, with fully autonomous rides to Empower Field “arriving soon.” We saw this playbook with the Raiders and Allegiant Stadium in CW34. 
But Waymo wasn’t the only one drawing a bigger map this week.
Houston, we have a rocket
Tesla expanded its Robotaxi service area in Houston from about 25 square miles to about 254, according to Robotaxi Tracker. Waymo’s Houston service area is about 50 square miles.
And the shape is the joke. The new geofence looks like a rocket. “Houston, we have liftoff,” the Tesla Robotaxi account posted. 
Source: X
Musk said it was just a coincidence, in a week when Tesla and SpaceX are exploring a merger, as we covered in CW44.
The map was not the only thing Musk wanted to talk about.
Musk declares victory over Waymo: “It was never a real contest”
Musk also declared victory over Waymo. Jason Scharf posted a chart on X with the comment that “the battle for Autonomous Austin might already be over.” Musk replied: “It was never a real contest.”
The number behind this: in the week ending 18 October, Tesla reported 438,000 miles in Austin with no safety monitor aboard, up 30% from the week before. Waymo averaged about 406,000 miles a week in Austin in the second quarter.
Source: X
That is real, and Tesla deserves credit for it. A year ago, there was no Tesla fleet carrying the public in Austin without anyone in the driver’s seat. Now there is, and it is growing quickly.
But “never a real contest” asks that one comparison to carry a lot:
It is one city and one week. Waymo averaged 3.90 million rider-only miles a week across all markets in Q2. Tesla did 558,000 in its best week. That is about 7x. Waymo’s Phoenix, Bay Area and Los Angeles markets each drive more per week than Tesla’s whole network.
Source: Robotaxi Tracker
The dates don’t match. Tesla’s figure is its latest week, while Waymo’s is an April to June average, because Waymo publishes about three months after each quarter ends.
The cumulative gap is bigger. Waymo has 271.3 million rider-only miles since 2020, and Tesla 3.11 million driverless miles since January. That is about 87x.
Here is how Tesla’s miles breaks down by market in the week to Oct18:
Austin: 438,000 miles
Dallas: 94,841
Houston: 7,288
Tampa: 6,131
Orlando: 6,008
Miami: 5,626
Source: Robotaxi Tracker
So Austin is about 79% of the total, and Houston, Tampa, Orlando and Miami together did about 25,000 miles. Waymo’s smallest tracked market, Atlanta, averaged 250,000 a week in Q2.
The reason for the Austin spike is simple: Cybercabs. Tesla started public Cybercab rides there on 3 September. Ashok Elluswamy, Tesla’s head of AI software, said on the Q3 call that Tesla wants to “swarm Austin with Cybercabs everywhere,” and Musk said the fleet there had grown “by a factor of 10” in about a month. More cars, more miles.
And Tesla’s manufacturing lead is real. As of 31 October, the Texas AV tracker shows 1,205 registered vehicles for Tesla, 573 of them Cybercabs, and 1,259 for Waymo. So on paper, the two are almost level in Texas. Tesla added 615 vehicles there in the last 30 days.
Source: Texas AV Tracker
The 615 vehicles added in a month probably outpace what Waymo’s Mesa site is upfitting, although that is only my guess. In CW40 I wrote that a registered vehicle is not automatically a vehicle carrying paying riders. That filter still applies.
The service limits are also real. Elluswamy himself called the constraint “technology-driven,” with highways, higher-speed roads, 24/7 operation and more weather conditions still to unlock, hopefully by the end of this year. After that, he expects it to become regulatory, “perhaps in Q1, Q2 timeframes.”
The Road to Autonomy team brought up a useful point about what to measure. They want a new benchmark: paid commercial unsupervised miles, with a separate number for deadhead miles, the empty ones between rides. The labels already differ today: Tesla reports miles without a safety monitor, and Waymo reports rider-only miles.
And if that metric sounds familiar, it should. As we reported in CW44, Tesla’s Q3 materials replaced its “Cumulative Paid Robotaxi Miles” chart with “Cumulative Unsupervised Robotaxi Miles.” So the metric that would settle this debate is the one that just left the slide.
This is also where routing comes in. Tesla’s Robotaxis currently avoid highways, and riders report that they avoid at-grade railroad crossings. In September, a rider’s 10-minute Cybercab trip in Austin became a 70-minute detour, according to Electrek. In Houston, Business Insider spoke to a creator whose first ride, about 13 miles from home to office, took from 8:00 to 9:23 a.m. The same commute typically takes him 45 minutes in his own Tesla on FSD (Supervised).
In fairness, Ethan McKanna, who runs the Robotaxi tracker and documented other Houston problems, also said the service “feels really good” there. 
But the routing matters for the number Musk was celebrating: a detour adds miles for the same rider. A long way round looks like progress on a mileage chart.
So my read is that Tesla’s ramp is real, and that it is at an earlier stage than the claim suggests. Dallas and paid miles will tell us more than this chart does.
Tesla’s driver assistance reaches a tenth EU country
Things keep moving for Tesla on the European front, though. Latvia has approved FSD Supervised, which is called Tesla Assisted Driving (TAD) in Europe. It is the tenth EU country to do so, and all three Baltic states are now in.
Source: Reuters
The rollout begins shortly by software update, at €99 a month. 
So let’s update our count from CW42. A qualified majority in the EU’s Technical Committee on Motor Vehicles needs at least 15 of 27 member states, representing 65% of the population. December is still the earliest likely date for a vote.
Latvia has about 1.85 million people, or 0.4% of the EU, according to Eurostat. Here is where that leaves things:
The ten approvals: about 14.3% of the EU’s population.
With Germany, which said it will advocate for approval: 11 countries and about 32.8%.
The gap: 4 countries and about 32 percentage points.
While Latvia barely moves the population share, it adds a country. 
And one of the biggest remaining countries just spoke up. Italy “supports a swift EU approval of Tesla Assisted Driving,” Deputy Prime Minister and Transport Minister Matteo Salvini wrote on X, after what he called a “very positive and constructive” conversation with Musk. Musk replied: “Much appreciated. Thank you!”
If that becomes a yes vote, it moves the count far more than Latvia did. Italy has about 13% of the EU’s population, according to Eurostat, so Germany and Italy would bring Tesla to 12 countries and about 45.8%. The gap falls to 3 countries and about 19 percentage points.
The usual caveats apply, and they matter. A national approval is not a yes vote in Brussels, as the Czech position showed in CW41. France, Spain and Poland have not committed, and the easiest path to 65% runs through them. And as I wrote in CW42, the rename may have settled Germany’s objection, but it did not touch the speed offset that Sweden and the safety groups object to. Although Germany says Tesla agreed to a 10% cap, and Latvia says Tesla lowered the threshold. Whether that is enough for Sweden and the safety groups is what December turns on.
For the next story, we stay in Europe.
Munich’s taxi industry enters the robotaxi race
Tensor and Münchner Taxi Zentrum (MTZ), which the release calls Munich’s largest taxi operator, signed a letter of intent to deploy Tensor Robocars as autonomous taxis in Germany.
Here is what they announced:
The fleet. Up to 500 Robocars in German cities, subject to definitive agreements and Level 4 approval in Germany.
Europe. A goal of 10,000 more Robocars with European operators within 24 months of the car becoming available there.
MTZ’s role. It runs the fleet under its own brand, through its existing dispatch system, and decides with the city where and when the cars operate.
Tensor’s role. It sells the car and provides the driving software, fleet tools, service and training as a subscription it calls Autonomous Driving as a Service.
The money. “Financial institutions” will finance the fleets. None is named.
Private buyers. The partners will also explore selling Robocars to individuals in Germany.
Munich has become one of Europe’s busiest testbeds. Waymo is doing test drives there, aiming for commercial service by the end of 2027. Uber and Momenta hold a nationwide Level 4 test permit, with Munich as their first European city. Uber, Autobrains and NVIDIA are testing between Olympiapark and Central Station. Nuro has its European hub in the area. And Stadtwerke München, MVG and IAV run the MINGA research project.
MTZ is a different kind of entry, because the taxi industry has so far mostly been on the other side of this debate. In New York, Governor Hochul withdrew her robotaxi proposal in February as the Taxi Workers Alliance celebrated (CW9), and Waymo’s city testing permits later expired. In Las Vegas, the Livery Operators Association and local taxi companies opposed the permits for Tesla, Uber and Waymo in August, warning of oversaturation and overcrowded roads. Nevada’s regulator approved them unanimously anyway.
So why would Munich go the other way? It helps to look at who signed.
MTZ’s CEO, Gregor Beiner, has also been president of Germany’s federal taxi association since last November, and he chairs Taxis 4 Smart Mobility, a coalition of taxi organisations from six European countries. Last year, he was a leading voice in the Munich taxi protests for minimum fares on Uber and Bolt rides. “We won’t let up, because it is about our existence,” he said in July 2025, as drivers demonstrated outside city hall, per the Abendzeitung. “Without minimum prices for all ride services, the taxi industry doesn’t stand a chance.”
There are two ways to read this.
Read one: the taxi industry has changed its mind about autonomy.
Read two: it is defending the customer relationship before someone else takes it.
I lean towards the second.
Beiner has also been preparing this for months. In April he was already presenting autonomous driving as an opportunity for the industry. In late September he was in Brussels with Pierfrancesco Maran, the MEP we had on the podcast in EP15 [ https://substack.com/redirect/bbce9cbc-a032-40c6-90a3-c93f40221392?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ], and with the cabinet of Commission Executive Vice-President Henna Virkkunen. And the taxi group of the international road transport union IRU, where Beiner is vice-president, has put robotaxi regulation on its 2027 work programme.
This is also why “the taxi industry” is too broad a term. New York’s fight was largely led by labour: the drivers whose jobs would go. Las Vegas was operators and livery companies, and they lost. Beiner is a business owner. An owner can replace a driver with a subscription and keep the customer. A driver can’t. So he is careful when he says that “our professional drivers remain indispensable,” and I would watch how that holds up inside his own industry.
The fine print matters here, too:
It is a letter of intent, with no launch date.
Nobody holds a German Level 4 vehicle approval yet.
I have not seen a Tensor in commercial service anywhere. In CW40 I wrote about its Sofia deal that the order book is running ahead of the product. It has now grown again.
Two more points deserve attention.
The balance sheet. Taxi Times put Beiner’s Munich business at just under 80 vehicles a year ago. So 500 Robocars would be more than six times that fleet, and the release gives no price for the car or the subscription. That is why the unnamed “financial institutions” are the most important words in the release. And we have heard twice recently why they might hesitate. Lukas Neckermann told us in EP31 [ https://substack.com/redirect/3ceb12de-4292-44df-9f1f-5a51f904be8d?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ] that residual value is why lessors wait. Doria Holbrook explained in EP33 [ https://substack.com/redirect/4a6def9a-3f5d-45c0-afa4-fb0b5e5147e8?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ] why an AV-only asset-backed security is hard to do: “there isn’t a proven residual market.” Who takes that risk for a car with no commercial record?
The sovereignty pitch. The release says control of the fleet, the customer and the data “stays with the taxi company and therefore in Europe.” Fleet and customer, yes, as far as we can tell. But the driving software and the fleet tools arrive as a subscription from a US company, which as we noted in CW34 last year is a rebrand of AutoX, with VinFast as its manufacturing partner. What happens to the driving data under that subscription isn’t disclosed, and it’s the first thing I would ask. A taxi company can own the rider and still rent the brain.
I could be wrong on the motive, and this may never become 500 cars. But everyone else in Munich is asking who can drive. MTZ is asking who owns the rider.
What this means and what to watch
First, Toyota probably changes Waymo’s 2028-2030, and its 2027 stays the same. Watch whether the year-end fleet count gets anywhere near the reported 8,000, and whether Toyota and Waymo ever disclose a volume. 
Second, watch which right-hand-drive market gets the car first after Japan. London is testing with I-PACEs today. If Waymo launches there before the Toyota arrives, the bZ4X becomes a scale car for London, not a launch car.
Third, Toyota is now on both sides. It builds for Waymo in Japan and for Pony.ai in China. Watch whether either relationship reaches the EU, where the two would meet.
Fourth, the Tesla test is Dallas and paid miles. Dallas is due for Cybercabs in November. If Tesla repeats the Austin ramp there and shows paid miles again, the case gets much stronger. If registrations keep running ahead of service, the mileage chart will keep saying less than it appears to.
Fifth, December is the next date for Tesla in Europe. Germany and Italy together would bring it to 12 countries and about 45.8% of the EU’s population, still 3 countries and about 19 points short. Watch whether Salvini’s support becomes a yes vote, what France, Spain and Poland do, and whether the changes to the speed offset are enough for Sweden and the safety groups.
Sixth, watch for a name next to MTZ. A bank or lessor willing to finance Robocars would tell us more than the letter of intent does. 
Seventh, watch if other taxi operators follow. T4SM gives Beiner the network to spread this model, from Paris to Vienna. If they sign up with Tensor or anyone else, Europe’s taxi industry will have chosen a different path from New York’s.

🇨🇳 China Corner: the permits are moving again
Six months after the Baidu outage in Wuhan froze new licences, China is handing out road trials again, this time to a German carmaker and to a Chinese challenger in which it holds a small stake.
Volkswagen gets a Level 3 test permit in Hefei
Volkswagen Group China now has a permit to test Level 3 driving on public roads in Hefei, and it says testing has started. The permit also covers large-scale driving data collection in the city. VW says it was issued under China’s strictest L3 framework. It didn’t say which authority issued it, for how many vehicles or which roads they can use.
What VW can’t do is just as important. This is a road test permit, so it can’t sell L3 cars to customers on the back of it. And VW isn’t leading here. China granted its first L3 product approvals in December 2025 to a Changan model, capped at 50 km/h on designated Chongqing expressways, and a BAIC Arcfox model, capped at 80 km/h on three Beijing routes. GAC’s Hyptec and XPeng already hold L3 test permits.
The software comes from CARIZON, VW’s driving software unit in China, which began as a joint venture between CARIAD and Horizon Robotics. Its system is called Hyper Sense 8. Behind it sits GAIA 2.0, a world model VW built itself, which it says simulates the equivalent of 5 million km of safety testing a day. VW is also developing its own C7H chip, and it has targeted the start of L3 deliveries for the second half of 2027.
Why it matters is liability. Once L3 is engaged, the carmaker is responsible for the driving, not the person in the seat. That is the responsibility this permit moves VW towards in China. It also shows the pattern we called “buy the brain, keep the body” in CW42, with a local brain in China.
But VW’s bet in China doesn’t stop at CARIZON.
XPeng gets its Level 4 trial in Guangzhou
VW holds a stake of roughly 5% in XPeng, and XPeng also got an approval this week.
Its robotaxi business, XPeng Yoyo, can now run Level 4 trial operations across its home city of Guangzhou. It isn’t open to everyone: access starts with invitation codes and expands step by step.
Yoyo has moved quickly this year. It got a road test permit in January, set up a dedicated division in March, built its first production robotaxi in May, and had completed more than 2,000 internal test rides by August. The car is based on the GX SUV and uses cameras only, no lidar and no high-definition maps, running on four in-house Turing chips.
XPeng’s target is passenger service without safety drivers in 2027, and it wants to work with ride-hailing platforms in China and abroad that same year. Its revenue plan combines vehicle sales, technology services and a share of operations. This is the camera-only bet, tested in a robotaxi service in China.
The bigger signal sits behind both permits. China suspended new robotaxi licences in April after the Baidu incident in Wuhan and launched an industrywide safety review. Approvals resumed in July, with Momenta among the first, but few companies have publicly said they got the green light. VW and XPeng in the same week suggests regulators have moved on, although the ministry did not respond to requests for comment. So I would read this as a sign of direction, not proof of volume.
But to really put vehicles on the road, you also need someone to insure them. And there is news on that front, too.
WeRide builds an insurance programme for tens of thousands of vehicles
WeRide formed a global insurance partnership with Allianz Insurance Singapore and Ping An Property & Casualty as founding members. It is designed to cover tens of thousands of its driverless vehicles.
The cover spans motor, general and product liability, plus privacy and cybersecurity risks, with policies tailored to each market as WeRide expands, including into Europe. WeRide expects long-term contracts, pooled programmes and a bigger fleet to lower insurance costs and improve per-vehicle returns.
The partners already know these cars. Allianz became Singapore’s first insurer of a fully driverless vehicle with WeRide’s Robobus on Sentosa, and it covers the robotaxis on the Ai.R service with Grab in Punggol. Ping An insures WeRide robotaxis in Abu Dhabi, Dubai, Beijing and Guangzhou.
Two details stand out. First, the scale: WeRide has about 3,600 vehicles today, more than 1,800 of them robotaxis, so “tens of thousands” is a plan on paper. Second, the name. In CW39 we reported on Waymo’s partnership with Allianz Partners, starting with London and Munich. They are different entities within the group, and that deal covers different ground, but the same insurance group now shows up next to both Waymo and WeRide. In CW41 I wrote that insurers increasingly see robotaxis as a product category. This points the same way.
CFO Jennifer Li calls insurance “a critical part of the infrastructure.” I would go one step further. As Chris Moore explained in EP10 [ https://substack.com/redirect/9d709f04-c22c-4cee-a2c8-eee0c88abc99?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ], liability in Europe can be unlimited. So a named insurer will be one of the first things a European regulator or partner asks about, and WeRide now has one ready.
What this means and what to watch
First, China’s pause looks over, but keep an eye on who gets through. 
Second, VW’s L3 is a liability decision with a date on it. Watch the second half of 2027. If deliveries start then, a German group will be carrying L3 liability in China before most of its rivals carry it at home.
Third, XPeng’s 2027 goal is the one to check. A camera-only robotaxi with no safety driver in a Chinese city would be a real test of the approach, and of its remote takeover system. It is also the closest comparison to Tesla we will get outside the US.
Fourth, insurance is becoming a scale lever. If WeRide’s pooled programme lowers cost per vehicle, that shows up in its per-vehicle returns. Watch for the first disclosed figure, and for the first European market it names.
