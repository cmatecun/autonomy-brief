Waymo takes the driver out in Detroit, and Tesla gives its driver a new name in Europe
In the same week Waymo took the driver out in Detroit, Tesla agreed to call its system in Europe what it is: assisted driving.
Waymo goes driverless in Detroit
Waymo has started fully driverless testing in Detroit. Since this week, its cars drive without a specialist behind the wheel in downtown Detroit and the surrounding neighbourhoods, including Corktown, Midtown, Hamtramck and Indian Village, and Waymo plans to expand the area from there.
Waymo has been in Detroit since last November, when it began mapping the city with its Jaguar I-Pace and the Zeekr-built Ojai. As we noted at the time, Detroit was always as much a winter test as a market. The Ojai carries the sixth-generation Waymo Driver, which is built to operate in wintry conditions. 
For now, only Waymo employees can ride. There is no timeline for a public launch. “We’ll follow our safety framework and serve riders in Detroit when we’ve properly validated our technology in the city, and our local operations are ready,” Waymo spokesperson Chris Bonelli told The Detroit News.
Analysts are more specific. Sam Abuelsamid of Telemetry expects a limited service by the end of this year and the Waymo app to open to the public in early 2027. 
So Waymo keeps expanding, at home and abroad, and that costs money. This week it refilled the war chest.
Waymo locks in a $5 billion loan
Waymo has closed a $5 billion loan, the first debt financing in its history. The lender list reads like a who’s who of private credit and asset management: PIMCO, Blackstone and Sixth Street, joined by Capital Group, Loomis Sayles, T. Rowe Price, Apollo, Blue Owl, Diameter Capital Partners, Franklin Templeton, Fidelity, HPS Investment Partners and Oaktree. Goldman Sachs was the sole lead bookrunner.
Waymo described the financing as an important step in its evolution into a “scaling commercial enterprise.”
As we reported in CW37, Waymo was in final-stage talks with PIMCO, Blackstone and Sixth Street for more than $3 billion, with Goldman running the process. It closed at $5 billion, as much as two thirds more than the figure reported a month ago.
Until now, Waymo has funded itself entirely with equity, from Alphabet and from outside investors:
$3.2 billion in 2020
$2.5 billion in 2021
$5.6 billion in its Series C in 2024
$16 billion in February 2026, at a valuation of $126 billion, led by Dragoneer, DST Global and Sequoia, with Alphabet remaining the majority investor
That is about $27 billion of equity in six years. 
According to a Waymo spokesperson, the debt gives the company “financial flexibility to strengthen its balance sheet” and positions it to “capitalize on the significant opportunities ahead, especially as a scaling business with proven commercial demand and improved road safety outcomes in the communities in which we operate.”
In CW37, I read the talks as a sign of ambition. Alphabet is not short of money, and a company that has never borrowed does not arrange billions in debt unless the plan has grown. The size at close supports that read. I cannot tell from the outside whether Waymo asked for more or the lenders offered more. Either way, thirteen large credit investors and asset managers have now taken a direct view on Waymo’s commercial business, and that is new.
The plan is large. Waymo now runs robotaxi services in 15 US markets, is testing in London and Tokyo, and is pushing into Europe and Japan. 
While Waymo adds cities and capital, one of its biggest US rivals is still working out how to stay on the road after dark.
Tesla’s Robotaxi struggles at night, and the answer is lidar
Late on Friday, 2 October, Tesla’s Robotaxi account announced that the Austin service is “now available until 11pm.” Until then, it shut down at 10pm.
What caught my attention was the explanation for the extra hour. Musk reposted the announcement and wrote:
“The main thing we’re trying to solve is making sure that we don’t run over pets when they’re hard to see at night. Literally trying to avoid grey kittens on grey tarmac in the dark.”
Source: X/@elonmusk
From a sensor point of view, that sentence describes a very specific problem. A camera is a passive sensor: it can only work with the light the scene sends back. At night there is far less of it, so the camera has to raise its gain or lengthen its exposure, and both add noise and motion blur to the image. A camera also cannot measure distance. Depth is inferred by the neural network from the image itself. A grey kitten on grey asphalt gives that network almost no contrast to work with. Low light, low contrast and a small object together are close to the worst case for vision.
Lidar and radar bring their own illumination. A lidar sends out laser pulses and measures the time of flight of each return, so it gets the range and 3D shape of an object directly, whether the street is lit or not and whatever colour the object is. A kitten on the road is a small bump above a flat surface, and that is exactly what a point cloud picks up. Radar adds the velocity of every object through the Doppler shift and keeps working in rain, fog and glare, although its coarse resolution makes something as small as a cat hard to separate from the road surface.
Source: Electrek
That is why no one running a driverless fleet at scale relies on cameras alone. Waymo’s sixth-generation Driver uses 13 cameras, 4 lidars and 6 radars, and its fleet operates around the clock. Tesla took the opposite path. It removed radar from its cars in 2021, dropped ultrasonic sensors a year later, and has never fitted lidar.
Musk has argued against lidar for years, and Electrek has collected his statements in one place. The record is worth reading in full, and two lines from it stand out. In 2019, he called lidar “a fool’s errand.” And in May of this year, he posted an image from Tesla’s cameras with the words: “This is why Tesla FSD can see so well at night or through extreme glare.” Set against that post, this week’s explanation is quite a change in tone.
What bothers me most, though, is the one-hour shift itself. If darkness were really the problem, it should make no difference whether the service closes at 10pm, 11pm or midnight. In Austin it is fully dark by 10pm all year round, and it does not get any darker an hour later. An extra hour of operation in the dark does nothing to solve a darkness problem. So I think there has to be another explanation for where the cut-off sits.
It also helps to remember where the service started. When Tesla launched in Austin in June 2025, the app took rides until midnight, as Electrek points out. Fifteen months later, even with the new hour, the service closes earlier than it did on day one.
So there are two ways to read the one-hour step. Read generously, it is ordinary validation: the operating window grows one step at a time as the data comes in, and Musk simply chose a memorable example. Read more sceptically, operations set the closing time more than the sensors do.
The archive gives the second reading some weight. In CW36 we counted three of Tesla’s 22 crash reports to NHTSA in which a human was operating the car remotely. And in CW41 we noted that Tesla is the only company that says publicly its remote operators can take direct control of a vehicle. If the service depends on remote staff more than Tesla has said, then the hours are a question of staffing as much as of technology, and every additional hour has a cost.
I lean toward the second reading. But this is my speculation, and I want to label it as such: Tesla does not disclose how many remote operators it uses or how often they intervene. What I find hard to accept is the explanation as given.
Either way, the problem Musk described is one that lidar handles with ease, and it is a camera limitation the industry has known about for years.
So why not simply add lidar? The cost argument no longer holds. Rivian said recently that lidar now costs it “a few hundred dollars” per vehicle, as Electrek reported.
I think the real obstacle is a promise. In October 2016, Tesla said that every car it builds has the hardware needed for full self-driving. Adding lidar now would raise an uncomfortable question for the millions of cars already sold on that promise. Pride may play a part too, given how strongly Musk has argued against lidar for years. But the installed base is what makes the camera-only approach so hard to walk back. So I expect Tesla to stay camera-only on principle, and to keep solving the night one hour at a time.
Staying with Tesla, but looking at Europe.
Slovakia to approve FSD
Slovakia expects to approve Tesla’s FSD (Supervised) within days. Transport Minister Jozef Raz announced it in a video on LinkedIn on Wednesday.
“Data shows that these cars reduce the accident rate and that’s exactly what we’re aiming for,” Raz said. The ministry will send a recognition letter to the Netherlands, whose regulator approved the system in April and leads the process for Tesla in Europe. “Once this letter is delivered, the FSD system will be available for use here as well. But remember: you’re still the driver, and you’re responsible for the car.”
That would make Slovakia the ninth EU country. We have followed the list week by week: Slovenia was sixth, the Czech Republic seventh, and Croatia eighth last week. A new country now joins almost every week. Tesla’s campaign appears to be working, even though the EU-wide vote has not happened yet. The earliest date is December.
And for that vote, Tesla has just won a major advocate.
Tesla’s Full Self-Driving (Supervised) gets a new name in Europe
In Europe, Tesla’s system is no longer called “Full Self-Driving (Supervised).” It is now “Tesla Assisted Driving.”
The change follows pushback from Germany. Federal Transport Minister Steffen Bilger said earlier this month that calling the software “Full Self-Driving” was misleading, because it does not perform the entire task of driving. He spoke with Tesla last month, and according to the ministry, it was Tesla that suggested the new name. Bilger now says he will advocate for approval of Tesla Assisted Driving across Europe ahead of the EU-wide vote. Musk thanked him on X.
TechCrunch called it “a big capitulation” from a company that has used the name for years, despite a system that requires a human to watch the road and take over when needed. It is also a pragmatic one, because Germany’s support matters enormously for the arithmetic.
An EU-wide approval needs a qualified majority: at least 55% of member states, which is 15 of 27, representing at least 65% of the EU population. Here is where the count stands, using Eurostat population figures for January 2026:
The nine countries that have approved FSD nationally or, in Slovakia’s case, are about to (the Netherlands, Denmark, Belgium, Estonia, Lithuania, Slovenia, the Czech Republic, Croatia and Slovakia) account for 62.7 million people, or 13.9% of the EU population.
Germany alone adds 83.5 million, or 18.5%.
Together that makes 10 states and 32.3%.
So Germany takes Tesla roughly halfway to the population threshold in one step. What remains is five more states and about 33 percentage points, and that can only come from the other large countries: France (15.3%), Italy (13.0%), Spain (11.0%) and Poland (8.0%). Without France, even Italy, Spain and Poland together would take Tesla only to 64.4%, just short of the threshold, and it would still need at least two more countries.
Two caveats. A national approval is not the same as a yes vote in Brussels: the Czech Republic approved FSD nationally and still raised concerns about the speed settings, as we noted last week. And France, which refused in July, has softened. Transport Minister Philippe Tabarot now says he is “working closely” with Tesla on changes “that will enable France to support its approval.”
There is also something the new name does not change. It settles Germany’s objection, which was about the name. It does not touch the objection we covered last week from the European Transport Safety Council and Sweden, which is the system’s ability to drive up to 50% above the detected speed limit. That is the feature the December vote is most likely to turn on.
For Tesla, the prize is substantial. An EU-wide approval would open FSD, or now Tesla Assisted Driving, to every major car market in Europe, which could be a potentially massive new revenue stream. As we wrote last week, Europe is where Tesla’s car business is growing again, and FSD is the lever analysts expect to add to that.
How Tesla got this far is the subject of a long investigation Reuters published this week.
Reuters: how Tesla is pressuring Europe’s regulators
For the piece, Reuters obtained hundreds of pages of email correspondence between Tesla and regulators in the Netherlands, Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Estonia, Italy and Switzerland. It reviewed the safety studies Tesla distributed across Europe, interviewed seven traffic-safety researchers about Tesla’s methodology, and asked five autonomous-vehicle regulation experts to review the exchanges. In CW26 we covered an earlier part of this story, when Reuters reported that Tesla had given inflated US safety data to Swedish and Dutch regulators.
The full article is long, so here are the core findings.
The safety data cannot carry the claims. Tesla’s Europe study compared FSD-equipped cars driven by its own professional engineering drivers with all Teslas driven by ordinary owners. Tesla’s FSD sample covered tens or hundreds of thousands of miles, against hundreds of millions of manual miles, which is too small to compare crash rates. So Tesla used driving behaviours as “surrogates” instead: horn honking, blinker use, hard braking, automatic emergency braking and hard cornering. In the fine print, Tesla itself says the comparisons cannot be read as “causal estimates.” All seven researchers said the study provides no evidence for the central claim of Tesla’s campaign, that FSD prevents fatal crashes. “When you start getting into ‘lives saved,’ that’s where it gets nonsensical,” said Marco Benedetti of the University of Michigan Transportation Research Institute.
Tesla pushed the Dutch regulator to scale back its review. The correspondence shows Tesla contesting the RDW’s costs, including a bill of €365,000 for a five-month period, and pressing to set the terms of testing. “Keep in mind that this is mission critical for our leadership,” one Tesla employee wrote. An RDW staffer later invited Tesla to “share any suggestions on how we might adjust or reduce the required effort,” and another wrote of “shared goals and objectives.” “An authority like the RDW needs to be very strong in saying: No, this is what we deem necessary,” said Nynke Vellinga of the University of Groningen.
Tesla mobilised its fans against regulators. When France’s transport minister refused support in July, Musk posted that “delaying the approval of FSD in France will cost lives,” and Tesla supporters flooded the minister’s account. Regulators in Sweden and Norway received dozens of emails citing the same claims. “We do not approve systems because they are popular, and we do not reject them because they are controversial,” said Stein-Helge Mundal of the Norwegian Public Roads Administration.
There is another side, and it belongs in the summary. The RDW says it assesses driver-assistance systems from all manufacturers “objectively and independently,” and that engagement with a manufacturer is a routine part of the process. Several of the researchers also said Tesla’s European study is an improvement on its US research, with more disclosure of its limitations. Daniel McGehee of the University of Iowa called it “potentially useful evidence of system performance,” while adding that it stops well short of showing FSD saves lives.
For me, the most important detail for the vote is a quieter one. Other national regulators rely heavily on the Dutch review when they decide, and the RDW has declined to publish its FSD test data, with Tesla and the RDW both citing trade secrets. In a process that runs on mutual recognition, the data at its centre is not public.

And FSD has company on its way to Europe: the Cybercab.
The Cybercab comes to the Paris Motor Show
Tesla will display the Cybercab at the Paris Motor Show, which opens today, 12 October. It is the first time the vehicle will be shown in Europe.
Visitors will not be able to ride in it. The Cybercab has no steering wheel or pedals. Instead, they can test drive six other Tesla vehicles with the driver-assistance suite, according to show director Serge Gachot.
In CW37 we wrote that the Cybercab had arrived in Austin while the permission to operate it at scale had not. In Europe, that gap is wider still. The continent has not yet approved even the supervised system across the EU. So for now, the Cybercab in Paris is a showroom piece, and a signal of where Tesla wants to go.
Staying in Europe, another American company made news this week, with the help of a Chinese one.
Uber and Pony.ai to launch in London
Uber and Pony.ai plan to launch a robotaxi service in London. Testing of Pony.ai’s seventh-generation robotaxis is due to begin in the coming weeks. “We look forward to working with local officials, and our partners, as plans progress to begin trials for self-driving vehicles under the UK’s frontier plan,” Pony.ai said.
There is no fleet size and no date for a commercial launch yet, and the companies have not said who will own and operate the fleet. 
London is becoming one of the most crowded robotaxi cities outside the US and China. Here is who is there:
Waymo has about 100 cars testing with safety drivers and has said it will not launch a public service until it can do so with nobody at the wheel.
Wayve launched London’s first supervised robotaxi service on Uber in September, with a safety driver in every car.
Baidu has been testing on both Uber and Freenow by Lyft since the summer.
Pony.ai now follows on Uber.
That is four driving systems and two platforms, and still no approved driverless commercial service. For Uber, Pony.ai is the third robotaxi partner in the city, after Wayve and Baidu. It is the same multi-partner approach Uber uses elsewhere: in Dubai, it works with both Baidu and WeRide. Uber now has deals with more than 30 autonomous vehicle companies and expects to offer autonomous trips in up to 15 cities worldwide by the end of this year.

Verne co-founder and CEO Marko Pejković confirmed it on LinkedIn, under a post by Uber CEO Dara Khosrowshahi about his visit to Verne in Zagreb. Asked directly by Jaan Juurikas from EVwire, he replied: “I can confirm - yes Uber has invested in Verne.”

The week before, co-founder Mate Rimac had already described Uber as “an investor and strategic partner” on Facebook. Until then, Uber’s only official word was that it “intends to invest,” a line from the March announcement of its partnership with Verne and Pony.ai. Neither company has disclosed the amount.
During his visit, Khosrowshahi took a fully driverless ride through Zagreb. Verne and Pony.ai removed the safety operator for invited riders in September, on a 22-kilometre route between the airport, Verne’s headquarters and the city centre. Rimac also mentioned talks about taking Verne beyond Croatia with its “final” vehicle, a purpose-built two-seater without steering wheel or pedals, built on the Mobileye Drive platform.
Let’s stay in London for a moment, or rather with London’s home favourite, Wayve.
VW chose Wayve?
According to Der Spiegel, Volkswagen has chosen Wayve to supply the AI software for the driver-assistance systems of group brands like Audi and Porsche, with the goal of bringing them level with Tesla and Mercedes. Spiegel cites three insiders. Volkswagen, Wayve and VW’s software unit Cariad all declined to comment.
According to the report, VW put Wayve, NVIDIA and about a dozen other providers head to head a few weeks ago, on a test drive in a large city in southern Germany. Wayve is said to have convinced both technically and financially. One decision-maker described an Audi A6 e-tron running Wayve’s software that recognised an oncoming driver flashing his headlights to give way, and then drove itself into a parking space.
The structure of the deal matters. Wayve would supply only the AI software. Cariad would integrate it into the hardware and software solution it has been developing with Bosch. NVIDIA, a major investor in Wayve, could still come in as the chip supplier. 
Regular readers will recognise the direction. In CW39, when MOIA’s search for investors collapsed, I wrote that I had been “wondering for a while when MOIA, or Volkswagen, would put a different technology partner behind it,” and that Wayve and Volkswagen would be “a European solution [...] very appealing as a sovereign answer.” In CW40 we reported, citing Manager Magazin, that VW had quietly started shadow-mode test drives with Wayve a year earlier.
If the deal is signed, Volkswagen would be Wayve’s fourth carmaker, after Nissan, Stellantis and Mercedes. As we wrote in CW40, Wayve is close to becoming the default choice for carmakers that would rather buy their driving AI than build it.
The reasoning behind that choice came from one of Wayve’s existing customers this week. Speaking alongside Alex Kendall at Stellantis’ Wave event in Turin, CEO Antonio Filosa said automotive scale “will decrease costs, which is very important, obviously,” and that working with outside technology partners is a way to compress development cycles that currently run at about two years. Note the structure, too: Stellantis integrates Wayve’s software into its own STLA AutoDrive platform, and according to Spiegel, VW would have Cariad integrate it into the system it built with Bosch. Both carmakers buy the AI and keep the integration in-house.
Kendall named the other side of the deal: Stellantis’ range of brands and vehicle types is exactly what Wayve needs to prove that one AI platform works everywhere. So each new carmaker makes Wayve’s software more portable, and over time that shifts bargaining power from the buyers toward the supplier.
Tesla’s push into Europe adds to the pressure on VW and other OEMs. FSD is already approved in eight EU countries, with Slovakia about to follow, Germany’s KBA is reviewing it, and Audi has fallen behind BMW and Mercedes on driver assistance in city traffic.
Then there is MOIA. To be clear, the Spiegel report is about driver assistance in private cars, and MOIA’s robotaxi service in Hamburg still runs on Mobileye. But the question I asked in CW39 now looks closer to an answer. According to Manager Magazin’s reporting at the time, Volkswagen has invested more than €2 billion in MOIA so far, would probably need at least another billion euros to make it commercially ready, and does not expect it to become genuinely lucrative before the 2030s. Giving MOIA up is seen inside the group as a humiliation to be avoided.
So if Volkswagen is prepared to put in that further billion or more, I think the partner behind it is far more likely to be Wayve than Mobileye. That is my own inference from the reporting, and Wayve would still have to show it can run without a safety driver, which it has not yet done in Europe. Time will tell. But I think it would be a good move for VW.
What this all means and what to watch
First, Detroit now has a dated marker. Abuelsamid expects limited service by the end of this year and public access in early 2027, and we will check against both. The more interesting test is winter: the Ojai was built for it, and Detroit is where Waymo will find out how well.
Second, Waymo is now a borrower, and that changes who is watching. Thirteen lenders have taken a view on Waymo’s commercial business, and lenders look at fleet economics more closely than equity investors chasing a category winner. Watch whether this is the first of several debt deals.
Third, Tesla’s night problem is measurable. Every extension of the Austin hours is a data point. If the service gets back to its launch-day midnight closing, and beyond, the validation explanation gains credibility. If it stalls, the operations reading gains it. 
Fourth, the new name brings Germany on side, but leaves the speed offset where it was. That is the feature Sweden and the ETSC object to, and it is the one the December vote is most likely to turn on. On the arithmetic, France is the swing country: with Germany, France and Italy behind it, Tesla is within reach of the population threshold. Without France, even Italy, Spain and Poland together would leave Tesla just short at 64.4%, with at least two more countries still needed.
Fifth, London is a race to the first driverless commercial service, with four candidates. Watch who operates Pony.ai’s fleet, since a Verne-style operator would confirm the Zagreb template, and watch whether Uber’s three-partner approach in London becomes the model for its other European cities.
Sixth, watch whether VW names a new technology partner for MOIA. If that partner is Wayve, Volkswagen would have its European chain for both private cars and robotaxis.

💡 Quick Takes
Init buys the software behind Europe’s first driverless scheduled bus
Init, the Karlsruhe-based public transport technology company, has acquired 67.74% of the Norwegian start-up Applied Autonomy, whose xFlow software runs Europe’s first driverless scheduled bus in Stavanger. The price was not disclosed. Applied Autonomy is small: eight employees and about €530,000 in revenue in 2025, and its auditor had flagged substantial doubt about its ability to continue as a going concern. What Init gets is the control layer: xFlow connects autonomous vehicles to a control centre, monitors them in real time and provides remote assistance, the role German law calls technical supervision.

Autonomous trucks get a five-year answer on warning triangles
The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration has granted a five-year exemption that lets Level 4 trucks use cab-mounted warning beacons instead of the reflective triangles a human driver would place behind a stopped truck. It runs until October 2031 and covers Aurora and any other Level 4 carrier that notifies the agency first. It replaces four three-month waivers Aurora has operated under since October 2025, which Kodiak, Waabi and Stack AV had joined. The beacons must switch on within five minutes of a stop, against the ten minutes a driver has for triangles. The agency weighed 402 comments, with truck drivers’ association OOIDA among the opponents, and had denied the same request from Aurora and Waymo in December 2024. “Now there is a firm answer,” said Jeff Farrah of the Autonomous Vehicle Industry Association.
🔗 FreightWaves [ https://substack.com/redirect/5a20ab0a-64cc-40ca-a271-dc52563d0245?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]
Einride’s cab-less trucks go live in Ohio
Two of Einride’s cab-less, driverless electric trucks are now running daily freight operations between EASE Logistics warehouses in Marysville, Ohio, on EASE property and local public roads. Nobody is in the cab. A remote operator monitors each truck and can step in. The deployment is part of the $8.8 million Truck Automation Corridor Project run by Ohio’s and Indiana’s transport departments, and it is EASE’s third autonomous trucking deployment. The routes are short, as they are at Lidl in Germany, but two continents now have cab-less trucks in daily commercial use.
🔗 Einride [ https://substack.com/redirect/5e4013a1-6382-492c-8472-c2cb54311634?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]
Kodiak opens a lane to Laredo
Kodiak and the cross-border carrier Charger USA are hauling refrigerated and dry freight on the 435-mile lane between Dallas and Laredo, Texas, for now with a safety driver behind the wheel. The first delivery was on 8 September. It is Kodiak’s first route into Laredo, the busiest commercial land port in the US, which handled more than $350 billion of trade in 2025, nearly 40% of all US trade with Mexico. Kodiak says its long-haul safety case was 96% complete at the end of September, and it still plans driverless long-haul service by the end of the year. Last week it named IKEA as its first driverless customer.

Volvo and Waabi start their first customer operation
Volvo Autonomous Solutions and Waabi have started hauling freight for Warp, an open freight network for less-than-truckload and full-truckload shipping, between Dallas and Houston. It is the first customer operation of their partnership. The Volvo VNL Autonomous, running the Waabi Driver, delivers directly to third-party facilities, which matters in less-than-truckload freight, where shipments from many customers have to move on tight schedules. For now, an observer sits in the driver’s seat.
🔗 Volvo Group [ https://substack.com/redirect/7300439b-5a20-4065-ace3-b9f3a8530cbf?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]
Driverless shuttles run among the aircraft at Tallinn Airport
At Tallinn Airport, Estonia’s Auve Tech now runs its MiCa shuttles on routes close to parked aircraft without a safety operator on board, for the first time. The shuttles move workers, tools and spare parts between the hangars of aircraft maintenance firm Magnetic MRO. Testing began in 2023 and commercial service in 2025, still with an operator. The airport’s safety board reviewed the shuttles’ data every week. “That’s why getting the permit is kind of a miracle,” said Auve Tech’s Deivis Sepp. Airports may turn out to be one of the more natural early markets: fixed routes, controlled access and a strong reason to automate.

Saudi Arabia adds delivery robots and autonomous buses
Riyadh has launched its first pilot of self-driving delivery robots in the Al-Falah district, run by the Transport General Authority. The vehicles are Level 4, monitored from a remote operations centre, and the authority targets delivery cost reductions of up to 85%. It did not say how many vehicles take part or when the pilot will expand, so for now the 85% should be read as a target. Separately, Japan’s TIER IV has won a government grant to run autonomous bus trials in Saudi Arabia with a European carmaker, starting in closed areas before moving to public roads. Saudi Arabia wants 15% of public transport to be autonomous by 2030.

Overland AI launches an autonomous vehicle for the Marines
Overland AI has launched Ibex, a fully autonomous ground vehicle built on the chassis, engine and drivetrain of the Polaris MRZR already in US military service. The US Marine Corps has chosen it for its counter-drone system, under what Overland calls the first production contract for the US military led by a ground autonomy company. Ibex carries 2,000 lb, drives above 35 mph autonomously, covers 250 miles per tank and needs no teleoperation. Using parts and fuel units already have is the point: defence buyers want autonomy that fits the fleets and supply chains they already run.
🔗 Automotive World [ https://substack.com/redirect/95e3a3ed-a538-4512-b37c-b6e446187945?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]
Moove exits Nigeria after Uber’s departure
Moove, the Nigerian-founded mobility company valued at $2.1 billion, plans to leave Nigeria after Uber closed its operations there on 2 September, ending a 12-year presence. Moove was founded in Lagos in 2020 and built much of its early growth by financing cars for drivers on Uber’s platform. It now runs about 42,000 vehicles in 13 countries, is Uber’s largest fleet partner worldwide, and operates robotaxis for Waymo. In August it closed a $250 million round led by Mubadala. 
🔗 Bloomberg [ https://substack.com/redirect/10ce60a6-0ee8-4fdf-a6da-9658cf24f41f?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]
XPeng names its robotaxi and opens registration
XPeng has named its robotaxi “XPENG YOYO” and opened online registration to the public in China, through invitation codes. Until now, rides were limited to paid internal testing at 1 yuan per trip. The pace this year has been quick: the first mass-produced robotaxi rolled off the line in Guangzhou in May (CW22), automated pickup and drop-off followed in July, and in August XPeng received permission to test on certain Guangzhou roads without a safety operator in the driver’s seat. The technical route is the interesting part. Each car runs on four in-house Turing chips with 3,000 TOPS, which XPeng calls the highest in-vehicle compute in the world, and on VLA 2.0, the same model Volkswagen licenses for China (CW24). It drives without lidar or HD maps. So after this week’s Tesla section, there is a second robotaxi without lidar worth watching. XPeng wants to stay a technology provider and leave operations to partners, earning from hardware, services and commission sharing. It has not disclosed a fleet size.
