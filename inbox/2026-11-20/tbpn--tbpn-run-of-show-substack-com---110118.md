The Riemann Hypothesis has been solved (maybe, but feels like probably). Levent Alpöge posted that he believes he found a proof with Claude’s help, but Scientific American reports it as unverified, still needing expert review. Clay Institute still lists RH as an unsolved Millennium Prize Problem.
Terance Tao had a great description why Riemann is so important in the math community on Conversations with Tyler:
The Riemann Hypothesis is a really big prize in mathematics. Recently we’re making some progress on that. It concerns one of the most basic objects in mathematics, the prime numbers, which we now use all over the place, like in cryptography. For instance, a lot of cryptographic protocols that we use to encrypt our finances rely on properties of the prime numbers. We look at the prime numbers, and they behave very randomly. Sometimes they’re close together, sometimes they’re far apart. The Riemann Hypothesis is a very precise way of quantifying this random-looking nature of the primes, and it has many, many consequences.
Any part of mathematics that touches the primes, if you know the Riemann Hypothesis, normally your results get stronger. There’s a large chunk of results in number theory which are conditional. If you have the Riemann Hypothesis, you get this good result. Otherwise, we can’t say anything. Most open questions in mathematics are like puzzles. We don’t solve them for their own sake. They’re useful for gaining insight and help us eventually understand more realistic, real-world problems. The Riemann Hypothesis is one of the few mathematical questions which is actually quite useful for its own sake. Knowing that it’s true would actually imply a lot of other things. - Terence Tao
