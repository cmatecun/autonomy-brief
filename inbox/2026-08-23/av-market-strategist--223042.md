One of those companies is winning the deployment race. The other one is telling you how the economics work.

The Leader’s Constraint Is No Longer Software
Waymo won the right to operate almost everywhere in California and cannot yet fill the cities it already has. The lead is real. The constraint has moved.
The regulatory position, and what it is actually worth
As we reported in CW34, the CPUC approved Advice Letter No. 4 at the end of the previous week, clearing Waymo to charge for fully driverless rides across 18 counties, 12 in the north and six in the south. That is roughly 40,000 square miles and about 25 million residents.
Grayson Brulte, who tracks AV regulatory filings more closely than almost anyone covering this industry, put a number on what that is worth on the Autonomy Signals podcast this week. His estimate is that Waymo sits about two years ahead of any competitor trying to reach the same regulatory position in California, and he was willing to name it plainly:
“Waymo now has the legal authority to operate in 40,000 square miles in the state of California from a driverless commercial perspective. To put that into population perspective, that is roughly 25 million California residents. I estimate this is about two years ahead of where the competition to get here. So if Waymo 10Xed in a year, where are they gonna be in two years? Waymo officially, I’ll say it for the record, has a regulatory moat in the state of California.”
Two years is a long time in a market where city test-to-launch has compressed from years to months. The reason it holds is the process rather than the decision. That advice letter was filed on 28 January, suspended for further review through 25 September, and required a supplemental filing in May covering unaccompanied minors and rider procedures during service disruptions. Seven months of filing, suspension, protest and supplement, for one letter. A competitor cannot compress that by being better at engineering.
Houston is the same argument in a different form. Waymo opened the city to all riders on Thursday, having started the phased rollout in February. Six months, and more than 100,000 riders taken from the interest list before the doors opened to everyone.
Nevada approves 7,000 robotaxis, and the applicants explain why that number is not real
The Nevada Transportation Authority unanimously approved three permits on Thursday, covering commercial robotaxi operations in Clark County. The headline total is up to 7,000 vehicles over the next 12 months.
The split matters more than the total. Tesla was approved for up to 5,000. Waymo for up to 1,000. Uber for 1,000, which it will run through its partnerships with Motional and Zoox. Zoox separately already holds a permit allowing 100 vehicles of its own.
In CW25 we covered Tesla’s original filing for those 5,000 Vegas robotaxis and described it as a permit ceiling rather than a commitment, on the grounds that the actual Austin fleet at the time was roughly 20 to 25 vehicles and contracting. This week Tesla’s own Cybercab chief engineer, Eric Early, used the same word at the hearing:
“The 5,000 has always been a ceiling for us. I don’t think we’ll be in a position by this time next year to deploy 5,000 vehicles, and it’s not [because of] the technology. I think we would be extremely happy and satisfied if we could get ourselves up to 2,500, maybe a bit higher than that in the next year.”
A company under permanent pressure to sound ambitious told a regulator, on the record, that its own approved number is roughly double what it expects to achieve. That is the kind of disclosure this newsletter has been asking for, and it came from the operator we most often criticise for the opposite.
Not everyone in the room was pleased. The Livery Operators Association and local taxi companies opposed all three permits. Their lawyer, Kimberly Maxson-Rushton, framed it as two problems: “the oversaturation of the commercial transportation industry as a whole in Nevada” and “the overcrowding of the roadways, and specifically the Golden Triangle,” the area between the airport and Las Vegas Boulevard where most AV testing has concentrated.
Uber used the hearing to make the argument it has been making everywhere: that robotaxis should operate on networks that also carry human drivers, allowing cities to absorb autonomy gradually rather than all at once.
Staying in Las Vegas: the operator layer arrives 
Moove has launched operations in Las Vegas, with a local general manager, fleet deployment specialists and technicians on the ground. That fills one of the three fleet operator seats that opened when Waymo went into driverless mode in Las Vegas, Tampa and Denver on 8 July. Tampa and Denver are still open.
Source: LinkedIn/Ming Maa
Speaking to TechCrunch on 5 August, co-founder Ladi Delano named Moove as Waymo’s fleet operator in Phoenix, Miami and Las Vegas, with London to follow. What changed this week is that the operation went live.
Moove, in autonomy, is a Waymo-only company, and Delano is candid that this was partly circumstance. After deciding in early 2023 that the fleet layer was the gap worth owning, he says Moove approached the entire field:
“In 2023, we started talking to every single AV company you could imagine, and as you know, God would have it, luck would have it, we managed to partner with Waymo first.”
The strategic logic underneath it: Delano describes evaluating four groups in the ecosystem, the AV developers, the vehicle manufacturers, the marketplaces like Uber, and the consumer, and concluding that none of them wanted to own the vehicles:
“In this world, who owns the vehicle? Who operates the vehicle? Who orchestrates the vehicle? Who does the servicing, the maintenance? Who does the lost property? Who does the cleaning?”
That is the unsexy layer, named by the person who decided to buy it.
The transition worth tracking is ownership. Moove does not own the Waymo vehicles it operates, and intends to buy them using debt financing. Delano gave no timeline. He also confirmed that Moove already owns robotaxis from another AV developer, without naming which. The stated ambition is to own hundreds of thousands of vehicles.
In CW33 we argued that operating maturity cannot be raised in a funding round, because capital arrives in months while workforce systems, training and regulatory relationships take much longer. Moove’s CEO Ming Maa described the Las Vegas launch in terms that make that point better than an analyst can:
Launching a new city should be a boring event, because all the compounding of small decisions made correctly, over and over, happens many months in advance.
His list of what those months contained: facility readiness, safety protocols, vehicle logistics, field-level risk assessments, vehicle cleanliness standards, rapid response capabilities.
I will keep tracking this layer closely, because it is where I expect the next two years of this industry to actually be decided. Tampa and Denver are still unfilled, London is Moove’s first real export test, and the question of whether any operator can rebuild its model in a foreign city remains open. There is a lot still to happen here.
The California ride data, and why the obvious reading is probably wrong
New CPUC data puts Waymo at roughly 315,000 weekly trips in California in June, with the count ticking down across May and June.
Source: reddit/Senior-Durian6966 [ https://substack.com/redirect/5ed41a59-81f8-4bc7-a1a1-dd51ef7aaaad?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]
Read quickly, that looks like saturation, and some coverage will treat it that way. I do not think it is, and the first reason is one we already reported.
In CW26 we covered Waymo’s fourth recall: 3,871 vehicles, the entire US fifth-generation fleet, over driving at speed into freeway construction zones, with no software fix available and all vehicles pulled off freeways in the interim. We noted at the time that the freeway unlock we had framed in November 2025 as the last structural constraint removed had been switched back off. A ride-count decline in the quarter following a freeway withdrawal is probably not a mystery. Freeway trips are the longest and most valuable in the mix, and they stopped.
There is a second reading, and it is the more strategically interesting one, though it rests on inference rather than disclosure. Waymo is probably not particularly focused on gaining share in its mature California markets at all, and the priority is geographic expansion: launching and testing in a growing number of new cities. Having already proven it can take meaningful share in a market like San Francisco, proving it can repeat that across the country may simply be worth more than squeezing a few more points out of the Bay Area.
If that is right, it is consistent with the regulatory argument above. Being in early conversations everywhere compounds; marginal share in a city you already lead does not.
It is also, structurally, what WeRide has been doing across Europe for the past year. Look at the announcement record rather than the deployment record:
France. Switzerland. Spain. Slovakia. Belgium. Denmark.
Six countries, and in most of them a partnership rather than a fleet. Zurich with Uber and Rydera. Madrid with Uber and Avomo. Copenhagen with GreenMobility. Slovakia, Belgium and France added around them.
Now set that against the vehicle count. When WeRide detailed the Zurich and Madrid plans in CW26, Eric Dong put the combined testing fleet across both cities at roughly 36 vehicles, with Zurich fare-charging due to start on about 12.
Six countries. Dozens of vehicles.
That is not underdelivery, and it is not a company running out of money. Dong explained the constraint himself: until the safety driver comes out, labour costs make material fleet expansion uneconomic. So you cannot scale first. What you can do first is be in the room.
Every one of those markets starts a regulatory clock, a permit process and a local partner relationship that a competitor arriving in 2028 has to start from zero. The flag is cheap. The conversation it opens is not.
Waymo appears to be running the same play at a different scale, with American cities instead of European countries, and with a supply constraint instead of a labour one. Spend limited vehicles on presence in many places rather than saturation in a few, because presence is what compounds into the regulatory position.
I lean toward the freeway explanation as the primary cause and the allocation argument as a secondary effect. The data point that would separate them is simple: if California trips recover once freeway operations resume, the first reading was right.
Waymo’s supply answer: 725 imported robotaxis arrived in July
Waymo opened its next-generation robotaxi, the Ojai, to all riders in Los Angeles, Phoenix and San Francisco this week. Denver, Las Vegas and San Diego follow later this year. For now riders may be matched with one; once there are enough in the fleet, riders will be able to choose between the Ojai and the older Jaguar I-Pace.
The vehicle is cheaper to build, operate and maintain than the Jaguar, and it runs Waymo’s sixth-generation Driver, which is modular by design and intended to work across multiple vehicle types. It also ships with a redesigned interface and Gemini as an in-car assistant.
Underneath the technology it is a minivan built by Zeekr, on Geely’s SEA-M platform. The vehicles arrive in the United States as shells, without any Chinese connected-car technology on board, and are sent to Waymo’s Arizona facility to be fitted with the self-driving system. Tariffs on China-built vehicles add cost to every unit.
Here is the interesting number:
MoffettNathanson tracks Ojai imports through shipping receipts and puts Waymo on pace for 5,000 vehicles landed in the United States by the end of 2026, which would more than double the current Jaguar fleet. In July alone, 725 Ojai vehicles entered the country.
Waymo has about 300 Ojai robotaxis in commercial service today.
One month of imports is more than twice the entire deployed Ojai fleet. Vehicles are arriving in Arizona substantially faster than they are being outfitted, commissioned and put into revenue service. That gap is not a supply problem in the sense the industry has used the term for the past year. Waymo seems to have solved getting vehicles. What it has not yet solved is turning vehicles into working robotaxis at the same rate.
Two chips, two very different reasons
Waymo confirmed this week that it has built a custom ASIC, now in production in its latest robotaxi generation. Worth being precise about what is new: Waymo has always designed its own compute and its own sensors, and integrated third-party silicon from Nvidia and AMD into computers of its own design. What changed is the chip inside that design.
The specifications put it in credible company. Waymo claims more than 1,000 TOPS, which is broadly where Nvidia’s latest autonomous driving systems sit, manufactured on TSMC’s 5nm process. Waymo’s stated reasons are faster sensor data processing, better reflexes in complex urban environments, and reduced cost. It is going into the Zeekr-built Ojai.
Longtime readers will recognise the pattern rather than the news. We have tracked custom silicon as the standard response to Nvidia moving up the stack into full-stack L4 software and competing with its own customers: Rivian’s RAP1 at 800 TOPS, BYD’s Xuanji A3, Xpeng’s Turing at 3,000 TOPS. Waymo is the fourth major operator to reach the same conclusion. It also sits inside Alphabet’s broader effort to control AI compute costs.
Which brings us to the company that has been making its own chips longest.
Tesla’s Cybercab, and the regulatory decision that matters more than the launch
Tesla has told staff it is preparing a public launch of the Cybercab, its first vehicle designed without a steering wheel or brake pedal, with Austin as the starting point and the end of August as the internal target.
The sequence, per The Information: employee rides on public roads first, then the vehicles fold into the existing Robotaxi service in Austin a few days later. Tesla has been testing Cybercabs on public roads since June, has been running employee rides on private roads around its Austin campus since July, and completed emergency training with Austin first responders last week.
The gating factor Musk named on the July earnings call was data: “we need to accumulate driving data that is specific to the Cybercab before we can put a lot of them on the road,” because the vehicle sits on a new platform. Tesla began Cybercab production in Texas last month and has said it no longer expects volume production in 2026.
The scale context is unflattering and should be stated plainly. Tesla reported 380,000 cumulative driverless miles across its Robotaxi hubs in July. Waymo, commercial since 2020, is past 220 million driverless miles with riders. Tesla currently has 186 Model Ys registered for the Austin service. Capex has more than doubled year over year to nearly $5.8 billion, largely on AI.
But the mileage gap is the comparison every outlet will run, and it is not the most consequential thing said about Tesla this week.
That came from Rob Grant on the Autonomy Signals podcast, and it is worth taking seriously because of who is saying it. Grant was at Cruise in 2018, where he worked on the exemption petition for a Bolt without a steering wheel or pedals, and later on the petition for the Origin. He has personally run the process he is describing.
His reading: Tesla is not filing an exemption petition for the Cybercab. It intends to self-certify.
Self-certification is the standard route to market. Every vehicle on American roads is self-certified by its manufacturer, which is what the sticker inside the driver’s door is. Grant’s point is that Tesla has self-certified the Model Y, Model X and Roadster for years and knows the process intimately.
The exception is Zoox. Zoox attempted to put its vehicle on the road without an exemption, that attempt was rejected, and it was forced into a long exemption process covering roughly eight federal standards, granted only weeks ago. That is why Zoox can now charge fares.
Which reframes the story. Zoox was pushed off the default path. Tesla is attempting to stay on it, with a vehicle that has no steering wheel and no pedals.
Why it matters commercially: exemptions carry volume caps and time limits. Self-certification carries neither. If NHTSA does not challenge it, Tesla faces no federal ceiling on how many Cybercabs it builds. Grant notes NHTSA’s posture in the Zoox petition, that ADS performance is not evaluated as part of self-certification review, improves Tesla’s odds further.
The capacity figure attached to this, via the show’s OMEGA analysis, is installed annual Cybercab capacity above 125,000 units at Gigafactory Texas, with production already started. For scale, that single-year number exceeds what Waymo has contracted from Geely or Hyundai across multi-year agreements.
And then the caveat, which comes from the same analysis and which I would put in bold if I only kept one line from this section. The assembly line is explicitly not the binding constraint. The named bottlenecks are unsupervised FSD software maturity, state and local approvals, and geofence expansion.
Which is the whole point. Tesla may be building a manufacturing position no competitor can match, for a service whose actual constraints sit somewhere else entirely.
So the honest read is two-sided, and both sides hold at once. Tesla is behind on deployed miles by a factor of roughly 580, and it may be ahead of every competitor on the manufacturing and certification path. Those are measurements of different things at different stages, and the industry keeps collapsing them into one scoreboard.
Two qualifiers, because the upside case depends entirely on them. NHTSA has not approved this. NHTSA has been silent, and silence is not approval. And Grant flags the durability question directly: a future administration could apply new scrutiny, particularly to the additional no-controls form factors Tesla executives have hinted at, and NHTSA is already working with SAE on actual ADS performance standards. His read is that a vehicle three or four years into safe service is unlikely to be reversed. A new form factor under a new administration is a different matter.
What this means and what to watch
First, the number to track for Waymo is commissioning, not imports. 725 vehicles landed in July against 300 in commercial service. If the deployed Ojai fleet has not grown roughly in line with imports by the end of the year, the ceiling is Arizona throughput and depot capacity, and the “vehicle supply constraint” framing this industry has used needs replacing.
Second, if California trips recover when freeway operations resume, the dip was mechanical. If they do not, the deliberate-allocation reading gets much stronger, and Waymo’s mature markets become a different kind of asset than its growth markets. 
Third, Las Vegas is now the first city with four operators competing under live permits, and the allocation question we raised in CW34 stops being theoretical. Uber will be routing rides between Motional and Zoox in the same market where Waymo and Tesla are running their own demand. Nobody has disclosed how that decision gets made.
Fourth, watch NHTSA’s silence, not Tesla’s launch. The Austin rollout will generate the coverage. The regulatory question underneath it is worth far more, and it will be answered by something not happening rather than something happening.

Pony AI’s Q2 2026 Earnings and Baidu goes fully driverless on Uber in the Middle East
Pony.ai’s robotaxi revenue grew 691% last quarter and its passenger fare income grew 849%. The second number is the one that matters, because fares are paid one ride at a time by people who could have taken something else.
The quarter in numbers
Total Q2 revenue reached US$36.2 million, up 69% from US$21.5 million a year earlier.
The composition is where it gets interesting. Robotaxi revenue hit a record US$12.1 million, up 691%, which CFO Leo Wang noted is itself an acceleration from the 395% we covered back in CW23. Within that, fare-charging revenue grew 849%.
That gap between the two growth rates is the point. Project revenue can be propped up by a single large contract. Fares cannot. They are paid by individual riders choosing a robotaxi over a taxi, a bus or their own car, one trip at a time.
Robotruck revenue grew 40% to US$13.3 million. Intelligent Solutions contributed US$10.8 million, up 4%, with the modest number explained by delivery timing on domain controllers; across the first half that segment reached US$26.3 million, up 77%.
Gross profit reached US$6.4 million, up 83.4%, lifting gross margin to 17.5% from 16.1%. Expanding margin while revenue grows 69% is the harder trick, and Pony managed 1.4 points of it.
Non-GAAP operating expenses grew 9.6%. Revenue grew 68.8%. That spread is the entire operating-leverage argument in two numbers. It shows up everywhere downstream: operating margin improved from negative 285.6% to negative 181.5%, net loss margin from negative 248.3% to negative 125.2%, both better by more than 100 percentage points. Net loss narrowed to US$45.4 million, down 14.9%.
Cash went from US$1.44 billion to US$1.39 billion, with US$44.0 million used in operating activities this quarter against US$25.4 million a year ago, plus US$32.2 million of capex. Management attributes the swing to accounts payable settlement and to inventory and prepaids ahead of second-half fleet expansion. At the current pace this is a company with years of runway, not quarters, but the burn is rising while the loss margin falls.
And one caveat that deserves to be stated loudly, because it qualifies the headline. Wang was explicit about where joint-deployment revenue is currently being booked:
“Under the Joint Deployment Model, we are currently recognizing upfront vehicle delivery revenues, which establishes a solid foundation for us to have high-margin, recurring revenue-sharing income going forward as our fleet operations scale.”
So part of that 691% is vehicle deliveries, not recurring service income. Upfront hardware revenue is real revenue, and it is also the lower-quality kind: it arrives once per vehicle and it does not compound. The recurring revenue-share layer Wang describes is the valuable one. The number to watch next quarter is not whether robotaxi revenue grows, but whether the mix shifts from delivery toward sharing.
That said, and this is the honest other side, we wrote almost exactly the operating-leverage sentence about WeRide seven days ago in CW34. One week later Pony reports almost the same structure.
Two companies, two consecutive weeks, same signal. The Chinese L4 cohort has crossed from growth-at-any-cost into visible operating leverage, and is disclosing it ahead of the US cohort. Waymo publishes no P&L. Tesla does not break out Robotaxi. The companies showing their unit economics right now are the Chinese ones.
Fleet, and why Uber picked Pony
The robotaxi fleet reached 1,975 vehicles at 30 June, against a year-end target of 3,500. Three Gen-7 models are in daily service: the GAC Aion V, the BAIC ARCFOX Alpha T5 and the Toyota bZ4X.
Overseas, Pony has secured more than 4,000 vehicle commitments, led by the 2,000-plus across five European cities with Uber that we covered in CW34. Wang framed those commitments as “a multi-year growth catalyst for 2026 and beyond,” which is a reminder that they are commitments rather than deployments.
James Peng was asked directly why Uber chose Pony for Europe, given how many AV partners Uber now has. His answer was refreshingly unromantic:
“Uber always looks for autonomous driving partners whose technology is reliable at scale and also whose cost structure brings attractive economics. That’s exactly the two reasons that what we can offer on the table.”
He then made a claim worth flagging precisely because it is unverifiable and consequential: “Combining hardware and operational costs, our total cost per mile is the most competitive in the industry.” No number was attached. Nobody in this industry publishes cost per mile. But it tells you what Pony believes it is selling.
Peng also confirmed the regulatory door is opening faster than the European industry admits:
“Compared to last year, there is a clear increase in the openness towards autonomous driving and the willingness to gradually open up some areas for pilot projects in Europe to bolster commercial application.”
That matches CW32, when Momenta received approval for nationwide urban road testing in Germany.
China operations
Registered users passed 1.5 million in August, up from 1 million in March. Half a million new users in five months is where a service stops being a novelty and starts appearing in ordinary travel decisions.
In Guangzhou, service expanded from Nansha into four central districts, Haizhu, Tianhe, Huangpu and Panyu, adding more than 300 square kilometres since January and covering a population above 7 million. Shenzhen went after transit hubs instead: Bao’an International Airport, Shenzhen Bay Port and Shekou Cruise Port, the highest-value trips in any ride-hailing mix. Peng noted the Shenzhen network was stress-tested by Dragon Boat Festival demand, rush hours and heavy rainstorms.
Peng also explained the density logic:
“Expanding the fleet size in this market shortens users’ wait times and boosts user retention. And that, as a result, directly translates into higher daily revenue per vehicle even as we scale up our fleet size.”
The claim is checkable against Pony’s own disclosure. The company has named exactly two cities where unit economics have turned positive: Guangzhou and Shenzhen. Those are also its two largest operational hubs, and the two where it spent this year adding operating area rather than adding cities. Guangzhou alone gained more than 300 kilometres.
The honest caveat is that they are also its oldest deployments, so operational maturity is a competing explanation for the same result. But it is the only evidence anyone has published either way, and it points in Peng’s direction.

Now hold that against the first story of this newsletter.
Waymo seems to spend a constrained vehicle supply on presence across many cities. WeRide has six European countries and dozens of vehicles. Both are betting that being early everywhere compounds faster than being deep first.
Peng is arguing the opposite, and he is arguing it with a margin number.
Both positions can be right, because the binding constraint is different in each case. Waymo cannot saturate mature markets and build up new ones simultaneously because it does not have the vehicles or it cannot commission the Ojai’s fast enough. WeRide cannot expand a European fleet materially until the safety driver comes out, because labour costs make it uneconomic. Pony has neither problem in Guangzhou. Concentration is available to it as a strategy in a way it is not available to the other two.
Pony saturates the highest-value markets first and expands second: more than 20 cities by year end, adding Changsha, Hangzhou and further Greater Bay Area cities, against a domestic market of over 10 million taxis and ride-hailing vehicles. Waymo and WeRide (overseas) seem to plant first and deepen later.
The number that sells the joint deployment model
CTO Tiancheng Lou was asked how operational efficiency is actually achieved. His answer collapses the whole question into one metric:
“At the end of the day, efficiency comes down to one thing: the fleet-to-staff ratio. With traditional taxis, it’s always 1-to-1, where 100 cars need 100 drivers to handle everything, from cleaning, charging, to daily maintenance.”
What replaces those drivers is not a better roster. 
“When our Robotaxis return to a depot, they require zero human assistance, autonomously navigating, locating available chargers, and executing self-parking even in tight spaces. Because of that, we only need 3 people for every 100 Robotaxis to keep daily operations running smoothly. That’s true whether we run them by ourselves or work with partners.”
Notably, Pony does not need a closed, dedicated depot for this. The vehicles share ordinary public parking with human drivers and find their own charging spots.
The final clause is the commercially important one. Three per hundred holds whether Pony runs the fleet or a partner does, which turns an internal efficiency metric into a sales document. An overseas operator taking on Pony vehicles is being quoted a defined labour requirement rather than an open-ended one, and that is exactly the number a fleet partner needs before signing. Lou said as much directly: the ratio is why “more and more partners are joining us to adopt our joint deployment model.”

Zagreb, and the three-party structure
Pony’s European deployment hit a milestone on 19 August. Riders in Zagreb can now book a Pony.ai vehicle through the Uber app, the first time anyone in Europe can hail an AV on Uber. Verne acts as fleet owner and operator; Pony supplies the driver; Uber owns the demand and the rider experience.
One qualification that should not be buried, because we have criticised others for burying it: a licensed operator remains on board, monitoring from behind the wheel, as part of a phased introduction before driverless operations begin. This is a supervised deployment.
Wang laid out the model in the plainest terms yet:
“In most cases, there are three parties each plays a different role: For Pony, we supply our Gen 7 Robotaxi with our virtual driver capability, that is an AI driver, a mobility platform that introduces user demands, and an operation company who can deal with fleet management and maintenance.”
Source: Pony AI Investor Presentation Q2/2026
And the strategic reasoning behind it, which is more interesting than the structure:
“We acknowledge that in different markets, the consumer can have choices on mobility platforms and there are existing operation companies. We don’t want to disrupt these ecosystems in this market, but instead, our joint deployment business model is trying to bring values to form a win-win alliance.”
In practice that means Uber and Bolt as demand platforms, with Verne in Croatia  as local operator. Financially it generates “sharing-based revenue or technology licensing fees,” which Wang expects to become “higher-margin recurring income across the entire Robotaxi operating lifecycle.”
Readers of our WeRide deep dive [ https://substack.com/redirect/ac5a4581-0113-43f6-ac54-59f12b077cb2?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ] will find that familiar. We described WeRide’s overseas structure as: license the virtual driver, hold the permit, rent everything else. Pony’s CFO has now described the same structure in his own words, while both companies continue to run their own fleets and their own apps at home. Two Chinese operators arriving independently at an identical split between domestic ownership and international asset-light licensing is a stronger signal than either company alone.
PonyWorld 2.0, and a claim that now has a number behind it
The technical foundation under all of this is what Pony calls PonyWorld 2.0, and Lou’s description of what it replaced is specific enough to test:
“The old way of entering a new city takes dozens of engineers doing manual work to review local driving issues, analyze the root cause of these issues, upgrade the world model, and re-train onboard models, and then deploy and validate the new models on the road. However, with PonyWorld 2.0, our system leverages AI to resolve these local challenges automatically. This turns city expansion from an effort that used to take dozens of engineers into a human-in-the-loop automatic process that just a few people can run.”
Source: Pony AI Investor Presentation Q2/2026
He gave a concrete example, and it happens to be the city in this week’s other Pony story:
“When we went to Zagreb, we noticed local drivers almost never slow down when they hold right-of-way, even near blind spots. PonyWorld 2.0 caught this difference automatically and we quickly trained a new version of Virtual Driver that fits local habits perfectly, with very few engineers involved.”
The underlying argument is that autonomy is a probability-matching problem rather than a behaviour-copying one. As Lou put it, the chance of a pedestrian jaywalking “isn’t 99%, and it’s not 1% either. Precision means matching the exact real-world probability.” Those distributions differ by city, which is why localisation costs money, and automating the localisation is what makes simultaneous multi-city launches possible.
Robotrucks, and the disagreement worth reading twice
Robotruck revenue grew 40% on deepening long-haul work with Sinotrans through the joint venture. Gen-4 Robotrucks have entered mass production and begun commercial operations, most notably at Shenzhen’s Mawan Port with China Merchants Port, where fully driverless trucks operate alongside human-driven ones.
Peng’s framing of the port deployment is the cross-segment argument in miniature: Pony applied experience from urban robotaxis and long-haul trucking to let its trucks handle traffic interactions inside a port, a third environment neither was built for.
Then there is the L4 light truck, and the detail here is far richer than earlier reporting suggested. Peng’s claim is aggressive: “The light truck shares almost 100% of our Robotaxi’s technology and operational infrastructure.” It was jointly developed with CATL and is described as the world’s first automotive-grade, fully redundant light truck purpose-built for L4. Four months after its unveiling at the Beijing Auto Show, Pony has signed SF Express and China Post Technology, with orders and deployment schedules in place.
The market case is the strongest part. China’s active light truck fleet exceeds 8 million vehicles. And against the low-speed robovans already deployed across Chinese cities, Peng claims the light truck offers 3 to 4 times the cargo capacity at 2 to 3 times the speed, which moves it from parcel delivery into distribution runs to malls, supermarkets and convenience stores.
Now hold all of that against what WeRide’s CEO told us on the podcast this week.
WeRide runs five product lines: robotaxi, robobus, robovan, robosweeper and ADAS. Tony Han’s entire defence of that portfolio is that they share one stack, one computational platform, one AI infrastructure, one labelling operation. And he applies that principle as a veto. WeRide refuses to build robotrucks. Forty-tonne highway vehicles have control and planning profiles unlike sedans and SUVs; mining trucks operate on unstructured ground. Both would require a second technology base.
“That’s when we violate our basic principles, so we don’t want to do it.”
So here are two disciplined Chinese L4 operators, in the same week, both invoking stack reuse as their governing principle, and reaching opposite conclusions about the same vertical. Pony says trucking is stack reuse. WeRide says trucking is what stack reuse forbids.
I do not think either is simply wrong, and the resolution sits in the detail. Han’s objection is specifically about heavy highway trucks, where vehicle dynamics genuinely diverge. Pony’s light truck, an urban-speed vehicle in mapped city environments claiming near-total robotaxi reuse, is the far more defensible half of its position. The disagreement is real at 40 tonnes.
But Pony was not the only Chinese operator with significant news, and for the next one we move to the Gulf.
Baidu takes the Middle East position
From 20 August, riders in Dubai can hail a fully driverless Apollo Go vehicle directly through the Uber app. Riders booking UberX or Uber Comfort in the service area may be matched with one, or they can select the “Autonomous” option to request one deliberately. The initial area covers parts of Umm Suqeim and Jumeirah, with expansion planned.
The fleet is operated by New Horizon Luxury Transport, and the vehicle is Apollo Go’s sixth-generation RT6.
The structural detail is the one to keep. Dubai is the first city anywhere where Apollo Go runs a dual model: commercial driverless rides through its own app, live since March 2026, and now through a third-party platform simultaneously. Baidu’s Nan Yang named it as a first.
We have seen this configuration before, and we know how it ended.
Phoenix was the only market where Waymo ran on its own app and Uber’s app simultaneously. As we covered in CW28, demand flowed almost entirely to Waymo’s own app, the Uber channel never scaled past roughly a dozen vehicles, and Waymo eventually rolled off the platform altogether. Uber disclosed the scope only on the way out, describing it as “intentionally limited.” The conclusion we drew at the time: Uber’s value to an AV developer peaks on day one and decays with every ride. A bridge, not a moat.
Dubai is the same experiment, run by a different company in a very different market. And there are two reasons it may not end the same way.
Waymo in Phoenix was operating on home ground. Its oldest market, its own brand, its own capital, and a rider base that could download an app once and never think about it again. Baidu in Dubai has almost none of that. Apollo Go has been live on its own app there only since March, across two neighbourhoods.
And the rider is different in a way that decides this. Tony Han made the point on this week’s podcast, explaining why WeRide went to Uber in the Gulf:
“When you get out of the airport, you can hail a local taxi, but it’s kind of quite expensive, and sometimes you probably need to speak Arab in order to hail a taxi. But for Uber it’s universal.”
Phoenix riders were locals. Airport arrivals are not, and they will not download a Chinese operator’s app for one trip into Jumeirah.
And then the third difference, which largely settles the other two.
In Phoenix, Waymo was the only autonomous supply on Uber’s platform. If Waymo left, Uber lost autonomous rides in that market entirely. That is what made the exit a weapon.
In Dubai, Uber has two suppliers. WeRide has been running commercial driverless rides in Umm Suqeim and Jumeirah since 31 March, the same two neighbourhoods where Apollo Go launched this week, and it is bookable through Uber under the RTA programme we covered in CW30. Two Chinese L4 operators, one platform, one geofence of a few square kilometres.
Which means Baidu has no exit option. If Apollo Go pulls off Uber, the rider who wants an autonomous trip does not migrate to the Apollo Go app. They get matched to a WeRide vehicle instead, and the demand Baidu walked away from lands with its competitor.
The bridge decays only for an operator who is the sole autonomous supply. Waymo’s leverage rested on being the only option, not simply on being strong. Where a platform holds two or more suppliers in the same service area, the bridge behaves more like a moat, precisely because neither supplier can credibly leave.
Which moves the power to the place we flagged in CW34 and could not yet observe. When a rider in Jumeirah selects “Autonomous,” the choice between Baidu and WeRide is not theirs. It is Uber’s.
So the thing to watch here is whether Uber’s allocation logic ever becomes visible. Two competing suppliers in one small geofence is the cleanest test case this industry has produced, and the disclosure question we raised last week now has an address.
What this means and what to watch
First, the revenue mix matters more than the growth rate. Part of that 691% is upfront vehicle delivery revenue booked on joint-deployment handovers, which arrives once per vehicle and does not compound. The recurring revenue-share layer is the valuable one and it is still ahead. 
Second, operating leverage is now the Chinese cohort’s disclosed advantage, and it is worth pricing. Opex up 9.6% against revenue up 68.8%, margin expanding, loss margins improving by over 100 percentage points. 
Third, the PonyWorld 2.0 claim has moved from marketing into the accounts, which is where it can be falsified. Management has explicitly tied the cost line to the technology. The test is simple: city count is set to rise from the current base to more than 20 by year end. If opex growth stays low through that expansion, the claim holds. If it does not, the cost discipline had another source.
Fourth, Zagreb still has a person behind the wheel, and the date that changes is the date that matters. Europe’s first Uber-bookable AV is a distribution milestone, not an autonomy one.
