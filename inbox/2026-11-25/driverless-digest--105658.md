# Waymo’s California Rides Hit A Record, But Empty Miles Are Piling Up

**From:** Harry from The Driverless Digest <thedriverlessdigest@substack.com>  
**Date:** 2026-11-25T18:56:58.000Z

---

View this post on the web at https://www.thedriverlessdigest.com/p/waymos-california-rides-hit-a-record

The latest data from the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC), covering the third quarter of 2026 and compiled by Robotaxi Tracker [ https://substack.com/redirect/2d279809-5bb2-402f-ba93-d4c08e16bc6a?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ], shows Waymo completing 1,528,823 driverless rides in California in September. That equates to about 51,000 rides a day, or roughly 357,000 rides a week, and it makes September the busiest month Waymo has recorded in the state.
This is the fourth time we have looked at Waymo’s growth in California. The first analysis [ https://substack.com/redirect/ca5a72c0-0656-439c-9cdb-231f8eea5821?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ] showed growth decelerating through the first quarter of 2026. A follow up from Matthew Raifman [ https://substack.com/redirect/37812a76-39ad-4cab-bfed-3cf2a14e29ce?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ] argued the curve was flattening but that this was not simply a demand story. Our most recent piece [ https://substack.com/redirect/057b00ad-1df4-43a2-9708-55913e32fcb6?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ] found weekly trips actually falling in May and June, and suggested the freeway pause might explain it. We set two tests for the next release: do trips rebound, and does average trip length recover?
The answer to both is yes. But the new data also adds a wrinkle of its own.
The Rebound Was Real, But The Quarter Wasn't
September’s total beats the previous record set in May and is up 50% on September 2025. Against June’s roughly 315,000 weekly trips, September’s run rate is about 13% higher.
The quarter as a whole tells a more muted story. Q3 came in at 4.08 million rides, 3% below Q2, because July and August were weaker. By our calculation, July and August together averaged around 288,000 rides a week, about 9% below June’s 315,000. September then jumped roughly 24% to about 357,000 a week. The dip lines up with the freeway pause: Waymo resumed freeway operations [ https://substack.com/redirect/e0ddbf91-80d9-4f85-b1b6-bb2274f99a1c?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ] at the end of July, more than two months after halting them, and access returned gradually.
The second test also passed. Rides with a passenger aboard averaged 4.25 miles in Q3, up from 3.93 in Q2 and above the 4.0 miles we saw in May. This is despite freeways only coming back part way through the quarter, so the longer trips are probably about more than freeways. A larger service area and airport trips are plausible contributors, though the data cannot say.
Two caveats. First, the year-on-year numbers are boosted by a weak summer 2025, when Raifman’s analysis [ https://substack.com/redirect/37812a76-39ad-4cab-bfed-3cf2a14e29ce?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ] showed ridership depressed by the Los Angeles protests until September. This makes September’s 50% the fairer comparison, and the quarter’s 53% a little inflated. Second, September is about 8% above April’s previous weekly peak of 330,400. Waymo is still growing in California, but the gain since spring is modest.
Nearly Half of Waymo’s Miles Now Carry No One
One of the biggest stories in this release is the empty miles. Waymo drove 33.3 million miles in California in Q3, up 18% on Q2 and nearly double a year ago, while rides fell 3%. Most of the extra driving, however, was empty.
Miles with no ride assigned rose 50% in a single quarter, and 47.9% of all miles had no rider aboard, up from 41.1%.
In Raifman’s earlier analysis [ https://substack.com/redirect/37812a76-39ad-4cab-bfed-3cf2a14e29ce?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ], the passenger-occupied share had climbed from 45% to around 58% as the service matured. A fall to roughly 52% hands back about half of that gain.
Why the increase in empty miles? The data cannot tell us, but there are several candidates, and they are not mutually exclusive:
Fleet ahead of demand. The fleet kept growing through every disruption we have previously looked at. A soft July and August with more cars on the road would push the empty share up.
Positioning for expansion. Waymo is preparing new territory, and vehicles driving without a ride assigned may be part of that.
A recovery from the freeway pause. Cars may have been spread less efficiently while the freeway network was only partly back.
There is also a cost to empty miles. Remote assistance stops, where Waymo’s remote team helps a car, totalled 161,824, or about 4 per 100 rides. Per mile the rate was flat at 4.9 per 1,000 miles in both quarters, so remote help scales with miles driven, not rides given. Empty driving is not free operationally.
The Reported Area More Than Tripled
The filing lists 6,298 census tracts across 18 counties, up from 1,796 tracts in five counties. New counties include San Diego, Orange, Alameda, Contra Costa and Sacramento.
This tracks the CPUC’s mid-August approval [ https://substack.com/redirect/ded093f6-5034-42b8-8696-0cc996b629ec?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ] of Waymo’s expansion across the Bay Area and Los Angeles and into Sacramento and San Diego, which covers exactly 18 counties. Waymo said the rollout will be gradual. Trips per tract are redacted, so this shows the area Waymo reports on and not where rides actually happened. It is a statement of intent more than a measure of growth.
Fewer Collisions, More Complaints
The safety numbers improved. Waymo reported 201 collisions, down from 291, even with 18% more miles, which is impressive. This is 6.0 per million miles, the lowest of the seven quarters CPUC has published in this format, and the rate also fell per ride, by roughly 29%, so it is not just a product of more empty miles. CPUC counts any contact, however minor. Waymo reported 3 serious injuries, up from 2 in Q2, and no fatalities.
Complaints went the other way, more than doubling from 514 to 1,135. Pickup and drop-off complaints rose from 79 to 346, accounting for around 40% of the increase. At about one complaint per 3,600 rides it is worth noting that this is still a small number, but the trend is worth watching.
The Next Test Is Already Here
September’s rebound might suggest the freeway problems are behind Waymo, but what happened last week says otherwise.
California’s Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV) banned Waymo from operating on all state freeways [ https://substack.com/redirect/84ea4759-359d-47c3-8b50-5c6660daa1b4?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ], after incidents in which its vehicles drove into construction zones, and Waymo voluntarily suspended freeway service the same day.
None of that is in this data, which ends on September 30. The DMV’s restriction took effect on November 11 [ https://substack.com/redirect/18817cdd-45fd-4a54-ae6e-4b1ad26d3b29?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ], so if it runs to year-end, Q4 will include roughly seven weeks without freeway service in California, our second natural experiment in six months.
The September record is encouraging for Waymo. It was set while freeway access was still returning, which suggests demand does not depend so much on freeways, though we cannot see how many riders had access. The questions for the Q4 release are whether rides hold up without freeways, whether trip length falls back, and whether the empty miles come down or keep rising as Waymo expands across its newly approved territory.
Either way, the next CPUC release should tell us a lot.
