# Tesla Launches the Cybercab, Waymo Opens Three Cities and Borrows $3 Billion

**From:** The AV Market Strategist <avmarketstrategist@substack.com>  
**Date:** 2026-09-07T05:30:09.000Z

---

View this post on the web at https://avmarketstrategist.substack.com/p/tesla-launches-the-cybercab-waymo

👋 Welcome back everyone,
Everybody was waiting for Thursday.
Tesla launched the Cybercab in Austin, the two-seater with no steering wheel and no pedals. It was one of the most anticipated events in this industry this year.
Tesla’s competitors knew the date too. Waymo opened three cities and went to the debt market for the first time in its history, both on Tuesday. Zoox announced airport service in Las Vegas on Thursday itself, after running in that city for a year without it. Uber and Wayve put the UK’s first robotaxis for hire on London streets on Thursday morning.
Then China showed the other side of that. Baidu reported its second quarter, and Apollo Go’s fully driverless rides had fallen from 3.2 million to roughly 1 million. The company explained it in nineteen words. 
Permission is the thread running through the whole issue. Who holds it, who is still waiting on it, and how quickly it can be taken away.
⏱️ ~8000 words, 36 minute read
AD
Everyone’s watching the AV rideshare race through four layers: tech, apps, fleets, and ops. But there’s a fifth layer hiding in plain sight, and it’s worth $200B: charging infrastructure. Without power, an AV can’t serve a single ride. Without a network, it can’t serve a city.
Terawatt breaks down why infrastructure demands its own specialized capital and expertise, why fleet operators can’t easily build it themselves, and what it’ll take to keep pace as AVs scale 200x by 2035.
Read the full breakdown of the industry’s missing piece.
The Cybercab Arrived. The Permission Did Not.
Tesla put a car with no steering wheel on a public road and self-certified it. Wayve put fifteen cars on London streets with a licensed human in every driver’s seat, because the permit that would let it take them out is one nobody has applied for yet. Two markets, two different ways of not having permission yet, and in both of them the regulator now sets the schedule.
Let’s start with the market leader, because Waymo spent the week making sure nobody forgot it exists.
Waymo opened three cities, and one of them is a genuine first
On Tuesday, Waymo began offering public robotaxi rides in Denver, San Diego and Tampa. That takes it to 14 US cities with commercial operations, and it follows the same playbook it has run everywhere else: invite riders on a rolling basis, ramp access slowly, expand the service area from there. A Waymo spokesperson told CNBC each of the three cities starts with dozens of vehicles, with “hundreds over time.” All three are hailed through the Waymo app rather than a partner platform.
The number that came with it is the one worth holding on to. Between the Jaguar I-Pace fleet and the newer Ojai minivan, Waymo has now confirmed it is past 4,000 vehicles. Against roughly 500,000 paid rides every week, and a target of one million weekly rides before the end of this year.
Denver is the one that matters technically. It is Waymo’s first cold-weather commercial market, and Colorado is a state it has never operated in commercially before. As we noted back in CW35, Denver was where Waymo removed its backup drivers and ran employee-only rides, with public service expected “later this year.” Later this year turned out to be three weeks.
There is a detail underneath the launch that most coverage skipped. In Denver and San Diego, the Ojai is the only vehicle riders can get. Not the I-Pace. Only the Ojai.
The Ojai is the Zeekr-built minivan carrying Waymo’s sixth-generation sensor and compute stack, assembled in Arizona, designed to be cheaper to build, run and maintain. It has automatic sliding doors, three screens and a Gemini integration. It is also, by TechCrunch’s count, still a fleet of only about 300 vehicles.
So Waymo opened two brand new markets exclusively on a platform that represents roughly 7% of its own fleet, and it did so in a market with snow. That is a statement of confidence in the new hardware, and it is also the clearest read we have had on the question we raised in CW35, when MoffettNathanson’s import tracking showed 725 Ojai landing in July against about 300 in commercial service. We said then that the constraint had moved from getting vehicles to commissioning them, and that the number to track is commissioning rather than imports. Putting the Ojai alone into two new cities is the first evidence that the commissioning pipeline is opening up.
And then Waymo went and did something it has never done before
On the same day, Bloomberg reported that Waymo is in the final stages of raising debt for the first time in its history.
The size is more than $3 billion. The lenders named are Pimco, Blackstone and Sixth Street, with Goldman Sachs running the process. The paper is unrated, and it could price at more than 500 basis points over the benchmark.
Every dollar Waymo has raised until now has been equity. Most recently $16 billion earlier this year at a $126 billion valuation. So this is a genuine change in how the company funds itself, and Bloomberg frames it the way the banks presumably pitched it, as a way for capital markets investors to get to know Waymo, following the path Uber took when it raised its first leveraged loan in 2016.
I read this as Waymo signalling the size of its ambition rather than solving a funding problem. Alphabet is not short of money. A company that has never needed debt does not go and arrange three billion dollars of it unless the plan for the next eighteen months is considerably bigger than the plan for the last eighteen. Waymo’s own stated goal is one million paid weekly rides across 20 cities globally this year, against 14 US cities and 500,000 today, with groundwork underway in more than a dozen more places including London and Tokyo.
Expect a lot more from this company, and expect it to be expensive.
Zoox was busy too, in two different directions
Amazon’s Zoox announced it will start testing this month in Houston and San Diego, using Toyota Highlander SUVs retrofitted with its mapping and automated driving systems, with human safety drivers on board. That is the standard Zoox entry pattern: retrofitted conventional vehicles map and validate the city first, the purpose-built robotaxi comes later. With those two cities, Zoox will have a presence in 12 US markets.
Worth keeping the scale honest. Zoox’s only paid service anywhere today is Las Vegas. San Francisco has a free service for selected riders, with no announced date for paid operations.
But testing in two cities was not the interesting part of the Zoox week.
Zoox goes to Las Vegas airport
From Thursday, Zoox is carrying paying passengers to and from Harry Reid International Airport in Las Vegas. It picks up and drops off at both terminals, near baggage claim. It is the only robotaxi operator serving that airport.
This is a bigger deal than the announcement makes it sound.
In CW34, writing about Waymo’s Allegiant Stadium deal, we said that every casino in Las Vegas is private property requiring its own agreement, and that the city was about to carry the deepest bench of autonomous operators in America competing for a finite set of venue agreements. Two weeks later Waymo signed The Venetian, and we set out the mechanism in CW36: the permits are abundant, the vehicles are not, and the curbs are finite. Tesla, Uber and Waymo all took Nevada permits last month, and together those allow up to 8,000 robotaxis across Clark County over the next twelve months.
An airport is the most valuable curb in any city, and it is usually the last thing a regulator hands over. 
Here is the part that is genuinely new, and I have not seen it happen before in this market.
Zoox now has a better curb position at that airport than Uber and Lyft do. Zoox riders are collected at baggage claim in both terminals. Uber and Lyft passengers walk five to ten minutes to the upper floor of a nearby parking garage, because that is where Las Vegas has put them. Every airport story in autonomy until now has been about the AV being disadvantaged at the curb, restricted to a distant lot, banned entirely, or stuck behind a taxi lobby. This is the first time the robotaxi has the better spot and the human incumbent has the walk.
Brad Templeton’s piece in Forbes makes the operational case for why this should go further, and it is worth reading in full. His second law of robocars is a good one: robots should wait for humans, humans should never wait for robots. A taxi rank works because cars queue and you take the first one. Uber works because a specific car is assigned to a specific passenger, which is why you wait. A robotaxi has no driver to treat fairly, no fare to accept or decline, and no objection to idling, so there is no structural reason to keep the Uber model at an airport at all. Templeton also notes the honest limitation of the Zoox vehicle in this specific setting: the face-to-face seating is good for groups, the four-wheel steering should help in airport pick-up chaos, and there is nowhere to put luggage except between your legs.
The event of the week: Tesla launches the Cybercab
Tesla launched the Cybercab in Austin, and the strangest thing about it was how quiet it was.
There was no livestream. No journalists. The invite list was short and consisted mostly of pro-Tesla content creators, several of whom were made to sign NDAs and agreed to an embargo until the event was over. Tesla influencers who had built live shows around the stream ended up broadcasting a webpage that tracks the number of registered autonomous vehicles in Texas instead. Pro-Tesla accounts who were not invited complained publicly about it.
By all accounts the keynote ran about fifteen minutes, and Elon Musk does not appear to have spoken at it.
For a company that has spent a decade building anticipation for exactly this vehicle, and whose CEO posted an AI-generated image of the Cybercab consuming Austin in a dust storm the night before, that is a remarkable choice of format. Set against the ordinary Tesla product launch, with its arena, its livestream and its global audience, this was closer to a soft release.
How many cars are we actually talking about
This is where the coverage got messy this week, and it is worth being precise, because three different numbers were in circulation and they measure three different things.
45. Cybercabs registered with the Texas Department of Motor Vehicles as of this week.
420. All autonomous Tesla vehicles registered in Texas, on the state’s own tracker at the time of Thursday’s launch. (Now 432)
Source: Texas AV Tracker
264. Tesla vehicles operating unsupervised, according to Robotaxi Tracker, across Austin, Dallas, Houston, Miami, Orlando and Tampa. The Bay Area service is still supervised and does not count here.
Source: Robotaxi tracker
Registered is not operating. A car on a state register may be sitting in a parking lot, and dozens of Cybercabs have been spotted staged in lots around the country for weeks. Texas is not six cities. And the only clean comparison in that list is 264 against 4,000 Waymos, because those are the two figures that describe vehicles actually carrying passengers without a human supervising.
That ratio is roughly 15 to 1 in Waymo’s favour, on vehicle count alone, before you get to rides, miles or cities.
What we learned about the car itself
Tesla released a set of PDFs and updated the terms of service in its Robotaxi app on Thursday. The rest came from the handful of creators who were in the room. Some of what emerged is genuinely interesting.
Children under 13 cannot ride in the Cybercab “at this time.” They can ride in Tesla’s Robotaxi Model Y vehicles, where the limit is 8 to 17 with an adult. Every passenger under 18 needs an adult with them in either vehicle.
The reason may be structural. The Cybercab does not have LATCH anchors, the standard mounting points for child seats. A child seat can only be fitted using the seat belt. Tesla has been explicit for years about removing parts to take out cost, and this looks like one of those decisions meeting a rule it cannot design around yet.
The crash behaviour is documented and, credit where it is due, it is thorough. On airbag deployment the doors unlock, the hazards and interior lights come on, the high voltage battery is disabled, the windows drop to vent position, the car brakes to a stop and parks itself, and the infotainment system opens a two-way connection to Tesla’s rider support team. The door unlocking is not a small detail: Tesla agreed to recall three million cars in China last month over electronic door latches that could trap occupants after a crash. Inside the Cybercab, the manual release sits in an obvious place on each door armrest.
The car uses brake-by-wire, with electronic actuators on the calipers instead of a hydraulic system. Musk framed it as removing the complexity of routing plumbing through the car. 
Tesla’s Cybercab fleet form
On Thursday, Tesla put up an interest form for businesses. The options include buying Cybercab fleets, building mobility hubs and infrastructure, event collaboration, and “other.” The page says it “helps us build our robotaxi network.”
This is the most consequential thing Tesla did all week.
Tesla has spent the last two years running its robotaxi service entirely in-house. First with modified Model Y vehicles, now with the Cybercab. When Musk was asked on the Q2 call about opening the platform, he closed the door: “We expect to be vertically integrated with Robotaxi.” We took that at face value in CW31, and we reasoned that Tesla declines outside distribution because it expects vehicle supply, rather than demand, to be the binding constraint.
A form asking third parties whether they would like to buy fleets and build infrastructure points the other way.
This is an indicator rather than proof. Tesla has not said it will sell Cybercabs to third-party operators, and a web form commits it to nothing. What it does show is where the company thinks the next phase goes, and the direction is clear enough: Tesla wants to scale, it does not appear to want to do it alone, and it may not intend to be only a robotaxi operator. Fleet purchasing and mobility hubs are two different businesses from running a ride-hailing service. 
There is already a bench of companies who would answer that form. Moove raised $250 million last month at a $2.1 billion valuation and runs Waymo’s fleet operations in Phoenix, Miami and Las Vegas, and told TechCrunch it intends to start owning the vehicles it operates. Avomo and New Horizon do the same work for Uber. Avis and Hertz are circling the category. If Tesla opens the door, that ecosystem can put Cybercabs into markets faster than Tesla can staff depots itself.
And then, hours later, NHTSA opened a file
On Friday morning, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration opened an investigation into Tesla’s decision to launch the Cybercab on public roads with no steering wheel and no pedals. It came hours after the first Cybercabs carried passengers in Austin.
Federal vehicle safety standards currently require manual controls, including brake pedals. The Department of Transportation has proposed removing those requirements for vehicles designed to be driven autonomously, and NHTSA said on Friday that it looks forward to “removing unnecessary barriers to American AV innovation in the coming months.” Then it added the sentence that actually matters:
“Until that work is completed, however, existing standards remain in force.”
Tesla told the agency it self-certified the Cybercab as compliant with all Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards. Self-certification is the normal route to market in the United States, and Tesla has self-certified every vehicle it has ever sold. What NHTSA has opened is an audit query to “examine the process and technical data on which Tesla relied when certifying the Cybercab,” and specifically to look at the extent to which that certification depended on Tesla deciding that certain federal standards simply do not apply to a car with no driver controls.
Administrator Jonathan Morrison put it this way: “NHTSA fully supports the safe development and deployment of automated vehicles. But as the federal regulator, we need to ensure that all of our laws are followed.”
The precedent here is Zoox, and it is instructive. Zoox self-certified its cabin-like robotaxi in 2022. NHTSA issued a special order for more information, then formally opened an audit query the following year, the same process it is now using on Tesla. That process slowed Zoox’s path to commercialisation considerably. Zoox eventually went the other way, applying for a temporary exemption from eight federal standards, which it received in July 2026. That exemption is what let it start charging for rides in Las Vegas on 10 August, and it comes with a cap of 2,500 vehicles per year for two years.
Whether Tesla faces a similarly long process is unknown. What is known is that a filing exists, it was opened within hours of launch, and the agency that would need to rewrite the rules has confirmed the old ones still apply.
My read on the Tesla launch
One thing deserves to be said plainly, because it is the part most likely to get lost in a week this noisy.
On Thursday afternoon, members of the public began paying for rides in a vehicle with no steering wheel and no pedals, in a major American city, across a full operating domain. You give it an address and it takes you there. Only one other vehicle without manual controls has ever carried paying passengers on American roads, and that is the Zoox in Las Vegas, which runs a fixed network of six or seven stops. Tesla is the first to do it point to point. Whatever happens to the ramp from here, that is a genuine milestone for Tesla and for autonomy in the US.
That said, there are two ways to read this week, and both are supportable from what we can see.
Read it favourably, and a company that had a couple of dozen unsupervised cars over the summer now runs 264 across six cities, has tripled its unsupervised mileage in six weeks, has put a purpose-built vehicle with no controls into public paid service across a full ODD, and has opened a channel to third-party fleet operators against a stated production capacity above 125,000 vehicles a year. That is the shape of a company preparing to scale, and the muted launch reads as a deliberate decision to stop making promises it then has to defend.
Read it sceptically, and a company that had spent years building to this moment held a fifteen-minute closed-door event with no livestream, its CEO apparently did not speak, the vehicle it launched cannot carry a child under 13 or a proper amount of luggage, and the federal regulator opened a file on its legality within hours. That is not the behaviour of an organisation that is certain.
On that last point I want to be fair. The investigation is what NHTSA is supposed to do. Self-certification is the ordinary route to market, and the agency’s job is to examine the process afterwards, particularly for a vehicle unlike anything it has seen certified before. Nothing about the filing stops Tesla operating today. It does, however, put the question of which regulatory regime Tesla ends up in onto the table, and that question carries real commercial consequences.
So where do I land. I lean sceptical on the pace of what comes next, and I hold it loosely. Thursday settled whether Tesla could build this vehicle and whether it drives. What it did not settle is how quickly the fleet grows, and there the constraint we have carried since CW31 still applies, stated by Musk himself: Tesla must accumulate driving data specific to the Cybercab chassis before it can scale it, and ten million customer cars did not pre-train the two-seater. The 264 unsupervised vehicles are mostly Model Ys, and the Cybercab count is 45.
What would change my mind is simple, and it is the same test as before with a corrected baseline. If Cybercab-specific numbers move materially by October, the cost curve argument starts to bind and the bull case gets serious. If 45 registered vehicles is still roughly where we are, Thursday was a product launch and not a ramp.
Now let’s take a look at what Uber did this week.
Uber and Wayve launched supervised robotaxi service in London 
Four weeks ago, we made a call about London. Not about the launch everybody was waiting for, but about the paperwork sitting underneath it:
“The thing to watch in London is not only the launch. It is also the applications. The private hire licence tells you Wayve can carry passengers this summer with a human aboard. The APS permit will tell you who owns the service in 2030.“
This week Transport for London answered it, on the record, in a statement to Bloomberg:
“It is our understanding that no operator has completed the first stage of the process, which is registering vehicles with the Vehicle Certification Agency, and no applications for a DVSA permit, or TfL consent to operate in London have been received.”
Nobody has started. Not Wayve, not Waymo, not Baidu. Zero vehicles registered with the VCA, zero permit applications at the DVSA, zero consent requests at TfL.
That is a stronger answer than the call anticipated, and it reframes everything else that happened in London on Thursday.
What actually launched
Londoners can now be matched with a self-driving car through the Uber app. Fifteen Wayve-powered Ford Mustang Mach-E vehicles, licensed by TfL last month as private hire vehicles. The option only appears after you book, and only if one happens to be nearby. The fare is identical to a standard UberX, Uber Electric or Uber Comfort trip, and you are not prompted to tip. The cars can go anywhere in London except the airports.
Every one of them has a TfL-licensed safety driver behind the wheel.
Wayve licenses its AI Driver software to Uber and charges based on usage. Wayve manages the fleet for now, with Otto Car overseeing the safety drivers, and Uber takes over fleet management with Otto Car “in the coming months.” The depot is near Wayve’s King’s Cross headquarters. Alex Kendall expects the fleet to deliver “thousands” of rides a week across Greater London.
This is a real milestone and I want to say so clearly. It is the UK’s first robotaxi service for hire, it is Wayve’s first commercial public service anywhere in the world, and London is a genuinely hard city: narrow, old, irregular, wet, and full of people behaving unpredictably. Both the Bloomberg and Guardian test rides describe a car that handled it well. It nudged into bumper-to-bumper traffic in a recognisably human way, took tight lane changes, dealt with a protest in Parliament Square, complied when a cyclist waved it forward at a mini roundabout, and in one nice moment pulled away and re-parked itself after stopping in front of gates other cars were trying to use.
On the BBC’s ride the driver took the wheel when a parked van’s rear doors swung open on a narrow side road and the car braked hard. Wayve says that was the driver’s choice and the system would have handled it. 
For a supervised service in central London, that is a decent showing. It is not a driverless service, and the gap between the two is not a matter of software confidence. It is a matter of paperwork that has not been filed.
The two clocks that have not started
To run without a safety driver in London, an operator needs three things: safety audits from the Vehicle Certification Agency, a passenger service permit from the Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency, and consent from Transport for London. As of this week, none of those processes has been opened by anyone.
That is the first clock, and it stands at zero.
There is a second one, and it has had almost no attention. The vehicles Wayve intends to use for driverless operations are Nissan LEAFs, and they are still in development. When they arrive they go through the same permitting process from the start. And critically, they will not be covered by the private hire licences the Mustangs are running on today. The licence Wayve has does not travel to the car Wayve needs.
Kendall was straightforward about it. The technology is “still maturing.” He declined to give a timeline for driverless service and said the company is working on the vehicle platform, the safety validation and the regulatory approval in parallel: “As soon as they come together, we’ll launch driverless services.”
Earlier this year the government and the industry were suggesting fully driverless cars for hire in London by the end of 2026. The Department for Transport now says it is working towards a framework being in place for 2027, and will not give a date. TfL’s Christina Calderato says they are “not holding up any decision” but “taking time to get it right.” Both of those are the language of a process that has not begun.
So the honest position on London is that a driverless commercial service before 2027 is now very unlikely, and that has nothing to do with whether Wayve’s AI can drive the city.
The Zagreb pattern, running for the second time
This is the second supervised commercial robotaxi service Uber has launched in Europe this year. The first was Zagreb, earlier this summer, with Pony.ai and Verne, also with a human in the front seat.
We said at the time that Zagreb was a real milestone and that it was also not the thing the market was waiting for, and the same is true here. Uber has now demonstrated twice that it can put a supervised autonomous service into a European city and make it commercially live. What neither launch demonstrates is the ability to take the driver out, because in both cases the human is what makes the service legal.
What this means and what to watch
First, the Tesla test still resolves in October. The baseline is now 264 unsupervised vehicles across six cities rather than 20 to 30, and the number that matters inside it is how many are Cybercabs rather than Model Ys. If the Cybercab count moves from 45 registered into meaningful commercial operation by early October, the cost curve argument starts to bind. If it does not, Thursday was a launch and not a ramp.
Second, the NHTSA audit query is the most consequential open file in American autonomy right now, and the reason is commercial rather than technical. If Tesla stays on self-certification, it faces no volume cap and no time limit. If it ends up where Zoox ended up, on a Part 555 exemption, it inherits a ceiling. Tesla has stated production capacity above 125,000 Cybercabs a year. Zoox’s exemption allows 2,500. Watch which regime Tesla ends up in, because that single question does a lot to the Cybercab business case.
Third, in London, watch the VCA register. The first operator to register vehicles with the Vehicle Certification Agency has started the clock on the permit that decides who can run a driverless service in that city without a partner. Right now that register is empty, which means the race has not started and everyone is equally far from the line. 
Fourth, watch Waymo’s commissioning rate in the three new cities. Dozens of vehicles each today, “hundreds over time,” and in two of the three it is Ojai only against a fleet of about 300 Ojai’s right now. If Denver and San Diego go from dozens to hundreds within a quarter, the commissioning bottleneck we identified in CW35 is clearing. If they stay at dozens through the winter, the constraint is still real and the 1 million weekly ride target for year end is in trouble.
Fifth, watch which operator takes the next airport. Zoox has Harry Reid to itself for now, in a county cleared for up to 8,000 robotaxis over the next twelve months. Airport access is the highest-value curb in the business and it has just been shown to be winnable. That will not stay uncontested for long.

🇨🇳 Baidu’s Autonomous Rides Drop, Pony’s Korea Expansion and WeRide’s 10K Robotaxi Target for Saudi Arabia
Apollo Go went from 3.2 million driverless rides in a quarter to about 1 million. Baidu explained it in nineteen words.
Baidu reported second quarter results, and the international section of the call was genuinely impressive. In one quarter Apollo Go collected:
The first fully driverless testing permits in Hong Kong, with testing starting on Airport Island in July, making it the first autonomous ride-hailing provider anywhere to run fully driverless testing in a right-hand drive, left-hand traffic market
Open-road testing in London with Uber and Lyft
A fully driverless commercial launch in Dubai in July, now at the largest driverless scale of any operator in the city, bookable through both the Apollo Go and Uber apps
Open-road testing in Switzerland with PostBus
An MOU in Kazakhstan with Turlov Private Holding
That is a serious quarter of international execution, and Robin Li’s framing of it was the most useful thing on the call. Hong Kong is one of the most demanding urban mobility markets in the world, and clearing it gives Apollo Go a credible claim to every right-hand drive market that follows. He said explicitly that the Hong Kong experience is already accelerating the London work. 
Then you read past the milestone slide.
The nineteen words
Here is what Baidu said about the domestic number:
“Ride volume during the quarter was temporarily affected by operational adjustments in certain domestic cities due to regulatory considerations.”
Alongside it, a mention of a “systematic review to further strengthen the robustness of our autonomous driving systems and the rigor of our operational processes.”
That is the tell, and here is what sits behind it.
At the end of March, Apollo Go vehicles stalled on a busy elevated road in Wuhan. Passengers were trapped for 90 minutes. The in-car SOS button did not work. Wuhan traffic police found a system malfunction. We covered it at the time.
Within weeks, three Chinese ministries suspended all new AV licences nationwide. No new vehicles into fleets, no new test projects, no new cities. For any operator, not just Baidu. And Baidu’s operations were suspended in Wuhan, its largest market and the first city where it reached unit economics break-even.
So the decline has three causes imo rather than one, and only the vaguest of them made the call. Wuhan went dark. The national freeze then blocked fleet growth everywhere else, in every city. And when service came back in July, some cars returned with safety drivers aboard, which means those rides do not count as fully driverless at all and never appear in the number Baidu reports.
The good news, and it is genuine, is that this looks temporary rather than terminal. The review closed in late June. Licensing resumed city by city from July. Apollo Go was back on Wuhan streets within days. Baidu says the affected cities were running again by August, and Li told analysts the company is confident ride volume regains momentum over the coming quarters. Shenzhen is picking up quickly and is now one of the largest markets.
I would expect a recovery in Q3. I would also expect the fully driverless count to lag the real operational recovery for as long as safety drivers stay in those cars.
What this actually says about Chinese AV regulation
This is a company with 23 million cumulative rides and, by its own measurement, one airbag deployment every 14.4 million kilometres. That is a strong safety record by any standard in this industry. It still had to pull operations in its home market.
Scale does not buy immunity. If anything it raises the price of a single systemic failure. One failure, in one city, on one night, halted licensing nationwide for three months. Pony.ai fell 5.5% and WeRide 4.7% on the news, and neither had anything to do with it.
Chinese AV regulation is usually described as permissive. Fast in both directions is the better description, and the second direction belongs in any valuation of a Chinese operator.
Baidu also changed where its shares live
Two corporate items, both quiet and both worth a line.
On 31 August, Baidu’s voluntary conversion from a secondary listing to a dual-primary listing on the Main Board of the Hong Kong Stock Exchange took effect. It is now dual-primary listed in both Hong Kong and on Nasdaq, with shares and ADSs remaining fungible.
And on 7 September, Baidu’s Class A ordinary shares are included in the Shanghai-Hong Kong Stock Connect programme. 
Neither is an autonomy story, but together they widen Baidu’s investor base considerably at a moment when the company is funding an international robotaxi rollout. Access to capital is a competitive variable in this industry, and Baidu just improved its own.
Let’s look at the week for the other Chinese players.
Pony.ai is going to Korea.
Pony signed a strategic partnership in Seoul on Friday with local mobility firm FutureLink to bring 200 Level 4 robotaxis to Korea by 2028. It starts with 10 vehicles for domestic certification, with the remaining 190 following once that process completes. Service launches in Seoul’s autonomous driving pilot zone in Gangnam, then expands across the city, the wider Seoul metropolitan area, and other major Korean cities. Certification and commercial launch timing is not yet fixed.
The structure is the joint deployment model we set out in the Pony deep dive [ https://substack.com/redirect/efd038b0-84c5-40e5-8283-ce7d291cea68?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ], and this is a textbook instance of it. Pony supplies the seventh-generation vehicles and the technology. FutureLink handles regulatory certification, market entry, vehicle and service operations, data management and localisation. A joint venture between the two is under consideration. Pony brings the driver, the partner brings everything that touches the local market, and the capital intensity is shared rather than carried.
FutureLink is not arriving empty-handed either. It has been running day-and-night testing in Gangnam using Kona Electric vehicles fitted with Pony’s kit, covering about 80,000 kilometres without an accident.
Worth noting the context this lands in. Chinese companies are pushing into Korea across the board: BYD is now the fourth-largest imported vehicle brand there, Zeekr launches the 7X this autumn, and XPeng, Chery and Xiaomi are all weighing entry. Korea’s own answer is Hyundai, which is pursuing a vehicle foundry strategy to supply robotaxi hardware to autonomous driving companies while developing its own stack, targeting a full software-defined vehicle production system in 2028 and vehicles running its Atria autonomous technology in 2029.
WeRide wants 10,000 robotaxis in Saudi Arabia.
AGBI reported that WeRide plans between 7,000 and 10,000 autonomous taxis in Saudi Arabia by the end of the decade, according to Jennifer Li, the company’s head of international and CFO.
The near-term checkpoint is much smaller and much more useful. WeRide already runs robotaxis in Riyadh with Uber and local partner Ai Driver, operating from the airport, and expects to reach 100 vehicles in Riyadh by the end of this year. Growth from there is described as exponential.
The expansion follows the zone method Tony Han described on EP24 of Autonomy Insiders [ https://substack.com/redirect/7f534a67-1929-45d4-a765-63c919fc5066?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]: start from independent zones, grow each outward, and let city coverage be the output rather than the target. The next zones are named. WeRide will shortly extend into Olaya, central Riyadh’s business district, and will launch a separate project next year in Qiddiya, the entertainment giga-project city just south of the capital.
It is not only robotaxis. WeRide already runs autonomous buses in AlUla, the tourist city in the northwest, and has introduced its Robosweeper street-cleaning vehicles in the Kingdom. Those have had to be recalled and upgraded to cope with the dust and sand levels in the Gulf, which is a good reminder that localisation in this business is a physical engineering cost and not only a regulatory one.
Saudi Arabia is targeting 15% of the country’s public transport vehicles being autonomous by 2030, and has been installing smart roadside communication devices since August 2024.
And then there is the detail I liked most in the whole story, because it is exactly the sort of thing that decides whether a service works. Li says the response to the self-driving Ubers has been generally positive, but that the cars frustrate people by not arriving precisely where the customer is. Unlike a human driver, they cannot be asked to pull forward a few metres. Riders have to walk to a designated pick-up point.
In most markets that has not been a big issue. In Saudi Arabia it is, and Li explained why in one line: “People don’t mind walking a minute or two minutes in the winter. But nobody wants to walk in August.”
On EP24, Tony Han argued that heat is a demand driver for robotaxis in the Gulf, because nobody wants to stand at a bus stop at 45 degrees. The same 45 degrees is now the reason a two-minute walk to a pick-up point is a problem. Same variable, working in both directions, and it is going to shape how these fleets are allowed to use the curb in that region.
What this means and what to watch
First, Baidu’s Q3 print is the test, and the metric is not total rides. Watch the fully driverless ride count specifically, because that is the line safety drivers suppress even after a city is technically back in service. If total volume recovers strongly but the driverless number lags, the recovery is real and incomplete, and you will only see that if you read past the headline.
Second, treat Chinese regulatory speed as a two-sided variable in any valuation. The permissive framing is half the picture. A single incident in one city cost the market leader two-thirds of ride volume and froze new licensing for every operator in the country for three months. That is a risk factor with a demonstrated magnitude now, not a theoretical one, and it applies to Pony, WeRide, DiDi and Momenta exactly as much as it applies to Baidu.
Third, for Pony in Korea, the number to watch is the certification, not the 200. The deal is well specified, with a named partner, a named city, a named pilot zone, a named vehicle platform and a phased volume. What it does not have is a date on Korean domestic certification, and that is the only gate that matters. Ten vehicles clear it or they do not. The other 190 follow automatically if they do.
Fourth, for WeRide in Saudi Arabia, the checkpoint is 100 vehicles in Riyadh by December, not 10,000 by 2030. A four-year target of that size is an ambition until the intermediate steps land. The Riyadh number arrives in under four months and is entirely verifiable, and Olaya opening on schedule is the second one.

💡 Quick Takes
DiDi opened fully driverless trials of its purpose-built R2 in two megacities at once
DiDi Autonomous Driving launched fully driverless service trials of the R2, developed jointly with GAC Aion, across selected demonstration areas in Beijing and Guangzhou, bookable by the public through the DiDi app. The vehicle carries 33 sensors and a triple-domain fusion central computing platform with multi-layer redundancy, and claims five-star safety compliance under both C-NCAP and Euro NCAP. The cabin is built around the rear passenger, with an AI voice system handling PIN verification, trip start, climate, windows and lighting without physical controls. The R2 entered public-road testing in Guangzhou, Beijing and Shenzhen after delivery in January 2026, following DiDi’s 24/7 fully driverless pilot in Guangzhou that started in December 2025.

May Mobility and NTT will put the first autonomous e-Palette on Japanese public roads
The two companies will deploy multiple Toyota e-Palettes running May Mobility’s autonomous driving system, the first public road operation of an autonomous e-Palette in Japan, with deployments at multiple sites during fiscal 2026 and more in 2027. May Mobility already runs demonstrations and services in nine Japanese cities including Tokyo, Nagoya and Hiroshima, and NTT ran e-Palette demonstrations at its Chofu training centre from March 2025 to March 2026. The stated goal is to build the operating evidence required under Japan’s Level 4 certification programme. NTT also launched an Operation Assist platform in July for remote monitoring and mission-critical communications.

Applied Intuition and HUMAIN will build what would be the world’s largest autonomous trucking network, in Saudi Arabia
The two announced a strategic collaboration to deploy thousands of autonomous trucks across key Saudi logistics corridors by 2030. HUMAIN is the PIF-owned full-stack AI company, and this is the first major initiative under its physical AI strategy, with later phases exploring robotaxis, ports and mining. Applied Intuition’s Self-Driving System and Vehicle OS sit at the centre, already running on roads in the US, Europe and Japan, where it operates L4 trucking with Isuzu. The operating environment is the hard part: extreme heat, blowing sand and long-haul routes, which the company argues its Komatsu mining work has prepared it for.

Three German companies signed an LOI to put autonomous shuttles into rural public transport
Vetter Verkehrsbetriebe, MoPla Solutions and Bliq will work together on a pilot in the Anhalt-Bitterfeld district in Sachsen-Anhalt, with the long-term goal of integrating Level 4 shuttles into scheduled public transport. The concrete target is five autonomous vehicles bookable by passengers next year, using the Hyundai Ioniq 5 and the seven-seat Kia PV5. Bliq’s remote technical supervision approach lets them run commercially now while the more demanding L4 approval is prepared, so real passenger operations start early rather than waiting. Bliq says it is the first company with EU approval to operate without a safety driver in the vehicle. Vetter brings the public transport and on-demand operating side, MoPla the digital platform covering scheduled routes, on-demand trips and ticketing. The problem being addressed is the right one for Europe: thinly populated areas with low bus frequency and long first and last miles. Anhalt University is providing academic oversight, and the project follows a funding commitment in July.

Momenta’s first results since its Hong Kong listing 
First-half revenue rose 75.9% to RMB 1.6 billion (about $236 million), with technical development services up 81.5% to RMB 995 million and licensing up 67.5% to RMB 607 million. Gross margin reached 73.2%, and the adjusted loss narrowed 96.6% to RMB 14.1 million from RMB 416 million a year earlier, which puts the company within sight of adjusted break-even. New installations in mass-produced vehicles hit about 321,000 units, up 83.7%, taking cumulative installations past one million. It delivered 37 new production models in the half for a cumulative 105, covering 26 automakers, and holds 219 model nominations with 114 not yet at start of production. R&D was RMB 1.16 billion, or 72.6% of revenue, growing far slower than revenue, which is where the operating leverage is coming from. On autonomy specifically: 13 billion kilometres of real-world data, the R7 World Model entering mass-produced vehicles in Q3, robovan pilots in Suzhou, and the first mass-produced robotaxi scheduled for Q4 with a target of hundreds deployed and licences in more than 10 cities by year end.

PlusAI is going public via SPAC, on its second attempt
The autonomous trucking company will merge with Texas Ventures Acquisition III Corp, a SPAC backed by funds managed by Yorkville Advisors, at an $800 million pre-money equity valuation. PlusAI is targeting $40 million to $50 million in contracted revenue in 2026. The context is the part to keep: PlusAI announced a merger with Churchill Capital Corp IX last year, and the companies terminated it in April on market conditions, with that SPAC liquidating in July. A second attempt at a lower profile, with a smaller sponsor, in a sector where Kodiak and Einride have already been through this door.
