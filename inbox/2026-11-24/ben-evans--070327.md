** AI Maths
------------------------------------------------------------

Last month, OpenAI announced a solution to a long-standing high-profile theoretical mathematics problem (Navier-Stokes); this week, a scientist at Anthropic announced a solution to another major problem, the Riemann Hypothesis. The solution is 250,000 pages, plus 17m lines of code.

I am in no sense a mathematician, but it seems to me that there are three types of question to think about. Firstly, what are the constraints of the kinds of problems that can be solved like this, both narrowly within maths and also more generally? Is this limited to problems you could describe crudely as brute force, where you try everything? (And, as a dumb non-mathmo question, are ALL maths problems solvable by brute force if you have enough brute force? Do we know?) How far are these kinds of solutions limited to fields with a closed feedback loop where the machine can check its own answers? What happens when you push to fields where we simply don’t know enough to check without doing a physical experiment, like biology? How many questions have the answer waiting there for us to find, if only we had enough scale and automation (see the decryption story below)?

Secondly, what does it mean if the machine can give us an answer that we can validate as correct but that we might not be able to understand? The obvious reference here is the machine in The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy that’s asked the answer to the purpose of life and replies ’42’. This proof is 250,000 pages of text. Is that useful? There are some mathematicians who object to this simply because they would like the enjoyment of finding the solution, but there are others who object because each solution is a stepping stone to further understanding and 250,000 pages of text might not be a stepping stone to anything.

And third, that’s also a broader question for the use of AI in contexts far outside mathematics. Where and how does it matter if we understand why the computer gave us the answer if the answer was correct? The old debate about AI understanding was whether the model truly understood the question and the answer, and whether that mattered if the model always gave you the right answer. The new debate might be whether we can understand the answers that the model gives us, and whether that matters as long as they’re right. ** Nvidia fallout
------------------------------------------------------------

There’s a lot more noise about Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang getting himself fired. He stated that he would be willing to return if asked, some shareholders think he should be brought back, and there’s a vibe in Silicon Valley (which can be very high school) that this is all some sort of plot. But the dilemma, as I pointed to last week, is that on the one hand he was a huge, important part of the company (and indeed structured it such that it would actually be really hard for someone to replace him).

But on the other hand, and I’m just going put this out there as a wild and crazy suggestion, maybe if you sleep with one of your customers, who you know is involved in some deeply shady and possibly illegal import/export transactions, and have your staff lobby the government on her behalf, and also have affairs with not but one but two of your employees, and then lie about this repeatedly to your board, then that might be a pretty big problem. And that’s just what we know, for certain, so far: it’s entirely possible that there is worse to come out. ** GTA
------------------------------------------------------------

Rockstar Games launched GTA VI, with $3.79bn of sales on the first day. This is a cultural event, even if (like me, and indeed most people) you don’t play these kinds of games, and the game is an impressive creative achievement. However, it also looks like the end of an era. All of that detail, entertainment and creativity is created by people. Someone had to decide that it would be really funny if you opened the door to that truck in a Florida backstreet and someone’s illicit pet tiger jumped out and chased you into a Waffle House. In the future, AI won’t just automate creating that street - it will be generating those kinds of ideas in real time, as you plan. Some people will be very upset by that, but it’s probably as big a deal as 3D or online play. ** Microsoft... integration, orchestration, layers, stacks and confusion
------------------------------------------------------------

Microsoft announced something about its approach to integrating Copilot AI and standalone applications. There’s a half-hour keynote from Satya Nadella with a lot of slides and diagrams with little boxes with product names and arrows pointing to other little boxes with product names. I’m not sure that I actually understood any of this, but that might be the point. I don’t think anyone quite knows how all of this stuff actually fits together: is the model supposed to be at the top, the middle or the bottom of the stack? Where is the orchestration layer and management tooling that controls all of this? Do you add apps into Copilot or Claude, or do you add Claude and Copilot into apps? And as I discussed in my presentation this week, who is it that works out the actual new things you can do with this stuff? It isn’t the users. ** RIP Mailchimp
------------------------------------------------------------

Five years ago, Intuit bought MailChimp, the mail service provider I use to run this newsletter, for $12bn, and this week it’s selling to Bending Spoons for $3.68bn. As I’ve noted before, Bending Spoons has an interesting PE model, buying half-forgotten SaaS companies with locked-in user-bases, plugging them onto a common core of software developers, doubling the prices and paying for the whole thing with high-yield bonds. Effectively they’re strip-mining the sediment of Web1 .0 and Web2.0. But the other side of this, of course, is the price which reflects the collapse in SaaS valuations with the rise of AI. I will probably have to move off Mailchimp, and AI will make it enormously easier for me to do that, which ironically is why Bending Spoons is getting it so cheap. LINK (https://us.list-manage.com/VEuqQOLggXV?e=bc4ee99da9&c2id=e2956131aa985567d31359cb60722597)

** Confusion
------------------------------------------------------------

A decade ago, I wrote an essay looking at all of the web-based no-code productivity apps that were flourishing at the time. All of them were trying to shuffle together some combination of chat, lists, tables, lightweight databases, collaboration and maybe whiteboards into some new kind of free-form workspace, breaking away from the old monolith of spreadsheet, doc, slides, and email. Some of those got quite far, most obviously Slack and perhaps Notion, but none of them got anywhere close to replacing Office, or even Google Docs, and it wasn’t always clear what problem these products were trying to solve. All of those arguments and questions come up again now as people try to work out what it would mean to work with AI. What are the ‘primitives’ or ‘artefacts’ - atomic units of stuff that people create and pass around? What is the data that feeds that, and where is that ‘orchestrated’ (another new concept), and managed? How do you share and permission this? And more importantly, how do you
help people work out what they could do that’s actually new and different?

There are all sorts of product creativity problems in here, but there are also pretty basic conceptual questions. What problems are you trying to solve? What is it that you're trying to do with AI? Is it there to do stuff for you and remove the grunt work that you were doing anyway (what I used to call giving you infinite interns)? Or is it also there to do new kinds of work that you couldn't have done before? Is this automation, or is it a new layer of abstraction that lets you ask different questions? On the other side, where does the AI go within these systems? Do you build AI as a feature within a deterministic system, or do you add deterministic capabilities to an AI system? We don't know which way is up.
