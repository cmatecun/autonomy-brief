The Boomer Put
The best PDF (investment thesis) I’ve read recently is The Boomer Bubble [ https://substack.com/redirect/7fc5d34e-cc00-402f-aa00-520ec6c2a396?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ] by Jason Lupatkin and Jacob Savage of Istari Capital Management. It’s a quick read and does a great job of addressing tons of different corners of the global economy and political landscape.
My read on the core thesis is as follows: The last ~40 years of rising asset prices were sustained by a political compact built around Baby Boomers, who became America’s dominant voters, consumers, and asset owners. Governments repeatedly cut rates, injected liquidity, and rescued markets because protecting accumulated wealth aligned with protecting the median voter (boomers). As they retire and die, that alignment breaks, even though some of the assets might pass down. Younger generations have less wealth to protect and more reason to favor cheaper housing, redistribution, and economic restructuring. This could lead to higher interest rates, greater volatility, and weaker government support during crashes, fundamentally changing the rules investors have relied on since the 1980s.
The key action that defined the Boomer Bubble era (and the balancing force that might go away in the future) was “the put”, described here:
And so the Boomer Compact was born: a forty-year political-cultural settlement in which capital, the intelligentsia, and the median voter found their interests uniquely aligned. Prices would go up; money would stay cheap; entitlement spending would continue to expand; and asset holders would be bailed out, whatever the cost. After Black Monday in 1987, when the Fed put was born, Boomers held an estimated 18% of American household wealth. By 2003, when Bush’s post-tech-bubble capital-gains cuts passed the Senate 50-50, that share had more than doubled to 44%. In 2008, when TARP passed 74-25, the Boomers owned 53% of national wealth. Through no real fault of their own, they had become an immovable, illiquid asset class.
Mechanically, the Fed put meant Greenspan, then Fed Chair, effectively sold (or gave away) a put option to the entire market. This put option was effectively Greenspan saying “if the stock market crashes badly enough, I will cut interest rates and inject liquidity into the economy.”
His exact statement on October 20th, 1987 (the day after Black Monday) was remarkably concise: “The Federal Reserve, consistent with its responsibilities as the nation’s central bank, affirmed today its readiness to serve as a source of liquidity to support the economic and financial system.”
Government interventions in American financial markets happened about every decade since the original Greenspan put. 1998 after the Russian default and LTCM collapse, then in 2001 after the dot-com bust and 9/11, then 2008 after the housing bubble burst, and in 2020 after COVID.
The recent market draw downs are starting to look worthy of yet another government intervention. James Mackintosh asked an important question in the WSJ on Sunday: Has the AI Bubble Already Burst? [ https://substack.com/redirect/e772ccbf-7079-4663-a71a-ff24729d49cd?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]
He highlights 7 key tech companies and how they performed post-dot-com vs today:
Consider some of the big technology names from the dot-com era that are still around. Here are the drops from their high to the end of the year in 2000 and in 2026:
Intel -60% in 2000 vs. -64% in 2026
Oracle -37% vs. -60%
Cisco -52% vs. -30%
Micron -63% vs. -53%
Qualcomm -54% vs. -42%
Seagate Technology -37% vs. -60% (It merged and was spun out again.)
Nvidia -62% vs. -48%
Some are almost identical, some worse, some better. But just from these it would be hard to say one was obviously a burst bubble and the other not.
So, with significant drawdowns across semis and tech broadly, the Boomer urge for another put is on the rise, but the Fed has kept interest rates high and doesn’t have a strong argument for cutting them. As a result, the Boomer put takes a different shape and results in a call to an Nvidia board meeting and a push for reinstatement of Jensen Huang. It sort of worked, adding $1T to Nvidia’s market cap and pushing the Nasdaq up 6.5%.
Tae Kim illustrated the role Jensen played at Nvidia in an interview with Ben Thompson:
He says he built Nvidia, where he’s like the race car driver of a Formula One car. He literally says that, like, “I built Nvidia in a way where I could drive it, and manage it, and steer it perfectly”. So, without Jensen there, do I see another CEO that’s reading 100 top-five emails a day, that is looking at all the AI discussion email lists inside a company, trading AI papers with his top engineers? I don’t know. I’ve met all the top Nvidia people, they’re all impressive, but they’re not on the Jensen level.
So as unorthodox as it is to step in and pick the managers at individual American companies, it’s all consistent with the Boomer put playbook, the only question is how many more puts can be magically instantiated when the time calls for them.
Microsoft Declares “Code Red”
Microsoft has internally declared “code red” after last week’s outage that affected Outlook, Teams, Exchange Online, and Copilot, per Reuters [ https://substack.com/redirect/d3c97b6f-c6b3-4330-b07a-a138da73f39e?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]. According to the Microsoft team, it was not a cyberattack, but a Windows bug that was found in July and that it was the “most customer-impacting incident in Exchange Online’s history.”
Microsoft also said that during the outage, its engineers exchanged over 11,000 messages across 11 different Teams chats to try to figure out what was going on. One executive described it as “The fog of war.” Under the “code red”, the company plans to solve any future cybersecurity issues within one hour rather than last week’s outage that lasted around 24 hours.
Frontier AI Models May Be Too Powerful to Release
Researchers at OpenAI and Anthropic are getting more worried that unreleased models are becoming too powerful to release safely, according to an Axios report [ https://substack.com/redirect/8a092854-9057-425d-b5cb-212f73fcc50c?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ] that cites more than two dozen sources familiar with the internal debates.
One of the researchers spoke with Axios and explained:
If we continue trying to make models much smarter, such that they’re actually smarter than us... they will in fact be able to trick us successfully… And once you get to that point, many of the schemes that we’ve had at work up until now sort of go out the window.
