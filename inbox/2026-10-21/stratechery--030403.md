Netflix Earnings
----------------

From the Wall Street Journal ( https://www.wsj.com/business/earnings/netflix-revenue-growth-slows-as-it-overhauls-programming-units-d6eac0b5 ) :

Netflix reported its slowest revenue growth in years and said it was working to spur more viewing time as it cuts staff and overhauls its leadership team. The streaming giant reported revenue of $12.9 billion, up 12% from the same period a year earlier. That is the company’s lowest quarterly-revenue percentage growth in three years. Net income was $3.66 billion for the quarter, a roughly 44% increase from the same period last year. Netflix’s stock fell about 6% in after-hours trading.

Over the past week, Netflix has cut its workforce by about 6%, or around 1,000 positions, and parted ways with its content chief and the head of its film unit, an acknowledgment the company’s programming was struggling. Investors have been increasingly worried about Netflix’s ability to grow since its failed bid last year for Warner Bros. Discovery’s film and TV studios and HBO Max. Netflix’s stock is down more than 40% over the past year.

I thought these earnings and some of management’s commentary on the call ( https://s22.q4cdn.com/959853165/files/content_files/Netflix-Inc-_Earnings-Call_2026-10-20_English.pdf ) were very illuminating about where Netflix has ended up, at least for now. The good news is that the subscription business is still working; every time that Netflix was asked about engagement numbers management pointed to the fact that they were able to raise prices without increasing churn. This was Co-CEO Greg Peters’ answer to an explicit engagement question:

*How would you characterize recent engagement trends in the U.S. relative to other geographies? How would you frame any variables such as content, seasonality, competition that might be driving any such divergences in engagement by region?*

*Greg Peters:* I’ll pick up this one and start with just overall engagement, which we think of is the sum total of actual value that we are delivering to members. So this is quality, quantity and variety of viewing. That remains healthy, and it drives our business model. And the best evidence that we’ve got that we’re delivering that value is churn. We’ve got healthy retention in every region. Q3 churn both globally and in the United States improved year-on-year. It was better than forecast. It’s already industry-leading. And the ultimate determinant of are we delivering that value is the reaction to price changes. And those recent price changes that we did, including in the United States have gone well. We’ve seen results that are consistent with past changes and our expectations. And consistent with all that performance, we do plan on delivering our full year guidance.

This answer came up several times, including in the investor letter ( https://s22.q4cdn.com/959853165/files/doc_financials/2026/q3/FINAL-Q3-26-Shareholder-Letter.pdf ) ; it was an obvious talking point to a very specific issue, which was engagement in the United States. This was co-CEO Ted Sarandos’ answer to a question about Netflix changing chief content officers:

Look, our business and our business flywheel are working. There is no need for a fundamental shift in the strategy. We do need better execution in the U.S. Our content around the world is largely working as you heard a couple of examples there from Greg. But we see titles across Latin America, EMEA, APAC, driving engagement, subscriber growth, revenue and profit.

Now the U.S. is also working. It’s just not growing as fast as it could or as fast as we’d like. So we had some really solid doubles and triples this year. But the other territories count on some of those titles being home runs so that they’ll travel around the world. So we really have got to — we’re focusing on that execution. And when we’re talking about like success in APAC, India is a great example, but that success is also happening in Korea and Japan. And when you look to EMEA, we look to the Nordics, which is a very mature market for us and the content slate is killing it there. And it’s proof that when we have the right focus, make the right choices and deliver creatively, we can drive outsized impact on the business around the world.

It was also notable that when Sarandos thanked former content chief Bela Bajaria he only talked about international. The problem, however, is that there is obviously a U.S. content problem, and that problem looms large because U.S. titles are watched internationally more than any others, so it’s an all-of-business problem. I will also note that the content point is one I’ve raised in interviews with Peters; from earlier this year ( https://stratechery.com/2026/an-interview-with-netflix-ceo-greg-peters-about-engagement-and-warner-bros/?access_token=eyJhbGciOiJFUzI1NiIsImtpZCI6IkxzanFRcEhoMUJUdmU0djJQUUVuejIiLCJ0eXAiOiJKV1QifQ.eyJhdWQiOiJzdHJhdGVjaGVyeS5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUiLCJhenAiOiJIS0xjUzREd1Nod1AyWURLYmZQV00xIiwiZW50Ijp7InVyaSI6WyJodHRwczovL3N0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LmNvbS8yMDI2L2FuLWludGVydmlldy13aXRoLW5ldGZsaXgtY2VvLWdyZWctcGV0ZXJzLWFib3V0LWVuZ2FnZW1lbnQtYW5kLXdhcm5lci1icm9zLyJdfSwiZXhwIjoxNzk1MTY5MDQyLCJpYXQiOjE3OTI1NzcwNDIsImlzcyI6Imh0dHBzOi8vYXBwLnBhc3Nwb3J0Lm9ubGluZS9vYXV0aCIsInNjb3BlIjoiZmVlZDpyZWFkIGFydGljbGU6cmVhZCBhc3NldDpyZWFkIGNhdGVnb3J5OnJlYWQgZW50aXRsZW1lbnRzIiwic3ViIjoiMDE5NTE2ZjEtYTM0Ni03NjE0LTllOTYtYjYzYmQxMWZlODU2IiwidXNlIjoiYWNjZXNzIn0.Bc4Yta-Ocfm5l8kphhEJC-U-dwt4KHM91lewx2_mIXnxMiLLTSBNX7fTnU9vGf0AcNM7ey1WBbdasrBbIsSniw ) :

*Isn’t it fair to say though, Netflix’s value has kind of been in the — I don’t want to call it low value engagement, which by the way, I think is actually a tremendously valuable place to be, there is value in bulk and knowing that there’s just a large amount of stuff that will fill a large amount of hours. Even if on the flip side, and we’ll obviously get to this property later, I believe the streaming service with the highest, the opposite metric, the highest dollar they get per viewed is in HBO Max. Which you think about HBO, you think about certain shows, you don’t need to watch it all the time, but you want to get whatever the hot new show is and that’s how their model works.*

*GP:* Yeah. You and I have disagreed a little bit on this.

*I think it was off the record though, so I had to bring it into the discussion!*

*GP:* I would say I think we’re operating quite across a wide spectrum and I definitely believe that we are delivering hours that people love. You think about the Stranger Things finale, those are very valuable hours that I think people were desperate to enjoy. Also, I think we’re doing things in live events. You mentioned live events that are very punctuated moments that I think are very, very high value hours.

So look, there’s no doubt that we want to do more and we want to do more across quite a wide spectrum. I think it’s also worth noting that each individual values their entertainment differently. So what might be low value to you might be high value to somebody else and so part of what we’re trying to do is capture those components.

But HBO is, it’s got great content, so there’s no contesting that, I think that’s one of the reasons that we were excited about doing the deal, for example, as well as doing things internally like producing more Bridgertons and more Stranger Things, and all those things that we also think are high value that come from our own development.

What struck me about this response is that the answer, as has long been the case with Netflix commentary on its content, came back to Stranger Things. That in itself is emblematic of the problem: Stranger Things is by far Netflix’s most well-regarded show, at least as far as widespread acclaim for content quality goes, and it premiered ten years ago! Shouldn’t Netflix have had other examples by now?

Now Netflix does have a lot of content, very high penetration, and an ever-expanding array of live events; it also, as Robert Fishman has noted ( https://stratechery.com/2025/encryption-and-the-uneasy-compromise-netflix-earnings-the-aggregators-compounding-advantage/?access_token=eyJhbGciOiJFUzI1NiIsImtpZCI6IkxzanFRcEhoMUJUdmU0djJQUUVuejIiLCJ0eXAiOiJKV1QifQ.eyJhdWQiOiJzdHJhdGVjaGVyeS5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUiLCJhenAiOiJIS0xjUzREd1Nod1AyWURLYmZQV00xIiwiZW50Ijp7InVyaSI6WyJodHRwczovL3N0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LmNvbS8yMDI1L2VuY3J5cHRpb24tYW5kLXRoZS11bmVhc3ktY29tcHJvbWlzZS1uZXRmbGl4LWVhcm5pbmdzLXRoZS1hZ2dyZWdhdG9ycy1jb21wb3VuZGluZy1hZHZhbnRhZ2UvIl19LCJleHAiOjE3OTUxNjkwNDIsImlhdCI6MTc5MjU3NzA0MiwiaXNzIjoiaHR0cHM6Ly9hcHAucGFzc3BvcnQub25saW5lL29hdXRoIiwic2NvcGUiOiJmZWVkOnJlYWQgYXJ0aWNsZTpyZWFkIGFzc2V0OnJlYWQgY2F0ZWdvcnk6cmVhZCBlbnRpdGxlbWVudHMiLCJzdWIiOiIwMTk1MTZmMS1hMzQ2LTc2MTQtOWU5Ni1iNjNiZDExZmU4NTYiLCJ1c2UiOiJhY2Nlc3MifQ.ibYSNtip1HM_XxgFjfbJ2IRWwcRcuhfj059ACqxAMbUncg7y5Yx1SPyqzQEn8yjiFDhW3JLy0N7qfR0LPQHjlw ) , provides far more hours of entertainment relative to its price than any of its competition. Management is right that churn is the ultimate indicator of customer satisfaction. The problem with pointing to churn, however, is that that is the last line of defense: if churn were up then by definition the content problem would be ruining the business, particularly given Netflix’s entrenchment in people’s viewing lives; what investors are concerned about is indicators that that moment might be approaching.

To that end, what I found concerning on this call were three things. First, there was the fact that management didn’t even try and dispute the engagement questions. Second, Netflix’s advertising growth fell short of its goal. To be fair that goal was ambitious — 100% year-over-year growth — and management said the issue was partner integrations; both may be true, but at the end of the day, advertising is a function of engagement, far more than subscriptions, which is to say that the result that is most directly tied to engagement fell short. Third was Netflix’s plan to improve.

Business or Franchise
---------------------

Netflix’s plan is, in so many words, to do better. Some selected comments from the call:

Turning to the U.S., the other region. Our engagement is solid, and it will allow us to continue to grow as we have for the last several years, but we know that we can do better. And that better is our upside.

Going back to that great list you just heard from Greg, there is a lot to be excited about. But I know that we could do better, and we want to build on that momentum.

Our business and our business flywheel are working. There is no need for a fundamental shift in the strategy. We do need better execution in the U.S. our content around the world is largely working as you heard a couple of examples there from Greg. But we see titles across Latin America, EMEA, APAC, driving engagement, subscriber growth, revenue and profit. The U.S. is also working. It’s just not growing as fast as it could or as fast as we’d like. So we had some really solid doubles and triples this year. But the other territories count on some of those titles being home runs so that they’ll travel around the world. So we really have got to — we’re focusing on that execution.

Those are three separate comments, to be clear, but there were others: Netflix needs to do better. The point in highlighting these comments, however, is not to question whether or not Netflix can do better. Rather, it reminds me of a Warren Buffett letter from 1991 ( http://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1991.html ) that I have cited in the past, where he explains the difference between businesses and franchises.

The fact is that newspaper, television, and magazine properties have begun to resemble businesses more than franchises in their economic behavior…An economic franchise arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation. The existence of all three conditions will be demonstrated by a company’s ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital. Moreover, franchises can tolerate mis-management. Inept managers may diminish a franchise’s profitability, but they cannot inflict mortal damage.

In contrast, “a business” earns exceptional profits only if it is the low-cost operator or if supply of its product or service is tight. Tightness in supply usually does not last long. With superior management, a company may maintain its status as a low-cost operator for a much longer time, but even then unceasingly faces the possibility of competitive attack. And a business, unlike a franchise, can be killed by poor management.

Until recently, media properties possessed the three characteristics of a franchise and consequently could both price aggressively and be managed loosely. Now, however, consumers looking for information and entertainment (their primary interest being the latter) enjoy greatly broadened choices as to where to find them. Unfortunately, demand can’t expand in response to this new supply: 500 million American eyeballs and a 24-hour day are all that’s available. The result is that competition has intensified, markets have fragmented, and the media industry has lost some — though far from all — of its franchise strength.

I am certainly not going to sit here and say that Netflix is poorly managed, but I am hardly the only person to have raised questions about the quality of content and the lack of breakout hits. What seemed true for a very long time, however, is that it didn’t matter: Netflix was a franchise, and seemed destined to Aggregate all of entertainment. To me that last part was crucial: I thought of Netflix originals as a bridge to a future where the other Hollywood studios came to their senses and sold to Netflix; in that world Netflix didn’t have to be the best at content, because it would get all of it.

In fact, however, competition is as strong as ever, and while Netflix is still growing, it doesn’t feel like the all-conquering force it seemed destined to be. That doesn’t mean it’s a bad business, it just means it is in fact a business, not a franchise, at least when it comes to growth. So yes, the company needs to do better. That that is a requirement is itself the bearish signal.

Disney’s Deal
-------------

All that noted, Netflix is still in a position of strength, because it has genuine distribution advantages. Go back to the beginning of this month and this item from Variety ( https://variety.com/2026/tv/news/disney-licenses-titles-netflix-ice-age-movies-will-trent-percy-jackson-and-the-olympians-felicity-1236897256/ ) :

Sure, Disney and Netflix compete for streaming subscribers — but the Mouse House also sees the rival’s massive customer base of over 325 million as a way to monetize and promote its own content, and Netflix is happy to write the checks. Disney Entertainment and Netflix have entered into an expansive new content licensing agreement, which will bring an eclectic mix of library titles and current-season TV shows to Netflix for varying periods of times.

In some cases, part of Disney’s strategy is to drum up interest in upcoming releases. Disney+ original series “Percy Jackson and the Olympians” Seasons 1-2 will be available on Netflix globally beginning Oct. 4 as part of a three-month promotional window in advance of its Season 3 premiere on Disney+ on Nov. 20. Similarly, all five of 20th Century Studios’ “Ice Age” films will be available on Netflix worldwide beginning Oct. 4 in a separate three-month promotional window ahead of the franchise’s sixth installment, “Ice Age: Boiling Point,” releasing in theaters on Feb. 5, 2027. The movies are “Ice Age” (2002), “Ice Age: The Meltdown” (2006), “Ice Age: Dawn of the Dinosaurs” (2009), “Ice Age: Continental Drift” (2012) and “Ice Age: Collision Course” (2016).

Sarandos highlighted the Ice Age angle in the call:

You mentioned the Disney deal we just launched in October. Now that deal brought all of the Ice Age films to Netflix, along with a bunch of other Disney films or Disney series and some upcoming films. And to your point, it is a win-win deal. And the reason it’s a win-win deal is that we benefit by expanding the entertainment offering for sure. Disney benefits from the revenue and from putting that content in front of our large global member base. Now our members benefit, of course, because they get to the addition of Disney content. And we are already seeing some of those benefits from the Ice Age movies. All five of them hit our top 10. That’s really exciting. Now this benefits Disney because as they gear up to release their next Ice Age moving in the theaters in February, it’s called Ice Age: The Boiling Point. More people would be recent fans of the Ice Age franchise. And by the way, yes, Netflix just plugged a Disney movie on our earnings call. You’re welcome, Josh.

This gives real credence to my long-run optimism about Netflix’s position in the value chain: when money is actually on the line — specifically, an upcoming theatrical release — even Disney realizes that its optimal strategy is to build an audience on Netflix, not its own service. In other words, for all of the concerns I might have about Netflix, much larger questions should be raised about why exactly everyone else in Hollywood is clearly under-monetizing their content by insisting on their own streaming services!

Alas, that is the nature of Hollywood, and that nature means that Netflix’s nature is a bit less of a sure thing than it might be in any other Town.
