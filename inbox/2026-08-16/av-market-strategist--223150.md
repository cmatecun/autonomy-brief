WeRide Q2/2026 Earnings and Pony AI’s 2,000 vehicle European expansion
WeRide talked about its overseas business model on Wednesday, and it is precisely what we described in our WeRide deep dive [ https://substack.com/redirect/c33739a9-eb63-4ebf-b7cf-893a5903c0a2?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]: license the virtual driver, hold the permit, and rent everything else. The model is working and the accounts have started to show it, just not on the line most people looked at. 
The quarter in numbers
Revenue reached RMB 231.7 million ($34.2 million), up 82% year on year and 103% quarter on quarter, against an analyst estimate of RMB 170.7 million. For the first half, revenue was $51.0 million, up 73%.
Gross margin came in at 37.5%, up 9.4 percentage points from 28.1% a year ago, and a record for the company. The mix is doing the work: high-margin asset-light overseas revenue, fast growth in ADAS, and a general shift toward service revenue rather than hardware.
The business is now reported in three segments, which is new and useful: L4, L2+/L3 ADAS, and AI infrastructure. L4 revenue was RMB 125 million ($18.5 million), up 47% year on year and 131% quarter on quarter, driven by robotaxi. The L2+/L3 line grew roughly 26 times year on year and 219% quarter on quarter.
Overseas is now close to 40% of group revenue, up 164% year on year and roughly 170% sequentially. That answers the first of the three questions we said in CW33 we would be watching on this print.
The disclosure that matters
For the first time, WeRide put numbers and structure on the overseas model.
The company does not own the vehicles. Local partners buy them, depreciate them and run them. WeRide handles localisation to national traffic law and road conditions, carries the regulatory compliance work, and licenses the virtual driver to the operator. It earns a recurring technology service fee plus mileage-based fees.
Founder and CEO Tony Han was explicit that this makes the phrase “unit economics” mean something different here than it does for Uber or Waymo. It is not fleet ownership plus a ride-hailing platform with revenue recognised at the gross fare. It is a licensing measure.
The number attached to it: based on optimal utilisation under normalised driverless operations, WeRide estimates steady-state annualised technology service revenue could exceed $50,000 per robotaxi. CFO Jennifer Li framed it slightly differently on the same call, as $40,000 to $50,000 with upside as density builds in each city.
Two things to hold about this figure. It is an estimate under normalised driverless operations, not a realised result. And it is the number two of our deep dives have been reconstructing from the outside, so having the company’s own version of it is genuinely valuable even in estimate form.
What it takes to get there
Here is the arithmetic that makes the model legible, and I want to be clear that this is a description of the ramp rather than a criticism of it.
WeRide’s overseas robotaxi fleet roughly doubled during the quarter, from about 200 to about 400 vehicles. At $50,000 each at steady state, that fleet represents roughly $20 million of annualised technology service revenue. Quarterly R&D was RMB 434 million, about $64 million, which annualises to something near $258 million at the current run rate.
So covering one year of research at today’s spending would take on the order of 5,000 overseas robotaxis earning at the company’s own steady-state estimate. WeRide has about 400 there now, targets 2,600 robotaxis globally by year end, and has stated a longer-term ambition of tens of thousands by 2030.
That is not a gotcha. It is what a company means when it guides to positive cash flow in a single quarter by 2028 and full-year break-even in 2029. Those dates are a statement about fleet density and ADAS volume, and now we can see roughly how much of each they require.
The rest of the accounts
Because the growth numbers alone would give a misleading picture.
Net loss was RMB 400.7 million ($59.1 million), narrowing 1% year on year, against roughly RMB 300 million expected. EBITDA loss narrowed 8.1% to RMB 335.4 million ($49.4 million). Gross profit rose 143% to RMB 86.9 million ($12.8 million), which does not cover the R&D line on its own. Total operating expenses were RMB 533 million ($78.5 million), up only 9.2% against 82% revenue growth, which is the clearest evidence in the print that operating leverage is starting to get real. R&D specifically grew 36%. Cash and liquid resources stood at RMB 5.4 billion ($795.6 million) at the end of June, down from RMB 6.97 billion at the end of 2025.
The market took the loss rather than the revenue. Shares fell as much as 9.9% in Hong Kong on 13 August before stabilising around 6% down. Citi’s Jeff Chung attributed the miss to core service margins outside AI infrastructure and L3, under seasonal pressure and the cost of investing in domestic robotaxi deployment.
Our standing position from CW33 was that the ambition is not in doubt but the unit economics are. After this print I would narrow it rather than repeat it. The cost side is no longer the open question: operating expenses grew 9.2% against 82% revenue growth, and the overseas structure is now disclosed with a number attached to it. What remains unproven is whether that number survives at ten times this fleet size. The doubt is smaller than it was in CW33, and a lot more specific.
One question from CW33 remains open. The roughly 50% gross margin WeRide has previously attributed to its Middle East business was not separately restated this quarter. Group margin at 37.5% is a record and is not the same disclosure.
Middle East, and a fleet that doubled through a difficult quarter
Fully driverless operations in Abu Dhabi and Dubai now cover more than 70% of the core urban areas. In Riyadh, the operating zone has been extended to the airport terminals and the surrounding central business districts, which is the first time an airport has appeared in WeRide’s Saudi scope.
And the overseas fleet doubled from roughly 200 to roughly 400 vehicles in the quarter, which Li pointed out happened despite the geopolitical tension in the region. WeRide has a plan to deploy 1,200 robotaxis across MENA with Uber through 2027.
Han’s framing of the phase change: this “marks that our overseas expansion is now entering a phase of large-scale commercialization.” Three new robotaxi partnerships were announced in the quarter, in Madrid, Zurich and Copenhagen.
Permits as the moat, said out loud
The most quotable exchange came from Walter Piecyk of LightShed, who asked the question this newsletter has been circling since CW28. Under an asset-light model, partners own the vehicles and the platform owns the riders. WeRide sits in the middle licensing the driver. So what is the leverage if Uber decides to route demand elsewhere?
Li’s answer: “Everyone can be on the platform, but not everyone can get the regulatory permit.”
She backed it with structure. Official autonomous driving licences in eight countries, operations in 12, and a stated view that the responsibility for obtaining permits sits with WeRide and is the durable part of the relationship. Han put a number on the lead: a combination of technology localisation, ecosystem integration and safety validation that he believes puts the company two to three years ahead, with regulatory approval in one jurisdiction accelerating approval in the next as frameworks converge.
I find this more interesting than a company defending its position, because we reached the same conclusion from a completely different vertical. In CW31 we found that in Chinese autonomous delivery, where no national certification system exists and every city is a separate application, accumulated permits protect the incumbents more than their technology does. 
China, ADAS, and the double flywheel
Domestic operations improved on every metric that matters for utilisation. The Guangzhou driverless zone expanded into key parts of Tianhe District including Pearl River New Town, and Haizhu District including the Canton Fair Complex, close to a threefold expansion of the operating domain. Average daily rides per vehicle reached 21, up 24% sequentially, with a peak of 28. Registered users in China grew about 35% quarter on quarter, and ride-hailing revenue rose 140% sequentially.
The global L4 fleet reached about 3,400 vehicles as of 31 July, up 22% since April, of which more than 1,800 are robotaxis after adding 500 in the period, a 40% increase.
The ADAS business is the part I would watch hardest. Roughly 30,000 units of the WRD 3.0 one-stage end-to-end L2++/L3 solution were delivered in the quarter, with production design wins now secured across more than 30 vehicle models. Guidance is more than 100,000 vehicles installed by year end and 500,000 cumulative next year. WeRide also launched an L3 proof-of-concept programme with Mercedes-Benz, and has now won six consecutive Chinese intelligent driving competitions.
Han credits two models for doing this with a team of 200 to 300 people. GENESIS, the world model that reconstructs long-tail scenarios in simulation, and the newly launched WITT (World Intelligence Toward Truth, named for Wittgenstein), which dissects road-test video into what he calls minimal fact units and establishes causal relationships between them. 
Source: WeRide
The claim he attached: WeRide runs a 200 TOPS domain controller where competitors use 2,000, and wins anyway, because distillation from the foundation models lets it target the compute at what matters.
And a day later, Reuters
The day after the call, Han told Reuters that WeRide is in conversation with regulators in Australia and considering South Korea, Japan and Southeast Asia, alongside continuing European talks. Right-hand-drive commercial deployments are set for Singapore and Hong Kong. The company now spans more than 60 cities in 13 countries, targets 5,000 L4 vehicles by year end including 2,600 robotaxis, and beyond the Geely Farizon purpose-built robotaxi is co-developing another model with GAC aimed at further hardware cost reduction. On capital: “Currently we don’t have a very immediate need for capital raising,” with the option left open on good terms.
That list is consistent with what the call described. Australia, Japan and South Korea are high-labour-cost markets with real driver shortages and functioning regulators, which is the same profile that made the Gulf work.
 But WeRide was not the only Chinese player in the news.
Pony.ai: 100 million kilometres, and 2,000 vehicles for Europe
On 10 August, Pony.ai announced its global autonomous testing and operational mileage had passed 100 million kilometres. On the same day it started mass production of its fourth-generation L4 heavy truck, rolling off the line at SANY Heavy Truck, aimed at highway trunk routes, bulk commodity corridors and ports, with deliveries over the coming months. The first-generation L4 light truck has entered intensive road testing for express, retail and cold-chain logistics.
The bigger news came on Friday. Pony.ai and Uber expanded their partnership to deploy more than 2,000 robotaxis across Europe, growing from the existing Zagreb service, which launched with Croatian operator Verne and is coming to the Uber platform, into four additional European cities. The expansion also includes plans for the Middle East. Neither company named the cities or gave a timeframe.
The structure is the joint deployment model we explained in the Pony.ai deep dive [ https://substack.com/redirect/c7f1e04a-76af-498a-9083-f08e23a81e07?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ], and the press release states it more plainly than before. Three functions are required to run robotaxi at scale: L4 technology, a mobility platform, and day-to-day fleet operations. Partners can hold more than one role, and crucially, vehicle funding and ownership can sit with different partners depending on the market. Pony supplies the driver, rider experience and operational expertise. Uber supplies booking, payment, customer service and its human-driver network. Local fleet partners run the depots.
Pony reports shortly. I will go through that call in the same detail and share the takeaways.
What this means and what to watch
The Chinese export machine did not slow down this quarter, and the cadence is now the point. WeRide announced robotaxi partnerships in Madrid, Zurich and Copenhagen, extended Riyadh to the airport terminals, doubled its overseas fleet, and a day after the earnings call named Australia, South Korea, Japan and Southeast Asia as what comes next. Pony committed to more than 2,000 robotaxis across Europe with Uber and put a fourth-generation L4 truck into mass production. 
What we usually do not get alongside those announcements is the economics underneath them. This week we did, and it was a good quarter. Overseas is now roughly 40% of WeRide’s revenue and growing faster than the group. The fleet keeps compounding. Gross margin set a record while volume doubled. And for the first time there is a published rate for what a single robotaxi earns the company licensing it: $50,000 a year.
So the question has moved. It is no longer whether the export push is real. It is what it earns, how fast it can grow, and what could take it away.
Start with what the $50,000 changes, because that matters more than the number itself. The overseas business is not a fleet business. It is a licensing business with a published fee rate, and the two are valued on completely different terms. A fleet operator is worth its vehicles, its utilisation and its cost per mile. A licensor is worth the number of vehicles running its software and the rate it charges them, and it carries almost none of the capital. That is a more attractive shape of business, and it is also a more fragile one, because the licensor does not control the deployment pace.
Which is why city density is the thing to watch. Both Han and Li said the same thing in different words: the fee rate improves as density builds within a city. So the disclosure to look for next quarter is not another headline fleet number. It is how many vehicles WeRide has running in Abu Dhabi, Dubai and Riyadh individually, and whether those individual city counts are rising faster than the number of cities on the map. 
On how fast this can grow, ADAS is the swing factor, and the reason is the gate rather than the capital. 30,000 units this quarter, guidance of 100,000 installed by year end and 500,000 cumulative next year. Neither business is capital-intensive for WeRide, which is the whole point of the model. What separates them is what limits their growth. Robotaxi scales city by city, and every city needs a permit, a localisation cycle, safety validation and a local operating partner, which is exactly the moat Han described and exactly why it cannot be rushed. ADAS scales model by model, through OEM production programmes, where one design win puts the software into a full model run without a new approval in every city. 
For Pony in Europe, the tell will be the city names. Two thousand vehicles with no cities and no timeframe is an ambition, and it deserves the same reading we give Uber's bench in the second half of this issue. The four cities, their regulators and their local fleet partners are the disclosure that turns it into a plan. 
As for what could take this away, the permit moat is real, and I would put a date on it rather than a duration. Han says two to three years. I would say it lasts until portable standards arrive. Watch European type approval and the UNECE work at least as closely as you watch fleet counts, because that is the mechanism that converts a two-year lead into a two-quarter one.
Which leaves the same question sitting under both companies. Both are licensing a virtual driver into someone else’s fleet, on someone else’s platform, under a permit they hold themselves. That permit is the only part of the arrangement they own outright. It is a genuine asset. It is also the one asset a regulator can standardise away.

Waymo Gets 18 Counties. Uber's Bench Gets Asterisks.
The fatal Dallas accident 
For Waymo, the week started with a tragedy.
On the evening of 9 August, a Waymo Jaguar I-Pace was travelling north on Maple Avenue in Dallas with nobody inside it. A pedestrian had left a convenience store with a companion and stepped into the roadway away from a crosswalk. The Waymo detected him roughly 150 feet ahead as he entered the road on the opposite side, anticipated that he might cross into its lane, and began slowing from about 24 mph.
Before he reached that lane, he was struck from behind by an SUV travelling roughly 35 mph in a 30 mph zone. The impact threw him about 30 feet into the Waymo’s path. By that point the robotaxi had slowed to about 5 mph. He struck its left rear side. The vehicle stopped immediately, the SUV driver remained at the scene, and the pedestrian died later in hospital. Dallas police have said nobody was at fault.
Brad Templeton’s analysis is the best thing written on this, and it is worth reading in full. Two takeaways carry. First, if a 35 mph impact threw a person 30 feet, a side contact with a vehicle moving at 5 mph is very unlikely to have caused the fatal injuries. Second, his conclusion after working through the physics: there is not much, if anything, the Waymo could reliably have done differently, unless it could have predicted the SUV impact well in advance, at night, from behind.
The comparison everyone reached for is Cruise, and Templeton is careful about where the parallel ends. In San Francisco in 2023 a pedestrian was also thrown into a robotaxi’s path by another vehicle. Cruise was not at fault for that either. What ended Cruise was the vehicle settling on top of her, then dragging her, and then a communications process with regulators that omitted the dragging. The company lost its permit and eventually itself.
This is Waymo’s third involved fatality. In Dallas the company published its speed, its detection distance, when it began braking and where the contact occurred, within a day. I am not going to treat disclosure as a moral achievement. But it is the variable that separated Cruise’s outcome from its crash, and it is the thing worth watching in the weeks ahead.
The rest of the week
Three announcements came off Uber’s partner bench this week, and in all three the company disclosed the fact that undercuts its own headline. Uber’s Tokyo robotaxi carries safety operators, named in the last paragraph of the release before the executive quotes. Zoox described its Las Vegas fares as competitive with the comfort tier, and then published a rate card 50 to 100% above it. AVride announced 100,000 autonomous rides, every one of them supervised. Nothing was hidden. It was placed. Waymo spent the same week taking the driver out in Denver, publishing hard numbers within a day of a fatal crash it did not cause, and earning the right to charge across 18 counties.
Waymo: 18 counties, seven months, one filing
The California Public Utilities Commission approved Waymo’s Advice Letter No. 4 on Friday. It clears the company to charge for fully driverless rides across the entire Bay Area and Los Angeles, and to open two markets it has never operated in commercially: Sacramento and San Diego.
The geography is 18 counties. Twelve in the north (Alameda, Contra Costa, Marin, Napa, Sacramento, San Francisco, San Mateo, Santa Clara, Santa Cruz, Solano, Sonoma, Yolo) and six in the south (Los Angeles, Orange, Riverside, San Bernardino, San Diego, Ventura). That is most of the population of the largest state economy in the United States.
Source: Waymo
The operating conditions Waymo asked for and received are as broad as the map: all speed limits, freeways, highways, city streets, rural roads, parking lots, driveways and rail crossings, day and night, in rain, fog and hail. Both the Jaguar I-Pace and the new Ojai on the sixth-generation Driver are covered. There is one meaningful carve-out, and it is worth holding onto for two paragraphs: widespread snow or ice.
The process is the part most coverage will skip. Waymo filed on 28 January. Regulators suspended the letter through 25 September for further review. Waymo filed a supplemental letter in May covering unaccompanied minors and rider procedures during service disruptions, prompted by the PG&E outage that hit San Francisco last December. Seven months of filing, suspension, protest and supplement, for one advice letter.
Now the qualifier that belongs in the same breath as the headline, and Waymo supplied it itself: the rollout “will be gradual and guided by our safety framework.” Approval is not deployment. An advice letter does not build a depot, install charging, map a city, or staff remote assistance. Sacramento and San Diego are currently authorised, not served. 
Waymo’s Phoenix expansion, six weeks after the Uber divorce
Waymo added 55 square miles of the East Valley to its Phoenix service area on Thursday, covering Gilbert and more of Chandler. 
Source: Waymo
Until now its cars did not go east of State Route 87. Service to Mesa Gateway Airport is coming, and the new territory is reachable by freeway. Total coverage in the Valley is now more than 350 square miles.
As we reported in CW28, Phoenix was the only market where Waymo and Uber operated together, and it was the first place the relationship ended. Our position then was that Uber’s value to an AV developer peaks on day one and decays with every ride, and that Phoenix was where the curve visibly hit zero. Six weeks later, Waymo expanded its footprint in that exact market by 19%.
The airport point is the one to keep. Once Sky Harbor, Scottsdale Airpark and Mesa Gateway are all inside the service area, Phoenix will have more airport access from Waymo than any city in the world. Airport legs are the longest and densest revenue runs in any ride-hail market.
Denver, and the snow problem
Waymo has removed the backup drivers in Denver. Until recently employees were driving the cars around the metro while the system collected data. Now the cars run without a human in the seat, restricted to company employees, with public rides expected later this year.
Denver is the only Waymo test city that regularly sees snow and ice.
That is the same condition the CPUC filing carved out. So in the same week the company won permission to operate almost everywhere in California except in widespread snow and ice, it took the driver out of the one city where it is trying to solve exactly that. The company has said it hopes to expand into the wider metro area and to Denver International Airport.
And a parking space at Allegiant Stadium
Waymo also announced that it is now the official autonomous ride-hailing partner of the Las Vegas Raiders and Allegiant Stadium, the first partnership between an NFL team and an AV company. One word in the release is the story: riders will have access to a dedicated Waymo pickup and drop-off area.
Allegiant Stadium is private property. So is the Sphere, where Zoox has the equivalent relationship. So is every casino in Las Vegas, each of which requires its own agreement before a vehicle can pick up on its land. In EP22, Bern Grush made the case that the curb, not the road, is the binding constraint on robotaxi scale, and that pickup and drop-off orchestration is the layer nobody has built. This is what that looks like when it becomes commercial: a scarce, exclusive, privately granted access right, allocated one venue at a time.
Road permits are non-exclusive by design. A stadium apron is not. This could become a real competitive front faster than most people expect, and Las Vegas is where it will be visible first, because Las Vegas is about to get a lot of autonomous operators.
Uber and Serve: the exit the counterparty read in a filing
Uber just recently had its first divorce with Waymo in Phoenix, and now the second divorce is already here.
Uber sold its entire remaining stake in Serve Robotics. The disclosure came in a regulatory filing, first reported by Bloomberg. Serve learned about it when it was published.
The history matters for the size of this. Serve began as Postmates X, the robotics arm of the delivery company Uber bought in 2020 for $2.65 billion. It was spun out as an independent company in 2021, partnered with Uber in 2022, and expanded that partnership in May 2023 to put up to 2,000 sidewalk robots on Uber’s app. Uber did not just invest in Serve. Uber helped create it.
On the 6 August earnings call, before the share sale was disclosed, CEO Ali Kashani laid out the operating picture. From Q1 2022 through Q1 2026, delivery volume through Uber grew for 17 consecutive quarters. In Q2 it reversed for the first time, which he attributed to lower than expected robot utilization. He described “differing views” with Uber on the operating model, specifically fleet coordination and merchant integration, and said Serve did not expect to renew when the agreement expires in early 2027. Over the same period, volume with its other food delivery partner grew nearly 50% in a single quarter. Per Autonomy Signals, full-year revenue guidance came down from $26 million to $10 million, against an operating burn of roughly $64 million a quarter and a cash position around $240 million.
There are two accounts of why this happened and I do not think we can pick between them from the outside. Uber’s is capital allocation: low-margin sidewalk logistics with hardware-heavy fleet operations, curb-space regulatory exposure and small basket sizes, against $10 billion of robotaxi commitments that need funding. Serve’s is demand: the orders Uber Eats was supposed to route did not arrive at the volume the fleet was sized for. Both can be true at once, and the relationship reportedly deteriorated over several quarters rather than in one.
What can be said without adjudicating is the sequence. Uber exited the equity and let the commercial agreement lapse in the same window, and the counterparty found out about the first part from a public filing. Serve is one of more than 30 AV companies Uber has partnered with or invested in.
In CW28 we flagged multi-partner allocation, the question of who decides which partner gets which order, as the next disclosure fight. That fight has now produced its first casualty, and it came from the delivery side rather than the robotaxi side.
Tokyo: four companies, one service, and a word in the last paragraph
Uber Japan signed an operational partnership with Hinomaru Kotsu to run day-to-day fleet operations for an autonomous vehicle pilot in Tokyo, launching late 2026. Hinomaru Kotsu handles depot operations, cleaning, maintenance, inspections, charging and vehicle uptime. Uber makes the rides available through its app.
The vehicles are Nissan LEAFs running Wayve’s AI Driver, under the memorandum of understanding Uber, Wayve and Nissan signed on 12 March. Per Autonomy Markets, the vehicles will launch with lidar at Nissan’s requirement, which is a small but real signal about what an OEM will accept on its own nameplate.
The structural point is in the release: under Japanese law, passenger transport services must be operated by authorised taxi companies. Uber cannot run this service. It has to rent an operator, and it rented one with a 76-year history. Waymo faces the identical constraint in the same city and solved it the same way, with Nihon Kotsu. Two competitors, one regulatory regime, one shape of answer.
And in the last body paragraph, before the executive quotes: “experienced Hinomaru Kotsu drivers will be on board as safety operators to monitor behind the wheel,” with fully driverless operation coming later, subject to regulatory approval. 
In CW33 we read out Uber’s pipeline of seven cities today and 15 by year end, and noted that four of the six near-term partner names are unproven at driver-out commercial scale anywhere in the world. Tokyo belongs in that column, and it is a good deal for Uber regardless. It is just not exactly the thing the headline says it is.
Zoox and the fare card 
Zoox began charging for rides in Las Vegas on 10 August, becoming the third company in America to take money for a driverless ride and the only one to do it in a vehicle with no steering wheel. In CW33 we covered the launch structure and the destination fees, and flagged a restriction in its federal filing capping the vehicle at 45 mph that Rob Grant found.
What is new this week is the arithmetic, and the LightShed’s note [ https://substack.com/redirect/bb9f0738-b4d5-4710-b7f8-140df25ddbf5?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ] of Walter Piecyk did it properly.
Zoox set the benchmark itself, describing its fares as competitive with the comfort tier, which typically runs 20 to 40% above a standard ride. The published rate card is $12 base, $1.50 per mile and $0.40 per minute, plus resort pickup fees, airport fees, up to $40 at some event venues, and a 3% transportation tax. 
Source: X/@samsnow850
Uber’s Las Vegas UberX rates run roughly $1.55 base, $1.28 per mile and $0.25 per minute.
The per-mile and per-minute numbers are close enough. The base fare is eight times higher. Run a typical 5-mile, 15-minute ride and Zoox comes out around $26, against roughly $14 on UberX and $17 to $18 on Comfort. 
An 8x base fare loads the entire premium onto short trips. Short trips are the only product Zoox can currently sell, on 105 vehicles spread across multiple markets, with pickups limited to 15 fixed stops, service hours from 11am to 1am, and wait times running 15 to 30 minutes. So on the rides it can actually complete, Zoox is priced 50 to 100% above the tier it named.
Walt also pointed out that price is not yet a competitive weapon in Las Vegas because nobody is competing. Zoox has no scaled rival there today, tourists will pay for a novelty, and a purpose-built cabin with no steering wheel is a genuine attraction. That changes when Motional brings its 100-plus FMVSS-certified Ioniq 5s to driver-out, which it is targeting by CES, and it changes again when Waymo arrives with freeway capability and airport access.
There is one more detail worth marking. Zoox launched on its own app, with Uber to follow. In Phoenix, when Waymo ran on both its own app and Uber’s, demand flowed almost entirely to Waymo and the Uber channel never scaled past ten cars. Zoox management has been open for years about wanting to stand alone.
A footnote on Avride
AVride announced it has passed 100,000 robotaxi rides on the Uber platform. Caveat: every one of them was supervised.
Source: X/AVride
What this means and what to watch
Waymo’s constraint has moved from software to build-out, and that is the number to track. The CPUC decision authorises; depots, charging, mapping and remote assistance determine the pace. Watch how quickly physical infrastructure appears in Sacramento and San Diego, not how quickly the approval is reported. 
Denver is the harder test than California, and it will be settled sooner. The CPUC’s only real exclusion is widespread snow and ice, and Waymo has just removed the driver in its only snow city. If Denver opens to the public driver-out this winter, the snow carve-out becomes a timing question rather than a boundary, and Waymo’s addressable map gets materially larger than the 18 counties suggest.
On Uber’s bench, watch placement rather than announcements. The signal is not that Tokyo launches supervised, which is probably the responsible way to launch. The signal is where the word sits in the release. When “supervised” moves from the last paragraph to the headline, or disappears because it no longer applies, that is the disclosure worth acting on.
And the allocation question is now live in a city, not in theory. Las Vegas will shortly have Zoox and Motional available through Uber, plus Waymo and Tesla inbound. Someone has to decide which partner gets which ride. Nobody has disclosed how that decision is made, and after Serve, every partner on that bench has a reason to ask.
