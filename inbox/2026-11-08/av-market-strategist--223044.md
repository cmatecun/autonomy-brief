# Uber’s Biggest Robotaxi Partner Wants Its Riders Back

Uber & Lyft reported Q3/2026 Earnings
Uber still wants every AV partner. It just stopped wanting to pay for all of them. And the partner that matters most for its numbers has now said, in public, that it wants the customer for itself.
Uber’s quarter in two sentences
Uber’s Q3 was strong overall. Gross bookings grew 20% to a record $59.8 billion, trips grew 16%, non-GAAP EPS rose 35% to $0.88 and trailing twelve-month free cash flow reached a record $10.4 billion. 
Source: Uber Q3/2026 Supplemental Data
The soft spot was Mobility, which came in 1 to 2 percentage points below Uber’s own expectations because driver supply in several international markets (Brazil, India, Canada) did not keep up with demand.
As always, autonomous driving was a topic on the call, just not as dominant as last quarter. In Q2, 75% of analyst questions had autonomy as a central topic, as we reported in CW33. This quarter it was three of nine: one on financing, one on the rollout and one on unit economics. That is about 33%.
700 AVs this year, 2,000 next year
Here is what Uber laid out for autonomous vehicles:
~700 fully driverless vehicles on Uber by the end of 2026
More than 2,000 by the end of 2027, roughly half in the US and half internationally
“Tens of thousands” from 2028, which Uber calls the start of a much larger commercial ramp
Source: Uber Q3/2026 Prepared Remarks
To put those numbers next to the market leader: as we reported in CW44, Waymo is aiming for about 8,000 vehicles by the end of this year and about 20,000 by the end of 2027, according to Semafor’s anonymous sources (Waymo declined to comment). If those targets hold, Waymo alone would run roughly 11 times Uber’s entire driverless network at the end of 2026, and about 10 times at the end of 2027.
That is not a knock on Uber. Uber does not build the cars or the driver, and 700 driverless vehicles across many partners and countries is real progress from a standing start. But it frames what “scale” means on each side. Uber’s whole partner network, combined, is a fraction of one developer.
How much of the 700 is Waymo?
This is the part I find most interesting, and it needs a little detective work.
Uber does not say how many of the 700 belong to which partner. But we have two useful data points.
First, in Austin and Atlanta, Waymo runs exclusively on Uber. Uber handles the fleet side there through Avomo, its fleet management partner, which cleans, charges, maintains and runs the depots for those cars. On its website, Avomo says it currently manages the operations of more than 400 autonomous vehicles across the two cities: around 250 in Austin and around 150 in Atlanta.
Source: Avomo
Second, Uber says it expects nearly 200 fully driverless cars in the UAE alone by year end.
Put those together and roughly 600 of the ~700 sit in two places: Waymo’s cars in Austin and Atlanta, and the UAE with WeRide and Baidu. Everything else, from Zoox to Motional, would share the remaining hundred or so.
Two honest caveats. I don’t know when Avomo last updated that figure, and Waymo’s fleets in both cities may grow before December. And “managing operations” is not the same as a precise count of cars available on Uber on a given day. So treat it as an order of magnitude.
But the order of magnitude is the point. More than half of Uber’s year-end driverless count is likely Waymo, the one partner that has already said it will run its own app in those same two cities from January 2028. That date lands one month after Uber’s 2,000 target.
It also tells us where the growth to 2,000 has to come from. If Waymo’s footprint on Uber stays roughly where it is, the other partners need to add something like 1,300 vehicles in 2027. As we noted in CW33, most of the near-term names on Uber’s list are still unproven at driverless commercial scale. So the 2027 number is really a bet on the middle of the market.
Las Vegas: two driverless partners and a new button
The next launches, according to Uber:
Zoox goes live on Uber in Las Vegas later this month
Motional moves to fully driverless operations there “soon thereafter”
That makes Las Vegas the first US city where riders can access two fully driverless partners through the Uber app. (Dubai already has two on Uber, Baidu’s Apollo Go and WeRide, so the claim holds for the US only.) Uber says it will start with limited fleets and add supply over the coming months.
And then there is a sentence I think matters more than the launches themselves. Uber plans “to introduce a dedicated autonomous option in our app as availability and reliability improve.”
That addresses one of the most common complaints about AVs on Uber. For a long time, riders in Austin and Atlanta have said that getting a Waymo through Uber felt like playing the lottery. You open the app, request an UberX, and maybe you get a robotaxi, maybe you don’t. If you specifically wanted a Waymo, there was no way to ask for one.
A dedicated option is not new everywhere. Uber launched an “Autonomous” category in Abu Dhabi in September 2025 and offers it in Dubai as well. So this is an import from the Middle East, not an invention.
Waymo explains why it wants its riders back
This week also brought the clearest statement yet from Waymo on why it is changing the Uber relationship.
As we first reported in CW31, Waymo will end its exclusivity with Uber in Austin and Atlanta in January 2028 and start running its own app alongside, with the underlying contract ending in May 2028. What is new is that Waymo’s co-CEO Tekedra Mawakana confirmed it herself in an interview with Bloomberg, and gave the reason.
Waymo partnered with Uber because Uber offered scale, she said. But the rider experience is “a little bit of a crapshoot,” because riders don’t know whether they will get a robotaxi or a human driver. And then the line I think explains the whole decision: “It’s important for us to have that relationship with the rider.”
Uber’s response was interesting. It told TechCrunch it still plans to host Waymo robotaxis on its app “for as long as the two companies agree.” And it told Bloomberg that it was Waymo, not Uber, that chose not to add a way to book a robotaxi directly in the Uber app.
So the inconsistent experience Waymo now cites as a reason to go its own way is, according to Uber, the result of a decision Waymo made. And the fix Uber is now announcing for Las Vegas is exactly the option Waymo reportedly didn’t want in Austin and Atlanta.
There are two ways to read this, and both sides come out of it looking self-interested:
Read from Waymo’s side: a dedicated Waymo tier on Uber would have made the Uber app a better place to get a Waymo, and trained riders to look for Waymo on Uber. If your long-term plan is your own app, you don’t build that habit on someone else’s platform.
Read from Uber’s side: blaming Waymo for the missing button is convenient for a platform that is about to lose its largest AV partner in two of its most important AV markets.
I can’t tell you which account is more complete. But I think the phrasing tells us something about the balance of power. “For as long as the two companies agree” is how the party that needs the relationship talks. Waymo set the date, Waymo gave the reason, and Uber is managing the exit. That is consistent with what we have called a controlled decline since CW33.
The rest of the roster
Uber’s driverless pipeline beyond Las Vegas, as described on the call and in the prepared remarks:
Nuro and Lucid: fully driverless testing starts this quarter, with a commercial launch in the Bay Area in Q1 2027 that will be fully driverless from day one. Houston and other cities follow.
Avride: driverless deployments in Texas around the middle of 2027.
Pony.ai and Momenta: added to Uber’s fully driverless inventory internationally in 2027.
NVIDIA: supervised deployments starting in 2027, then driverless.
Rivian: supervised from 2028.
Wayve: supervised testing outside the US, and Dara added Waabi with a “hopefully”.
Already live without a safety operator: WeRide and Baidu in international markets, while Wayve runs supervised commercial rides on Uber in London.
Dara’s summary: Uber is already in those markets, has relationships with the cities and has operations there. “We don’t have to build demand. The demand is already there.”
Open platform, concentrated capital
Then came a sentence that I think is the most important strategic update of the quarter:
“Building a broad ecosystem does not mean spreading our resources evenly.”
Uber says it will keep the platform open, but concentrate capital, integration resources and hands-on deployment support behind a smaller group of partners “with the clearest paths to commercial scale and attractive economics at maturity.” The CFO, Balaji Krishnamurthy, said the same about capital allocation: Uber will increasingly concentrate its AV investments behind the partners and deployments with the strongest expected returns.
Since CW7, we have described Uber’s strategy as one that needs a fragmented AV market, so that no single developer gains too much leverage. In Q2, Uber even turned fragmentation into an industry forecast with its foundation-model analogy. This quarter refines it. Fragmentation still holds at the platform layer: anyone can plug in. At the capital layer, Uber is now picking winners.
Uber also told us where it thinks the money will end up. As more developers solve the driving, Uber expects “an increasing share of the value” to accrue to “distribution, infrastructure, and commercial execution.” That matches what we have argued for a while about the unglamorous operating layer. It is also, of course, exactly the layer Uber owns.
The utilization argument
Uber’s case for why partners should give it a cut of every ride rests on utilization. Its illustrative example: 30% more paid trips per vehicle, with fixed costs unchanged, lowers fixed cost per trip by about 23%, which leaves room for higher fleet profits even after paying Uber’s fee.
And it says it is already seeing this. Uber reports about 30% more trips per vehicle per day for its US AV partners than first-party deployments nationwide. On the call, Balaji went further: in the new markets Waymo and Tesla launched this year, he said, trips per vehicle per day are “in the low teens,” against “high 20s” on Uber’s hybrid networks.
On the AV markets themselves, Uber says its trips grew faster than its overall US business in markets where AVs operated throughout Q3, and that the ridesharing category in mature US AV markets has grown 7 percentage points faster than the rest of the country for several quarters. Its conclusion: “AVs are not a zero-sum game.”
What Uber didn’t mention: the fee per ride
There is one data set this week that adds an important nuance to that picture. The Gridwise’s Q2 2026 AV Impact Report tracks what drivers in the five main robotaxi cities (Atlanta, Austin, Los Angeles, Phoenix and San Francisco) actually see on their trips. Between Q2 2024 and Q2 2026:
Median platform fee per trip in the AV cities: $2.61 to $2.25, down 13.8%
Nationwide, excluding those cities: $2.97 to $3.54, up 19.2%
San Francisco’s fee rate (the share of the fare the platform keeps): from 19.0% to 10.2%
So in the cities with robotaxis, Uber and Lyft keep a smaller share of each ride, while everywhere else their share grew.
There are two ways to read this:
The competitive read: where riders have an autonomous alternative, the platforms have had to give back part of their cut to keep drivers and riders. More rides, but a thinner slice of each.
The cautious read: Gridwise itself says the data cannot separate AV deployment from other factors. Premium rides grew strongly in these cities, rider prices still rose in four of the five, and incentive structures shifted.
I lean toward the competitive read, mainly because the gap is so large and so consistent across cities. But I hold it loosely. What I am fairly confident about is that Uber’s growth claim and Gridwise’s fee data can both be true at the same time. Growing faster in AV markets while earning less per ride there is, I think, the real shape of the question for Uber’s AV business.
Uber on who pays for the cars
The financing question came up again, and Uber gave its most detailed answer so far.
Balaji pointed to the $1 billion financing arrangement Uber set up with Santander earlier this year for its traditional fleet partners as the template, and said announcements on both infrastructure and vehicle financing are coming. In the long run, he said, Uber expects third-party capital to finance this. Near term, with 700 cars this year and 2,000 next, the capital needed is “fairly limited.” Once Uber moves into the “10,000 plus territory in 2028 and beyond,” it wants third parties to fund it.
Dara added why it starts on Uber’s balance sheet. Normal fleet financing (think rental cars) is based on residual value, and “the residual value of [AV] vehicles isn’t established yet.” So he expects AV fleet financing to be based on the revenue potential of the fleet instead. And he made an argument I hadn’t heard before: if a fleet operator fails, Uber can move its vehicles under new ownership, and they keep earning on the Uber network. In his view, that lowers the cost of capital compared with financing a robotaxi that only one operator can use.
If the residual-value problem sounds familiar, it should. It is exactly the bottleneck Doria Holbrook, CEO of Oro Mobility, described on Autonomy Insiders two weeks ago (EP33) [ https://substack.com/redirect/e4817a4d-f559-46f5-b638-fe4ee2d5c99e?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]: there is no proven second-hand market for AVs, so lenders don’t know what a car is worth if they have to take it back. Her proposed bridge was a large mixed-fleet asset-backed structure until that market exists. Uber is now describing the same problem from the platform side, with a different answer: the network itself as the collateral.
One more remark from Dara that is worth noting. He said potential financing partners have been “quite interested,” and that they “want to diversify away from data centers, so to speak.” That is his characterisation, not a disclosed deal. But it is an interesting signal: infrastructure capital that has piled into AI data centers is looking for the next physical asset class, and robotaxi fleets want to be it.
Uber’s rival Lyft also reported this week, though with fewer AV headlines.
Lyft: small and deep
Lyft’s quarter was its best yet by most measures. Gross bookings grew 17% to $5.6 billion, adjusted EBITDA rose 47% to $204 million, rides grew 10% to a record 274 million, and active riders reached 31.6 million. It has now delivered more than $1 billion in trailing twelve-month free cash flow for five quarters in a row. For Q4, it guides to gross bookings growth of 13% to 17%.
Sources: Lyft Q3/2026 Supplemental Slides
On autonomy, the focus was Nashville. Lyft’s pitch fits in one line from its letter: “Waymo builds the driver. Flexdrive keeps the fleet on the road. Together, we connect to riders.”
The details:
Lyft’s fleet subsidiary Flexdrive has serviced the Waymo fleet in Nashville since June, for rides booked through both the Waymo and the Lyft apps.
In October, Lyft opened its permanent AV depot there. By 2027, more than 85 full-time employees will work behind it, many of them former Lyft drivers trained through a certification program built with Waymo.
By year end, Lyft expects to significantly grow the fleet, including Waymo’s newest Ojai vehicles. Waymo has expanded the service area, and airport rides are coming soon on the Lyft app.
CEO David Risher explained why combining the marketplace with fleet management matters with a simple example. When a car needs charging or a sensor cleaning, he said, “that’s just another ride.” The same system that matches riders to cars can send the car to the depot at the moment the technicians are ready and electricity is cheapest.
Lyft is bringing the same model to Europe. In London, it is testing Baidu’s Apollo Go vehicles on public roads ahead of a commercial launch in 2027, and is building its operations team ahead of opening its first AV depot in Europe. Lyft did not name a site or a date. Risher added that Baidu has “relatively fewer capabilities” than Waymo, so Lyft takes on more of the technical work there, and also a regulatory role, because regulators want to work with credible partners.
On the partner strategy, Risher was clear: “We’re looking for a small number of partners to go deep on.” Baidu and Waymo are the two Lyft is most focused on, though “there will be others for sure.” A deep partnership involves a lot of integration, he said, and “you don’t do that with 20 different partners.”
And on financing, Lyft funds all of its AV capital needs from its own balance sheet today, which the CFO called “relatively immaterial” next to its free cash flow. As scale grows, Lyft expects third-party capital to come in.
What strikes me about this week is how similar the two platforms now sound. Uber talks about concentrating on “a smaller group of partners.” Lyft talks about “a small number of partners to go deep on.” They get there from opposite directions: Uber narrowing down from more than 30 partners, Lyft building up from two. But for the first time, both say the scarce resource is integration and capital, not the number of logos.
What this means and what to watch
First, Uber’s driverless count is more concentrated than it looks. Roughly 600 of the 700 likely sit in Austin, Atlanta and the UAE, and more than half is probably Waymo. Watch whether Uber starts reporting partner or city splits. If it keeps reporting only the total, the total will keep saying less than it appears to.
Second, the 2027 number depends on the middle of the market. Getting from 700 to 2,000 means roughly 1,300 new vehicles from partners like Nuro, Avride, Pony.ai and Momenta. Watch the Bay Area launch in Q1 2027: driverless from day one is the strongest commitment on Uber’s list, and the easiest one to grade.
Third, Las Vegas is the first real test of the dedicated option. Watch whether Uber launches the autonomous button there, how it decides which partner gets which ride, and whether the button changes rider behaviour once the novelty wears off.
Fourth, watch the fee per ride as closely as the rides. Uber’s growth in AV markets is real. Whether it keeps its cut there is the open question, and Gridwise’s next report is the place to look.
Fifth, financing is the next disclosure. Uber has promised announcements on vehicle and infrastructure financing. Watch for a named lender, a structure and, above all, whether the residual value gets priced or simply assumed away.
Sixth, Lyft’s European depot. A named site and date for London would tell us how close the Baidu launch really is.

Tesla, Zoox and Japan’s new rulebook
Tesla’s week ran from a bigger map in Texas to a smaller speed margin in Europe and a consumer complaint in Korea. And the most consequential news for purpose-built robotaxis came from a regulator in Tokyo.
Tesla launches in Fort Worth
Next to the two ride-hail players, Tesla also made headlines again this week.
Its Robotaxi service opened to riders in Fort Worth at 6 a.m. on Monday, 2 November, its seventh US city, and according to Sawyer Merritt it runs unsupervised.
The geofence is large:
About 143 square miles 
About 13 miles east to west and 15 miles north to south at its widest
Centred on downtown Fort Worth, reaching White Settlement in the west, Haltom City in the east, Benbrook and Forest Hill in the south, and the edge of Saginaw in the north
Source: X
That makes it Tesla’s third-largest geofence, behind Austin and Houston, and more than one and a half times the size of neighbouring Dallas (81 square miles). As we reported in CW44, Houston itself grew from about 25 to about 254 square miles only two weeks ago. Tesla is clearly in a phase of drawing big maps quickly. As I wrote last week, how many cars actually serve those maps, and how many paid miles they drive, is what will tell us how far along it really is.
The standard car business also looks better again in Europe.
Tesla’s European sales are recovering
Tesla’s registrations rose sharply in October across several markets, according to Reuters:
Spain: +271%
Sweden: +248%
Portugal: +186%
Norway: +131%
Italy: +94%
Denmark: +34%
Reuters names easier comparisons with a weak October last year first, followed by higher fuel prices, government incentives and continued growth in EV demand. Tesla also said in October that it ended Q3 with its largest order backlog in four years.
Norway was the bright spot: Tesla’s best October on record and the country’s top-selling brand with a 9.8% share.
But the percentages flatter the picture in some markets. In Denmark, Tesla’s share was only 0.73%, behind BYD, Leapmotor, XPeng and MG, which each took between 1.6% and 5.5%. In Italy, Tesla’s share rose to 0.38%, still below BYD, Leapmotor and MG at 1.21% to 4.55%. And even in Norway, Chinese brands reached 22.5% of new registrations, their highest monthly share on record.
So the recovery is real, but it is a recovery from a low base, in a market where the Chinese brands are growing faster.
A topic we have followed for months is Tesla Assisted Driving (TAD) in Europe, and here something moved this week.
Tesla caps the speed margin in Europe
Quick recap. Ten EU countries now recognise the Dutch approval: the Netherlands plus Belgium, Croatia, Czechia, Denmark, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Slovakia and Slovenia. As we reported in CW45, Germany said it will support EU approval after Tesla agreed to drop the “Full Self-Driving” name and cap the speed tolerance at 10%, and Italy’s transport minister Matteo Salvini has since said Italy supports a swift EU approval too.
Sweden had raised a different concern. In June, it asked the EU to reject the system unless Tesla removed the ability to set speeds above the posted limit.
That is now what Tesla is addressing. The Dutch regulator RDW has approved an update that:
Cuts the maximum speed margin from +50% to +10%. Before, the system could be set to drive up to 150 km/h on a 100 km/h highway, or 45 km/h in a 30 km/h zone.
Resets the margin to 0% every time the car starts
Turns other speed-related functions off by default
Renames the system from “FSD Supervised” to “Tesla Assisted Driving” in the car, the manual and other driver information on HW4 vehicles
The RDW said it found no adverse or unintended effects and folded the changes into the existing type approval, without amending or extending it. It also stressed that the new name changes nothing about what the system is: driver assistance, with the driver responsible.
Here is the catch. Sweden’s position was zero: no setting above the limit at all. France has also cited speeding in cities as one of its reasons for not recognising the Dutch approval. So the cap clearly moves Tesla towards the critics, and it delivers Germany’s conditions in full. Whether it is enough for Sweden and France, we will see.
The scoreboard from CW45 still applies. A qualified majority needs 15 of 27 member states representing at least 65% of the EU’s population. With Germany and Italy, Tesla would be at 12 countries and about 45.8%, still three countries and about 19 percentage points short. The vote has slipped to December at the earliest, and the talks in the committee are confidential.
Korea: FSD that most owners can’t use
In South Korea, FSD has a different problem.
When Tesla enabled “FSD (Supervised)” in Korea late last year, it only reached the Model S, Model X and Cybertruck. Those are built in the US and enter Korea under the KORUS free trade agreement, which recognises US safety standards. The Model 3 and Model Y, which come from Giga Shanghai, have no equivalent agreement and would need a Korean approval that FSD does not have.
The numbers make it a big issue:
Tesla’s Korea country manager told lawmakers that FSD works on about 20% of registered Teslas. By Tesla’s own figures, the complaint argues, that means 99% of Teslas sold in Korea last year could not run it.
Tesla has sold more than 170,000 Shanghai-built cars in Korea since 2023.
For years, it charged a one-time 9.043 million won (about $6,700) for FSD, and since August a monthly subscription of 150,000 won (about $112). It has been taking FSD payments from buyers of China-built cars for more than three years.
Now the consumer group Citizens United for Consumer Sovereignty is filing a complaint with the Korea Fair Trade Commission. It wants the regulator to temporarily stop Tesla from marketing FSD for ineligible cars, impose fines and refer the case to state investigators. The KFTC already opened a probe into Tesla’s FSD advertising earlier this year.
Two details round out the picture. At a parliamentary hearing on 8 October, Tesla’s country manager testified that Tesla had never formally asked the transport ministry to test FSD. Tesla filed for certification about two weeks later. And the ministry told a lawmaker that FSD in its current form does not comply with local rules, including the requirement to keep hands on the wheel.
To be fair to Tesla, a Seoul court has already dismissed a refund lawsuit from owners, pointing to Korean regulation rather than Tesla as the reason the features never launched. So the legal outcome is open. But selling a feature for three years that the regulator has not been asked to approve is a hard position to defend in front of a consumer authority.
Zoox wants to go abroad
Zoox also had news this week. Speaking at WSJ Tech Live, CEO Aicha Evans named London, other EU cities, South Korea and Japan as potential markets for the Amazon-owned company.
What she did not give was a timeline. Zoox has not said when testing abroad will begin or when its purpose-built robotaxis could enter those markets. The first step would be modified Toyota Highlander SUVs fitted with sensors, to collect data on local roads. Zoox does not plan to offer public rides in them.
For context, Evans said Zoox runs about 150 robotaxis across several US cities today. And it starts on Uber in Las Vegas later this month.
And that brings us to the last news of the week, which should please Zoox in particular, and Tesla too.
Japan writes rules for cars without steering wheels
Japan’s transport ministry (MLIT) plans to create one uniform set of safety standards for driverless vehicles without steering wheels, brake pedals or accelerators. Until now, Japan has certified such vehicles one by one.
Here is what the plan covers, according to Nikkei:
Scope: Level 4 and Level 5 vehicles
Timing: vehicles produced from December onwards
What can go: steering wheel, pedals, rear-view mirrors and the driver monitoring system
What stays: crash tests with dummies for passenger vehicles like taxis and buses. Cargo vehicles are exempt from those tests, but manufacturers must show that accidents can be avoided if a system fails.
The reason is industrial policy. Removing the controls lowers production costs and frees up interior space, and Japan wants mass production. The government’s target is at least 10,000 Level 4 buses and taxis on its roads by fiscal 2030. As a ministry representative put it, Japan wants to move toward that target “by making mass production easier.”
It also fits the international picture. At its June meeting, the UN’s World Forum for Harmonization of Vehicle Regulations (WP.29) agreed on safety standards for vehicles without driver controls, and those are expected to be adopted in December. Japan is aligning its domestic rules with them.
The rule covers the vehicle, and only the vehicle. As Rob Grant pointed out on Road to Autonomy this week, operators will still need separate road-use permits to run a driverless passenger service on public roads, and the police authorities still have a say in how quickly vehicles go into service. And as we reported in CW34, Japanese law requires passenger transport to be run by an authorised operator, which is why Waymo works with Nihon Kotsu and GO in Tokyo and Uber with Hinomaru Kotsu. So Japan has pulled the vehicle lever first, while the service side still moves one permit at a time.
The US is moving on a different track. It is not part of the WP.29 agreement that provides mutual recognition of approvals between countries. American manufacturers self-certify that they meet federal safety standards, and a vehicle that doesn’t fit those standards can apply for an exemption. That is the route Zoox took, with an exemption that allows up to 2,500 vehicles a year (CW39), while Tesla self-certified the Cybercab and now faces an NHTSA audit query on exactly that (CW37). At the same time, the US Department of Transportation has proposed removing manual-control requirements for vehicles designed to drive themselves, NHTSA proposed rule changes for vehicles without brake pedals in June, and it recently proposed lighting rules for driverless vehicles.
So the timing is good for Zoox. Its robotaxi has no steering wheel and no pedals, and in the same week Zoox named Japan and South Korea as potential markets, Japan announced it will certify exactly that kind of vehicle under standard rules. For Tesla, whose Cybercab has carried riders in Austin without a steering wheel or pedals since September, it is a positive signal too. Japan has said it will write a standard but has not published the technical details yet, and a vehicle self-certified or exempted in the US does not qualify automatically. Tesla and Zoox may well need a Japanese variant of their purpose-built robotaxis.
What this means and what to watch
First, Tesla’s maps are growing faster than its proof. Fort Worth adds a large geofence in a week. Watch the Texas registrations and, above all, whether Tesla reports paid miles again.
Second, December turns on speed. The 10% cap delivers Germany’s conditions in full. Watch whether Sweden and France accept 10% or hold out for zero, and whether Italy’s support becomes a yes vote.
Third, Japan is the first major market to standardise the steering-wheel-free car. Watch which manufacturer files first under the new rules. The candidates are already lining up: Toyota with Waymo, reported three days before the rules came out (CW45); Nissan with Wayve, whose CEO Alex Kendall took a Japanese minister on a drive through Tokyo on the day the rules were released; and Isuzu with Applied Intuition. Because the vehicle approval is only half of it, also watch for the first road-use permit for a driverless passenger service, and whether Zoox puts a date on Japan or Korea.
Fourth, the regulatory gap between the US and the rest is changing shape. The US still runs on self-certification and exemptions. Japan and the UN framework now offer a standard route. Watch whether NHTSA’s proposals turn into final rules, because that decides how fast purpose-built vehicles can scale in the US compared with Japan.
