Everyone’s watching the AV rideshare race through four layers: tech, apps, fleets, and ops. But there’s a fifth layer hiding in plain sight, and it’s worth $200B: charging infrastructure. Without power, an AV can’t serve a single ride. Without a network, it can’t serve a city.
Terawatt breaks down why infrastructure demands its own specialized capital and expertise, why fleet operators can’t easily build it themselves, and what it’ll take to keep pace as AVs scale 200x by 2035.

Tesla’s comeback runs through Europe, and Waymo’s car for Munich may be German?
Tesla’s car business is growing again, and it no longer needs the US to do it. Europe is where the growth comes from, and Europe is also where its driver has to win permission.
Tesla is on track to end two years of falling sales
This was a good week for Tesla, and for once the good news came from the car business.
Tesla delivered 486,532 vehicles in the third quarter, against an analyst consensus of 456,896 compiled by Visible Alpha. 
Source: Reuters
That is about 30,000 more than expected and roughly 6,000 more than in the second quarter, so it is the second strong quarter in a row after a weak start to the year. Production came in at 464,391. The shares, which had lost about a fifth of their value this year, rose more than 5% in early trading.
Compared with last year, the quarter is still slightly down. Tesla delivered about 497,000 vehicles in Q3 2025, its best quarter ever, because US buyers rushed to use the $7,500 federal tax credit before it expired at the end of September 2025. Without that credit, Tesla’s US sales were down nearly 20% year over year before Friday’s numbers, according to Cox Automotive.
The annual picture is what changed. To match last year’s total, Tesla now needs 311,448 more deliveries in the fourth quarter. It has not delivered fewer than that in a single quarter since mid-2022. So unless Q4 falls off a cliff, the two-year streak of falling annual sales ends this year. Analysts have moved accordingly: the consensus for full-year 2026 is now 1.82 million deliveries, up from 1.65 million in June.
Most investors look past these numbers. Tesla’s valuation of roughly $1.4 trillion rests on AI, robotaxis and the Optimus humanoid robot, and car sales have become something Musk rarely talks about. Last month Tesla added the purpose-built Cybercab to its robotaxi service in Austin, and this week it secured up to $30 billion in new credit lines to fund those projects.
But vehicle sales are still Tesla’s largest source of revenue. A core business that grows again is what pays for the long-term bets, and it gives the company room it did not have six months ago. Tesla reports its full quarterly results on 21 October.
Europe is recovering
The growth came from outside the US, and above all from Europe.
Last year, Tesla’s European sales slumped. Part of that was the backlash against Musk’s politics, and part was competition from cheaper Chinese EVs. This year the trend has reversed. EU registrations rose by about two-thirds from January to August compared with a year earlier, according to the European Automobile Manufacturers’ Association (ACEA). The recovery gathered pace in the third quarter, with strong registration growth in France and Denmark and broader gains in September. Tesla is reportedly expanding capacity at its German plant to meet the demand, and its Shanghai factory kept selling well too, partly thanks to newer export markets such as Japan, Australia and Lithuania.
Reuters names three drivers of the European rebound: government incentives, easy comparisons with a weak year-ago period, and rising consumer interest in EVs.
Analysts also expect Full Self-Driving to help sales further. Morningstar’s Seth Goldstein called FSD “a differentiator that drives consumers to choose Tesla over other autos.”
I would be careful about giving FSD credit for the recovery so far. The first approval came in the Netherlands in April, and most of the countries that have followed are smaller markets. Germany and France, the EU’s two largest car markets, are not on the list. So far the European recovery is mainly about incentives and comparisons. FSD is the lever analysts expect next, and whether it gets pulled depends on Brussels.
But the list grew again this week. 
Croatia joins the FSD list, and Europe’s safety lobby pushes back
On Tuesday, Tesla said supervised FSD has been approved in Croatia, with a nationwide rollout expected to begin soon. Neighbouring Slovenia approved it earlier this month. Croatia makes it eight European countries.
Source: Reuters
The Dutch regulator RDW, which approved FSD first in April, is also proposing that FSD should be approved across the EU. That vote was expected in October and has been pushed back, with the next realistic chance not before December.
And the opposition is getting organised. Last week it was a Belgian advocacy group, whose test drives in Brussels found FSD over the limit in more than half of the 30 km/h zones it tested. This week it was the European Transport Safety Council (ETSC), a prominent road safety group.
ETSC urged EU member states to reject FSD’s approval, and its argument is precise:
FSD includes a speed offset function that can raise the car’s maximum speed by as much as 50% above the limit the vehicle detects
Tesla has not sought an exemption from the relevant UN Economic Commission for Europe rules to allow the system to exceed posted limits
The Dutch approval granted exemptions to a series of UN rules, but the speed offset was not one of them
ETSC also wants regulators to reject a separate feature that matches driving speed to surrounding traffic
Sweden, France and the Czech Republic have all raised concerns about speeding, according to Reuters. The Czech name is an interesting one, because the Czech Republic recognised FSD nationally only recently, as we noted in CW40. 
Tesla did not immediately respond to Reuters’ request for comment. Musk has said in the past that delaying FSD approval “will cost lives.”
Last week I wrote that Tesla’s European path runs through one binary question: does Tesla offer an EU configuration that cannot exceed posted limits? ETSC is now asking for exactly that. On paper, it is the easiest of Tesla’s European problems to fix, because it concerns a feature rather than the system’s underlying capability. Whether Tesla is willing to ship a version that drives more cautiously than the one it sells in the US is a different question, and December will tell us.
Tesla was not the only American EV maker reporting on Friday. Rivian had its best quarter ever.
Rivian’s R2 delivers a record quarter
It built 19,751 vehicles in the third quarter and delivered 19,248. That is roughly 45% more than a year ago and 58% more than in the second quarter. Rivian did not say how many of those were R2s, but this was the first full quarter of sales for its new, more affordable SUV after the June launch. Electrek reads the R2 as “likely responsible for much, if not all, of this growth,” even though Rivian is only selling the higher-priced Performance version until the end of the year. The R2 starts at just under $60,000, and cheaper versions at around $45,000 are planned.
Here is how the year has built up:
Q1 2026: 10,236 produced, 10,365 delivered
Q2 2026: 12,613 produced, 12,194 delivered
Q3 2026: 19,751 produced, 19,248 delivered
That makes 41,807 deliveries in the first nine months. Rivian reaffirmed its full-year guidance of 65,000 to 70,000 vehicles, which means it needs roughly 23,000 to 28,000 deliveries in the fourth quarter. That is another step up of about 20% to 45% on a quarter that was already a record. According to TechCrunch, Rivian expects to sell 20,000 to 25,000 R2s by the end of the year, which would make it one of the fastest-selling EVs in US history, second only to Tesla’s Model Y.
If Rivian hits its range, 2026 will be its best year ever, after several years stuck at around 50,000 vehicles. It would also be a sign that the R2 is selling well into a weak market. The share of EVs in US light-vehicle sales has hovered around 6% to 7% this year, down from a peak above 10% in 2025, after the federal purchase credits ended.
For our industry, the R2 matters for a second reason. It is also the car Rivian will turn into a robotaxi for Uber. Rivian believes robotaxis are a big enough market that in March it pushed back its 2027 profitability target to invest more in autonomy. So the R2 now has two jobs: stop years of heavy losses in the car business and carry the autonomy bet. This quarter suggests it can do the first. Rivian reports full financial results on 29 October.
From the companies building the cars to the rules for running them.
New law will fine robotaxi operators for blocking first responders
California Governor Gavin Newsom has signed Senate Bill 1246, introduced by State Senator Dave Cortese. It sets new rules for what happens when a robotaxi breaks down or gets in the way of emergency services, and it creates ways to hold the companies accountable if they fail.
The law follows a string of incidents in California in which robotaxis broke down and disrupted traffic, drove into crime scenes or got in the way of first responders. Most of them involved Waymo, the largest robotaxi operator in the US, with about 4,000 vehicles in its commercial fleet nationwide, about 1,200 of them in the San Francisco Bay Area. NHTSA has also sent a letter to AV developers demanding “solutions” to the problem.
“California has embraced autonomous vehicles, but we cannot embrace innovation at the expense of public safety,” Cortese said.
What changes:
Fines: companies can be fined if a robotaxi blocks police or firefighters during an emergency for more than 30 minutes
People on the ground: companies have to provide “local incident technicians” who can help with crashes and obstructions
Information for cities: during system-wide failures, companies have to tell cities and other local authorities where their vehicles are and what state they are in
Remote drivers: anyone who remotely drives a robotaxi must be based in the US and hold a US driver’s licence
The last point is the one with the most interesting detail. The law uses the term “remote driver,” and it defines it narrowly: a human who directly operates or drives the vehicle from afar. That is much tighter than “remote operations,” a term the industry has used very loosely.
The distinction matters because most companies say they do not drive their vehicles remotely at all. Waymo, for example, uses remote assistance: the self-driving system stays in control, and a human can give guidance or send commands if the car needs help. Waymo’s remote assistance network is global, including staff in the Philippines, and it runs command centres in Arizona and Michigan. Zoox says its remote operations teams are based in the US. Tesla is the only company that has publicly said it has remote operators who can take direct control of its robotaxis.
So the law is softer than the headline. The Philippines question senators put to Waymo in February was about remote assistance, and by the law’s own definition that work is very likely not covered by the US-only requirement. Waymo’s statement points the same way. It thanked lawmakers “for the amendments made to the bill, which ensure autonomous vehicle operators can still feasibly serve Californians.”
There is also a long runway. The law takes effect in July 2028, and the California DMV still has to set guidelines on parts of it, including the response times companies will have to meet. So the principle is set today, and the number that will matter most in practice, how fast a technician has to arrive, comes later.
Waymo and Zoox have both said they will comply.
From the regulator to the operator.
Lyft opens its 80,000 square foot depot and takes its app to Europe
Flexdrive, Lyft’s fleet management subsidiary, has officially opened its new 80,000 square foot depot in Nashville. Waymo vehicles will move in on 12 October. Since September, Nashville riders can be matched with Waymo rides on the Lyft app, and Flexdrive has taken over day-to-day fleet operations for Waymo’s vehicles there.
What is inside:
Charging: dozens of fast chargers, able to support hundreds of autonomous vehicles
Maintenance: bays for high-voltage repairs
Parts: secure storage on site
The jobs are a big part of the story Lyft is telling. The facility brings more than 70 new full-time positions this year: technicians, fleet operations managers and associates, repair coordinators and maintenance specialists. Lyft says it could grow to hundreds of jobs in the coming years. Current and former Lyft drivers get priority for interviews, and Flexdrive trains people in-house on vehicle systems, sensor calibration and fleet operations. Lyft frames it as proof that AV growth creates driver-adjacent careers.
Flexdrive is also already looking beyond this building. As the fleet grows, it is exploring additional locations in Nashville to cut wait times for riders and reduce congestion. “Dozens of chargers for hundreds of vehicles” gives a sense of the fleet size Nashville is being built for, and a second or third site would be one of the clearest public signals that it is getting there.
Lyft takes its app to Europe
Nashville was not Lyft’s only expansion this week. Since 30 September, North American Lyft riders can use their Lyft app when they travel to Europe. Early access started in Barcelona and Hamburg. Throughout October, London, Rome, Paris, Berlin, Athens and Warsaw follow, and by the end of the month the app will work in ten countries: Spain, Italy, Greece, Poland, England, Scotland, Ireland, Austria, France and Germany. Riders keep the same app, account and payment methods, and within the next year they will be able to choose between paying in their home currency or the local one.
It is worth being precise about who this is for. The launch targets North American travellers, while riders in Europe keep using Freenow by Lyft and Gett by Lyft. For drivers on those platforms, it is a new source of demand from visitors crossing the Atlantic.
The more interesting part sits underneath. Lyft built a new backend framework that connects its rider app to a third-party marketplace and gives riders access to that marketplace’s driver supply. Europe is its first application, and Lyft says it can bring on more marketplaces in more countries. In Lyft’s words, the company is moving from “your neighborhood rideshare app to a global, multi-mobility platform.”
I think the framework matters beyond tourism. An app that can plug into someone else’s supply is a close cousin of what Lyft already does in Nashville, where riders on the Lyft app are matched with Waymo’s vehicles. Lyft has not said it will use the framework for autonomous supply, but the plumbing for it now exists.
On the Autonomy Markets show from The Road to Autonomy this week, Walter Piecyk raised the bigger strategic question. If Uber ends up owning its own autonomy stack, could Lyft, with a growing global footprint, become the alternative platform for AV companies that do not want to compete with Uber’s own robotaxis on Uber’s app?
I think he has a real point. AV companies are happy to share Uber's app today, but that calculation changes the moment Uber owns a competing driver. A Lyft that already reaches riders across North America, and through FreeNow across Europe, would be the obvious place to go.
Put together, Lyft’s week covered both ends of the business: a depot to keep autonomous vehicles on the road in Nashville, and a network to reach riders well beyond North America.
Waymo gets a new depot in Nashville. Does it also get a new carmaker in Germany?
Waymo’s new German OEM platform leaked?
Mario Herger has published a video, recorded by Bernhard Kremer while driving past Waymo’s depot in Aschheim, just outside Munich. At first glance it looks unremarkable: more than 30 Waymo Jaguar I-Pace, and among them a BMW 5 Series Touring (G61) with a roof box, the kind of car you would assume belongs to an employee.
Source: Mario Herger
But drive around the corner to the other end of the depot, and there are at least three more G61 Tourings with roof boxes. In between them stand four BMW 5 Series saloons.
Source: Mario Herger
Is that a coincidence? It could be. But like Mario, I think it may be more than that. And it could be one of the reasons Waymo chose Munich, BMW’s home city, as the base for Waymo Deutschland GmbH.
When Waymo announced Munich in CW36, its FAQ named districts, testing hours and the legal basis. What it did not name was the vehicle. Herger adds a few points that make a German platform plausible. Waymos in Germany will have to drive on the Autobahn, where the recommended speed alone is 130 km/h, well above the roughly 105 km/h (65 mph) limit on most US highways, and even higher speeds are allowed. The roof boxes, he suggests, could be used to simulate the sensor housings of the Waymo Driver for aerodynamic testing at those speeds. And BMW would gain experience integrating the Waymo Driver into a privately owned car.
For Germany, it would be a strong signal if the Waymos on our roads were built on German vehicles rather than on the Chinese-built Zeekr that underpins the Waymo Ojai. I also think German carmakers probably bring more regulatory weight in their home market, which could make the approval process easier.
All of this is speculation, of course. But based on the pictures, I think it is a fair one. I am curious whether the German launch will come with a German platform.
What this all means and what to watch
First, Tesla’s growth now comes from outside the US, and the full Q3 results on 21 October are the next checkpoint. Watch for regional detail on Europe, and for whether the recovery shows up in margins as well as volumes. A car business that grows again gives Tesla more room to fund its robotaxi and robot plans without leaning on its credit lines.
Second, Europe’s FSD decision has narrowed to one feature. December is the earliest date for an EU-wide vote. If Tesla offers an EU configuration without the speed offset, ETSC’s main objection falls away and a qualified majority becomes plausible. If it does not, each new national approval adds momentum but not a majority. And the more FSD becomes part of the sales story in Europe, the more this vote matters for the core business too.
Third, Rivian needs 23,000 to 28,000 deliveries in Q4, with only the Performance version of the R2 on sale. Watch the R2 split on 29 October, and watch the margin. The R2 has to make the car business work and carry the robotaxi programme with Uber, and the second job depends on the first.
Fourth, the Lyft and Flexdrive model in Nashville gets its real test from 12 October. Watch for a second depot, which would be the clearest sign that the fleet is outgrowing the first.
Fifth, watch for Waymo naming a vehicle for Germany. If it is a BMW, Germany's first Waymo service would run on a German car, and the decision would say as much about approval strategy as about engineering.

Momenta’s two businesses both make news, and Stellantis picks one driver per region
Momenta has only been a listed company since July, when it raised around $751 million in its Hong Kong IPO. It runs two businesses: driver-assistance software that it sells to carmakers, and its own robotaxis. This week, both made news.
Momenta brings its driver to Peugeot and Jeep
Start with the business that already makes the volume.
Momenta has signed a global strategic partnership with Dongfeng Stellantis Automotive Technology (DSAT), based in Wuhan. The agreement was signed in Shanghai on 24 September and announced on Monday. The precise counterparty matters: DSAT is the joint venture that Dongfeng, Stellantis and other partners formed in August to compete better against Chinese rivals. So this is Stellantis’ Chinese joint venture choosing Momenta, for cars that will then be sold well beyond China.
What they plan:
The system: an advanced driver-assistance system based on Momenta’s R7 world model, for new Peugeot and Jeep production models
Co-development: Momenta takes part in product definition and joint development, tuning steering, braking and powertrain response to each brand, Jeep’s off-road capabilities and Peugeot’s handling
The cars: several all-new DSAT models, battery-electric and plug-in hybrid, from 2027
The markets: China, Europe and other global markets
Beyond assistance: the partnership also covers autonomous driving technology, including Level 3
The numbers behind Momenta’s production business explain why carmakers keep signing. In the first half of this year, its solutions went into about 321,000 additional vehicles, up 83.7% year over year, according to its interim report. By the end of June, cumulative installations had passed 1 million, across 105 production models and 26 carmakers, and vehicles with its software had been exported to 10 countries and regions. Reuters puts the number of vehicles on the road with Momenta’s software at more than 1.2 million today.
The deal also changes who Momenta’s international customers are. Until now, its best-known partners outside China were German premium brands. Peugeot and Jeep are volume brands, and through DSAT’s export plans, Momenta’s software will reach European roads under Stellantis badges. Momenta CEO Cao Xudong said the company is preparing for an inflection point in the global driver-assistance market in 2028 and wants to be positioned ahead of it.
Stellantis’ other bet: Wayve
But Momenta is not the only driver Stellantis put on display this week.
Stellantis has fitted Wayve’s AI driving software to development versions of the Fiat 500e and the Maserati Grecale. Both will give hands-free demonstration rides at the Wave by Vento event in Turin from 7 to 9 October, following a Jeep Grand Cherokee prototype shown at Stellantis’ Investor Day in May.
How it works:
The stack: the Wayve AI Driver combined with Stellantis’ STLA AutoDrive platform
The level: supervised, door-to-door automated driving at Level 2++, on highways and in cities
The range: one system across a small electric car and two SUVs, which suggests software that can move between platforms without separate development for each model
The timing: the first product is due in North America in 2028, with the launch brand not yet named
One driver per region
Put the two announcements side by side. Momenta for cars developed with Stellantis’ Chinese joint venture and sold in China and Europe. Wayve for North America. Stellantis is clearly hedging its driving software bets, and the hedge follows geography. One reason is likely to keep Chinese code out of the US market. And US rules leave Stellantis little choice. 
From model year 2027, US rules ban Chinese software in connected vehicles sold in the US, and Chinese hardware follows from model year 2029. A Momenta-equipped Jeep could not be sold in America. So the regional split is at least partly a compliance decision rather than a free choice. It fits what we argued in CW39: the problem with Chinese autonomy technology is, above all, an American one. In Europe, the same Chinese driver is welcome enough to be built into Peugeots.
Stellantis also sits on the other side of the table: it supplies vehicles for other companies’ robotaxi plans, including Uber’s and Bolt’s. Few carmakers are sitting on as many sides of this market at the same time.
Momenta’s robotaxi: from 100 vehicles to several thousand
Now to Momenta’s second business.
Today, Momenta’s robotaxi business is still small. It has more than 100 vehicles deployed across three countries, and it is testing in five Chinese cities, in Munich and in Abu Dhabi. In Germany, Momenta holds a nationwide Level 4 testing permit, which we covered in CW32, and Munich is its first European city with Uber.
The plans are much bigger. Shuo Xie, who heads Momenta’s robotaxi business, laid them out in an interview with Reuters this week:
Fleet size: “hundreds” of robotaxis by the end of this year, and “several thousand” by the end of 2027
Middle East: vehicles deployed in Dubai next year
Europe: talks with “a few” European cities about further expansion
Asia: Japan named as a priority
The most interesting detail is about cost. Momenta has been working with chip company XHeart on chips designed specifically for its driving software. Xie said their cost should be “significantly lower” than chips with equivalent computing power from Nvidia. XHeart is already working on a next-generation chip, the X9. “In a couple of years the robotaxis that I am going to deploy around the world, hopefully a lot of them will be hosted on that X9 chip,” she said.
That is how the two businesses fit together. The driver-assistance business brings in volume, data and customers across 26 carmakers. The robotaxi business is the bet on top of it, and Momenta, backed by Mercedes-Benz and partnered with Toyota, BYD and Uber, remains unprofitable as it spends heavily on research and development. A cheaper in-house chip is the lever that could make the robotaxi side pay, once the fleet is large enough to need it.
What this means and what to watch
First, the supplier route is how Chinese autonomy reaches Europe at scale. Momenta’s robotaxis in Munich are still testing. Its software in Peugeot and Jeep models will be on sale from 2027. Watch which European markets get the first DSAT models with Momenta’s system, and whether the Level 3 part of the agreement turns into a product.
Second, one driver per region is becoming a template for global carmakers. US rules make it close to a requirement for any group that uses a Chinese software supplier and also sells in America. 
Third, Wayve’s Turin demos are a shop window. Running one driver across a Fiat 500e, a Maserati and a Jeep is the argument for buying driving software rather than building it. The question is whether Stellantis names the launch brand, and whether the 2028 date holds.
Fourth, Momenta’s robotaxi targets have dates attached. “Hundreds” by December and “several thousand” by the end of 2027 are numbers we can check. Watch for the Dubai launch, the first named European city after Munich, and whether Munich moves beyond testing.

💡 Quick Takes
Kodiak names IKEA as its first driverless customer
IKEA will be Kodiak’s launch shipper for driverless freight later this year, after a four-year partnership in which Kodiak delivered more than 1,300 loads and logged more than 750,000 miles carrying IKEA goods autonomously, with a safety observer on board. The first driver-out leg covers 219 miles, mainly along Interstate 45 between Kodiak’s Houston and Dallas-area facilities, as part of the 292-mile route from IKEA’s distribution centre in Baytown to its store in Frisco. Kodiak says it is completing the safety case for driverless long-haul operations.

Volvo Autonomous Solutions passes 3 million tonnes hauled autonomously
Volvo Autonomous Solutions (V.A.S.) has hauled more than 3 million tonnes of material autonomously in mining and quarrying, across customer sites in Europe. Insurers are building the robotaxi policy
Two more insurance moves this week, two weeks after Waymo and Allianz Partners announced their partnership for London and Munich. Dubai opens a test site that runs from closed track to public road
Dubai’s Roads and Transport Authority (RTA) has opened Dubai Mobility Labs at Dubai Silicon Oasis, a test site that combines closed tracks with a public-road route to Al Qudra. DEKRA will operate the site for its first three years. WeRide, Pony.ai and Tesla have already run trials there. The RTA’s aim is a single path from development to certification, with its target of 25% autonomous trips by 2030 now just over three years away.

Munich’s automated city bus is out on line 178
Since early September, an automated 12-metre MAN Lion’s City 12 E has been driving on bus line 178 in Munich, between Petuelring and Freimanner Hölzl, as part of the MINGA research project. The automated driving system comes from MAN’s technology partner ADASTEC, and the bus carries five lidar sensors, six radars and eight cameras. Trained safety drivers stay on board throughout. From around November, a closed group of test passengers can ride along, with the bus running as an extra vehicle on the regular line. The KBA granted the operating permit under Germany’s AFGBV, and the federal transport ministry is funding the project with around €13 million until mid-2027. With Waymo and Momenta also testing there, Munich is fast becoming one of Germany’s busiest cities for autonomous driving.
