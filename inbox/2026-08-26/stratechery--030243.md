Apple Updates Mini and Studio
-----------------------------

From the Wall Street Journal ( https://www.wsj.com/tech/personal-tech/mac-mini-mac-studio-apple-ai-memory-chip-41d2a74b ) :

Apple is releasing new models of its AI-friendly desktop machines — the Mac Mini and Studio — after the previous generations became sleeper hits, and vanished from shelves. While the next-generation hardware looks identical to previous models, it packs faster, pricier chips, not to mention higher price tags. But can Apple build enough to meet demand?

The Mac Mini with an all-new M6 chip starts at $899, and one with an M5 Pro starts at $1,699—both prices $100 higher than the previous M4 and M4 Pro Minis. The Mac Studio with an M5 Max starts at $2,499 while one with the M5 Ultra starts at $5,499, a $200 jump from the previous M3 Ultra model. Apple says the new chips boast artificial-intelligence performance that’s at least four times faster than predecessors while graphics performance is at least two times faster.

Demand has exceeded supply for the previous Mac Mini and Studio. Apple even removed the largest memory options earlier this year—but now the company is once again offering those higher tiers and more: The Mac Studio supports a whopping 512 gigabytes of RAM, a configuration that will ship late October. The new models ship on Sept. 22, with preorders beginning Tuesday.

There are a bunch of interesting tidbits just in this excerpt. First, those price increases are on top of the price increases Apple enacted in June ( https://www.macrumors.com/2026/06/25/apple-just-increased-prices/ ). The M5 Pro Mini, for example, is $300 more than its predecessor was originally; the M5 Ultra, meanwhile, is $1,500 more than its predecessor was originally, which makes sense, given its significantly higher base memory and storage. The real takeaway, then, is that memory prices continue to increase even more than Apple anticipated a couple of months ago.

Second, it’s fascinating that Apple is pre-announcing these so early; usually you would expect new models to ship later the same week as an announcement, or maybe the following week, but for the Mini and Studio you will need to wait nearly a month. My guess is that Apple is very uncertain about where demand will fall in terms of configuration, and it doesn’t want to waste any precious RAM or SSDs (or SoCs, for that matter, given its shortages there) on models that won’t be sold immediately. Call it negative working inventory, and the pressure is on consumers to commit before their shipping date slips.

Regardless, I do think a Mac mini is a pretty ideal AI machine: I have one running in a rack with nothing but Codex and Claude Code; all of my interaction happens via the ChatGPT or Claude apps on my other computers and phone, which both limits the blast radius of the agents and also lets jobs run regardless of whether or not my regular computer is turned on and online; I also am basically always in sync. What that means in practical terms is that Apple just shipped the best consumer-grade AI computer ever. A Mac Studio with an M3 Ultra chip and 512GB RAM can run a 4-bit quantized version of DeepSeek R1 — a state-of-the-art open-source reasoning model — right on your desktop. It’s not perfect — quantization reduces precision, and the memory bandwidth is a bottleneck that limits performance — but this is something you simply can’t do with a standalone Nvidia chip, pro or consumer. The former can, of course, be interconnected, giving you superior performance, but that costs hundreds of thousands of dollars all-in; the only real alternative for home use would be a server CPU and gobs of RAM, but that’s even slower, and you have to put it together yourself.

The market has changed since then, particularly Nvidia’s introduction of the DGX Spark, a not-much-bigger-than-a-Mac-mini Blackwell machine with 128GB of unified memory; the biggest problem with the Spark, however, is that it had a paltry memory bandwidth of 273 GB/s, which resulted in relatively slow token generation. The next step up — other than gaming cards, which max out at 32 GB of VRAM — is the RTX Pro 6000 Blackwell card, which has doubled in price to $16,000 ( https://marketplace.nvidia.com/en-us/enterprise/laptops-workstations/nvidia-rtx-pro-6000-blackwell-workstation-edition/ ). That has 96GB of very high speed memory, for 1,792 GB/s of bandwidth, but you need a computer to put it in.

This is where the M5 Ultra is incredibly compelling. The memory bandwidth has been increased from 800 GB/s to 1,200 GB/s, the memory size is relatively huge, the power draw is less than half that of the RTX card by itself, and oh yeah, you get an entire computer! It really is an incredible local AI machine. Of course the whole computer part is its downside, too: you don’t get the CUDA stack, and Apple’s overall MLX pipeline is still pretty immature. Still, even at $14,299 (for the 256 GB RAM model with an 8TB SSD) this is honestly an incredible machine. Say what you will about Apple’s AI software; this is still the best hardware company in the world.

OpenAI Jalapeño
---------------

Meanwhile from OpenAI ( https://openai.com/index/jalapeno-first-results/ ) :

Since announcing Jalapeño, OpenAI’s first custom inference chip, we have been testing the chip and the system built around it. The results show a significant performance advance: Jalapeño can serve more AI work per unit of power while also returning responses more quickly. Jalapeño delivers both higher throughput and lower latency with one architecture, where existing hardware systems often have to make a tradeoff between the two…

OpenAI models also accelerated Jalapeño’s development. Earlier generations helped the team design and bring up the chip, while our latest models are accelerating how we optimize and program it. Jalapeño’s performance extends across GPT‑OSS 120B, DeepSeek R1, and Kimi K2.5 1T, showing that the architecture works across models developed both inside and outside OpenAI. Across all three, Jalapeño delivered 1.5 to 1.9 times more AI work per watt at peak throughput and 1.7 to 3.6 times lower end-to-end latency than the comparison systems. For highly interactive workloads, it delivered 2.1 to 4.1 times higher performance.

Jalapeño is also evidence of a broader full-stack advantage. OpenAI can design models, products, serving software, chips, memory, networking, and systems together, using what we learn from real workloads to improve every layer of the stack. Jalapeño is working first-party silicon with measured results, and it is the beginning of a multigenerational platform. In the months ahead, we will ramp Jalapeño to deliver faster, more capable, and more efficient products for our customers.

SemiAnalysis made the call in their headline: OpenAI Jalapeño: Better Than Nvidia Blackwell ( https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/openai-jalapeno-better-than-nvidia ) :

OpenAI has spent the past couple years quietly building “Jalapeño,” an inference chip just announced at Hot Chips. Rumors of a successful tapeout had been swirling for a while. But now we have details. OpenAI invited us to look at their chip, go to their labs to check out how real it is, and benchmark ( https://openai.com/index/jalapeno-first-results/ ) it with our InferenceX ( https://inferencex.semianalysis.com/ ) suite.

In June, OpenAI unveiled the chip program ( https://openai.com/index/openai-broadcom-jalapeno-inference-chip/ ) in partnership with Broadcom, built from a blank slate exclusively for LLM inference. Design work began in the middle of 2024 ( https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/openai-chip-team-is-now-serious ) , going from initial team hiring to manufacturing tape-out in ~16 months, an extremely fast ASIC development cycle. In general first generation chips are not competitive, but OpenAI bucks the trend by being industry leading and beating every Nvidia, AMD, and Google chip we have been able to test on multiple top open source models. OpenAI does this with extreme hardware software codesign. Surprisingly, OpenAI is not over specializing on any specific part of model inference, but instead by focusing on being a general chip that delivers high performance in all scenarios.

There are some important caveats, as SemiAnalysis notes, including:

* OpenAI ran the tests, not SemiAnalysis; the tests were also not necessarily representative of actual production workflows.
* Jalapeño is using HBM4, which makes sense given its brand new and not shipping in volume; therefore the better comparison is Vera Rubin, which also smashes Blackwell.
* Jalapeño won’t actually ship in volume until next year.

On the flipside, there are a lot of optimizations that Jalapeño has not yet implemented, so it will get considerably more performant, and the speed with which OpenAI has achieved these results — by deeply using AI — suggests they will get there.

I’m not going to break down Jalapeño any further than that — again, the SemiAnalysis link is here ( https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/openai-jalapeno-better-than-nvidia ) — other than to note this is another piece of the puzzle in terms of understanding Nvidia’s Risky Business ( https://stratechery.com/2026/nvidias-risky-business/?access_token=eyJhbGciOiJSUzI1NiIsImtpZCI6InN0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LnBhc3Nwb3J0Lm9ubGluZSIsInR5cCI6IkpXVCJ9.eyJhdWQiOiJzdHJhdGVjaGVyeS5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUiLCJhenAiOiJIS0xjUzREd1Nod1AyWURLYmZQV00xIiwiZW50Ijp7InVyaSI6WyJodHRwczovL3N0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LmNvbS8yMDI2L252aWRpYXMtcmlza3ktYnVzaW5lc3MvIl19LCJleHAiOjE3OTAzMzA1NjMsImlhdCI6MTc4NzczODU2MywiaXNzIjoiaHR0cHM6Ly9hcHAucGFzc3BvcnQub25saW5lL29hdXRoIiwic2NvcGUiOiJmZWVkOnJlYWQgYXJ0aWNsZTpyZWFkIGFzc2V0OnJlYWQgY2F0ZWdvcnk6cmVhZCBlbnRpdGxlbWVudHMiLCJzdWIiOiIwMTk1MTZmMS1hMzQ2LTc2MTQtOWU5Ni1iNjNiZDExZmU4NTYiLCJ1c2UiOiJhY2Nlc3MifQ.OYy284kl-LpY4VOvTIa9XFUldqT_bEztE86d9ZRQd6EjE6rLWH8Alt-VPCahEf3ph_IAxsPerbm9a3s-2oaWJ_pCmcQKwMHgG7LbmdJhmDkbmXp4GfiE-WwS4POl9bZzDWzcOa-ixsblDEz0txeO8mp8yb6Y-oJb91CJKtBHCeJU6So1s1e36G0hUr40aMYPF3AN1cpMohMUSzW6RvUZKk6NZQOe5sFqwNMix9pvkjYtrBEJvAV5uB1ZJonyWLzACJcJexK6B94nChP-uowNTtT4bHo3TkrB_qriVYAP3jqcvWtUBs5rJX0B0n4iY2jSWzytY5AnIHiS7S5WeVijvw ). For years Nvidia thrived by building one architecture in multiple configurations, differentiating it with software, but now it has focused rivals, from Apple to OpenAI, working diligently to peel off different parts of its market. In other words, Nvidia’s willingness to use its balance sheet to backstop customers who commit to its chips isn’t just a bid to get AI over the line in terms of paying for itself; it is very much an attempt to lock in Nvidia’s current advantages before the CUDA moat is gone completely and supply chains — Nvidia’s true advantage — stabilize for would-be challengers.
