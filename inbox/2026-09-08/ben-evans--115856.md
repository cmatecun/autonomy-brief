** AI’s step two
------------------------------------------------------------

One of the classic ways that you can think about the deployment of a ‘platform shift’, or indeed any important new technology, is that it follows three stages. (I’m a consultant - everything is three bullet points). First, we use it for the things we already do: we make the new tool fit our existing work, we automate the tasks we already know we have and we solve problems we can already see. In the enterprise, this generally means cost savings. Second, over time, we realise the new things that this makes possible - the things that could not have been imagined and perhaps that did not even look like problems. We move from bottom-line innovation to top-line innovation. Incumbents add new features, and startups unbundle incumbents. And third, sometimes, we have ‘disruption’ - someone works out how to change the question. Airbnb doesn’t sell software to hotels - it changes what ‘hotel’ means. And of course, this varies by industry: the internet disrupted travel agents but not airlines.

** GPT6, ‘AGI’ and Muse
------------------------------------------------------------

Open AI released its new foundation model, ‘Astra’, which puts it back ahead of Anthropic at the top of the benchmark tables. There’s some debate about how much of a jump this is, although clearly, compared to where we were a year ago everything is a huge jump. However, OpenAI’s cofounder Greg Brockman goes a lot further than that, saying that this model is now effectively ‘AGI’, which OpenAI defines as the ability to ‘outperform humans at most economically valuable work’. This seems like bubble thinking.

AGI started as a thought experiment, saying that it ought to be possible to make a machine that was intelligent as people in any meaningful sense, because, after all, a calculator is better than people at maths and a database is better at people than memory: we ought to be able make something that's actually as good as people in the sense that we, any normal person, would understand that. These models can do many more things better than people (or just faster, which is not the same thing), and they pass benchmarks for different kinds of problem, but that tells us that they can do some parts of what people do, which is not what people are and how what ‘general’ means. This tech may scale to that, and may not; we don’t know, but we do know we’re not there yet.

This all reminds me a bit of the old theologian's joke that the problem for Jews is that you wait and wait and wait for the Messiah and he never comes, whereas the problem for Christians is that he came and nothing changed. None of this is to deny the fact that we have a spectacularly important new technology that continues to get steadily better, and those people who still claiming that this is all some sort of a scam or doesn't really work are idiots. But equally, we are really really not at ‘this is actually like people’ yet. LINK (https://us.list-manage.com/17eGSHnmOdY?e=bc4ee99da9&c2id=e2956131aa985567d31359cb60722597)


** Muse on the frontier
------------------------------------------------------------

Indeed, I think a much more practically consequential release this week might be Meta’s Muse, which takes Meta back to the frontier. Meta and SpaceX/XAI were both on the leaderboards this time last year, dropped off spectacularly, but now, after billions of dollars of acquisitions and aquihires, are back up level with the leading foundation labs. As things stand, frontier models are clearly on a path to be commodities: they’re all building roughly the same thing in roughly the same way and getting roughly the same results, with no network effect or any other winner-takes-all effect that would allow one of them a structural advantage over the others. That is not a path that leads to pricing power or any other kind of value capture. LINK (https://us.list-manage.com/161gMTOXqWh?e=bc4ee99da9&c2id=e2956131aa985567d31359cb60722597)

** Google wins antitrust
------------------------------------------------------------

Last year Alphabet lost a US competition case over its model of paying companies to make Google the default: this week a judge issued remedies, and Gogoe won this stage. It will have to change a bunch of business practices, but the judge rejected all of the proposed break-up measures (none of which really made much sense).

The failure of the great neo-Brandeisian anti-tech antitrust push of the last decade is now pretty much complete, while the parallel EU effort has pretty clearly spiralled into irrelevance. This might be a moment for some humility and introspection from the people who shouted so loudly for it, but part of the reason for their failure, of course, was the sense that they’d never actually read any of the arguments against their position. In this case, as I wrote at the time of the verdict, there were two problems. First, search is a natural monopoly, and while one could argue having one winner limits innovation, it’s hard to see a lack of innovation at Google, and second, none of the break-ups proposed were likely to create more competition anyway.

If you really want to change all of this, you’ll need a coherent theory of harm, a legal approach that lines up with what the law actually says (or change the law!), and a theory of how the market and products work that doesn’t make people in the industry start laughing at you. Otherwise, you’ll get court cases you lose, remedies that change nothing (the US result), or laws that have no effect except annoying everyone (the EU result).

The challenge that interests me is that you could propose there’s a tech platform shift every 15 years. So if it then takes at least five years to become clear how it’s going to work and who’s going to win, another five years for it to be clear that people are abusing their monopoly, and then a third 5 years to bring the case through the process, then by the time the judge is handing out remedies, even if it’s not too late, the next thing is making it look irrelevant.
