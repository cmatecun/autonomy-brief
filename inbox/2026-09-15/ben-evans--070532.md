** AI naivety
------------------------------------------------------------

The underlying analytic problem with AGI or ASI (‘artificial super-intelligence’) is that we lack any good theory of how these models work, of how fast and how far they will scale, of what intelligence is, or of what AGI or ASI would actually be like. We don’t have any way to chart what these models will be like - we are all vibes-forecasting. This technology is clearly achieving capabilities and scaling in a way that make it foolish to say definitively that it can’t do at least some of the things people worry about, while, on the other hand, we can’t definitively say that it would. P.G. Wodehouse explained the problem very precisely in 1934:

“I was reading in the paper the other day about those birds who are trying to split the atom, the nub being that they haven’t the foggiest as to what will happen if they do. It may be all right. On the other hand, it may not be all right. And pretty silly a chap would feel, no doubt, if, having split the atom, he suddenly found the house going up in smoke and himself torn limb from limb.”

AI researchers today are in a similar and very tangled logical position. They’re trying really hard to build this stuff while also saying that they’re worried it might have really bad consequences, but then they go straight back to trying to build it even faster and trying to raise more money to carry on building it.

Anthropic’s head of safety said he thinks there’s a 10% chance of AI killing us all (which he’s said many times before.) ** AI does maths
------------------------------------------------------------

Much drama in AI and theoretical maths this week: Open AI published a paper claiming to have solved a long-standing problem (which has a $1 million prize attached to it), but another pair of mathematicians claimed that they’d been working on this problem using AI, that OpenAI had got wind of this, and immediately thrown millions of dollars of compute and a research team at the problem to try to scoop them. They further claim that OpenAI tried to bully them into giving up credit, and may also have looked at their work on its systems.

It’s not particularly clear exactly what happened, but 25 Fields Medallists signed an open letter expressing broader concerns. First, theoretical mathematics of this kind is not an urgent matter of life and death with immediate utilitarian consequences. A cancer researcher who says they don’t want AI to find the cure this year, because they would rather find it themselves even if it takes longer, is putting their own ego ahead of other people’s lives. But in mathematics, things are a bit more complicated. Narrowly, science thrives on open exchange of ideas, but if you say that you have an intuition that AI could be used to solve this and, immediately, someone with access to far more compute than you just goes out and does it, then the incentive is for people no longer to discuss their ideas. Further, for many of these problems, the value is not so much in a yes/no answer as in the new patterns of reasoning and ideas that might be used to get there, and having a machine brute-force the
answer means that less might be learnt than if people had worked it out (I can’t take a view on this myself: my last exposure to mathematics was at university, when a law professor introduced a maths professor to me as “This is Dr. Smith. He does sums.”) COVERAGE (https://us.list-manage.com/P07c_qXiF23?e=bc4ee99da9&c2id=e2956131aa985567d31359cb60722597) , OPEN LETTER (https://us.list-manage.com/2LbLcAaMC3R?e=bc4ee99da9&c2id=e2956131aa985567d31359cb60722597)


** Meta tries consumer agents
------------------------------------------------------------

Meta launched Muse, a new stand-alone brand and app for customer AI agents, which can send emails and take action online, such as booking trips or making purchases. This is necessarily experimental - no-one really knows what will work best nor the best way to drive consumer adoption, but there’s a lot of thoughtful work here, and it looks like Meta will try to apply the same growth engine it’s used to take Threads to 500m MAUs. It’s also free for a ‘generous’ amount of weekly use, with $20 and $100 monthly plans - marginal cost is real for AI. ** In other news
------------------------------------------------------------

Elon Musk’s tunnelling company ‘Boring’ has a new contract in Dubai and raised $3bn more funding. Reminder: Boring uses standard tunnelling tech, but digs smaller tunnels to save money (with a few other cost-saving shortcuts) and then runs Tesla cars through them instead of trains, which is much less efficient. Engineering is trade-offs, but fund-raising is story-telling. LINK (https://us.list-manage.com/1CSRGu9vTpK?e=bc4ee99da9&c2id=e2956131aa985567d31359cb60722597)

The UK air traffic control system failed on Tuesday (my flight from JFK to Heathrow was diverted to Edinburgh): the FT reports that the cause was a malformed flight plan filed by the Royal Air Force. The underlying tech platform dates back to the 1960s: an upgrade has been in discussions since the 1990s and is currently envisaged for the 2030s. Memo to Silicon Valley accelerationists - the real world is a lot more complex and a lot harder to change than you think. LINK (https://us.list-manage.com/NTMgx7zP2gO?e=bc4ee99da9&c2id=e2956131aa985567d31359cb60722597)

Google removed a range of vertical search features in its search products in the EU to comply with local competition rules, pointing out as it did so that this ipso facto makes the products worse for consumers, while it also transfers value from hotels, restaurants and airlines to an oligopoly of third-party booking services, since Google is no longer allowed to play that role. Policy is about trade-offs and competition isn’t simple. LINK (https://us.list-manage.com/YfdTwhDhiL2?e=bc4ee99da9&c2id=e2956131aa985567d31359cb60722597)


** Folding iPhones
------------------------------------------------------------

Apple’s new CEO John Turnus launched new iPhones this week, and in a massive surprise, they have better chips and cameras, and may even be ‘the best iPhones we’ve ever made’. It also launched its entry into the (high-end, $2000) folding phone segment with the ‘Duo’. In typical Apple fashion, this is not the ‘first’ (both Steve Jobs and Tim Cook repeatedly said that Apple rarely aims for that, pointing out that the iPod, iPhone and iPad were not ‘first’) but aims to do it right, and there are a lot of nice touches and new thinking, from clever UI ideas to dust-proofing (dust in the hinge has been a real issue in this segment). It’s interesting as something kind of between an iPhone and an iPad, again probing at what ‘computer’ means. But also… this is the last platform, not the next one.
