High Interest Rate Phenomenon
The yield on the 10-year Treasury note broke 5%, not a great indicator of economic health. Bad for the US government, bad for the prospective American homebuyer, it’s even bad for AI companies with ungodly amounts of money who need ever more to finance the buildout of massive super computers. So headline reason is a war with no end in sight, you don’t exactly need a PhD in statistics to see the correlation here:
War doesn’t always cause interest rates to spike, the underlying issues here are energy prices and inflation. Energy, specifically oil transported through the Strait of Hormuz, is a key pressure point for control of the conflict, and that drives up inflation, which hurts the value of Treasury bonds, causing yields to spike.
It doesn’t always go this way. During Gulf War 1, there was a textbook oil shock. Iraq invaded Kuwait on August 2nd of 1990. The U.S. military buildup began basically immediately, with the Desert Storm offensive starting January 17th, 1991. Interest rates spiked from 8.29% to 9.05% in less than a month, but 6 months later, they were lower than the start of the war, sitting at 8.03%.
The Afghanistan war played out in reverse. Immediately before the invasion, the 10-year was 4.52%, and yields fell to 4.22% in the first month of the offensive. But 6 months into the war, the 10-year hit 5.25%, up significantly from the start of the offensive.
So now Warsh is in a tough spot, the market expects a rate hike to help bring inflation back to target. August consumer-price inflation was 3.4% year over year. The Fed’s preferred inflation measure, the PCE price index, was 3.7% in July, or 3.3% excluding food and energy. Inflation is clearly spilling over from energy into other parts of the economy and it’s complicating the Fed’s case for aggressive easing.
AI is also part of the problem, but could be a piece of the solution. Today, AI creates investment demand before productivity benefits arrive. Data centers require financing, construction capacity, equipment and electricity. That spending happens today, but broader productivity benefits take longer to diffuse. Economists describe AI as a source of upward pressure on real rates (and potentially prices) during the compute buildout. There’s also an interesting indirect effect where the AI-related stock-market boom supports consumer spending (people who make money on SanDisk spend that money in the real economy).
