Amazon Earnings
---------------

It was, needless to say, a very busy November; so busy, in fact, that I am only just now getting to Amazon’s earnings, which dropped at the end of October. From Bloomberg ( https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-29/amazon-spending-soars-on-effort-to-meet-demand-for-ai-services ) :

Amazon.com Inc. shares dropped after the company reported greater capital spending than anticipated and the slowest growth in the core e-commerce business in three years. Amazon said it spent more than $77 billion on property and equipment in the third quarter, far more than analysts’ estimates, while pursuing its effort to build data centers and fulfill demand for artificial intelligence services. Third-quarter sales were lower than anticipated, rising 12% to $201.9 billion.

Revenue at the cloud unit, Amazon Web Services, accelerated for the sixth straight quarter. Online sales gained just 2% to $68.5 billion, the lowest increase since early 2023, when Amazon was still working through the post-pandemic slump in online retail. Advertising sales, a business closely tied to the e-commerce franchise, rose 11%, the slowest rate since Amazon began breaking out such results in 2020. The shares fell 4.4% to $233.10 as the markets opened in New York on Friday. The stock is up about 1% so far this year.

CEO Andy Jassy did not reassure investors on the earnings call ( https://seekingalpha.com/article/4959804-amazon-com-inc-amzn-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript ) about Amazon’s spending; in fact, he doubled down:

On the CapEx involved to grow the way we’re talking about, it’s meaningful. And I think that this — the way we think about it, Doug, is that this is a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity. I think what we’re seeing with AI, it’s the biggest transformation in our lifetime. And if the idea of being able to grow 39% with $184 billion annual run rate and to grow 40% a year on top of that in each of the next 2 years and ultimately end up as $1 trillion-plus business, we’re going to invest to continue being the leader.

We are the leader today in the, I’ll call it, the infrastructure and AI space. And would it be more convenient if this type of acceleration and opportunity happened over 25 years and you could spread the CapEx over 25 years? Yes, it might be more convenient, but that’s not the world that we live in, and that’s not the opportunity. And we can fund this plan. And I think it will forever change the size of Amazon. It will forever change our revenue. It will forever change our free cash flow. And we’re going to invest and we’re going to pursue it to continue to be the significant leader like we are today.

The part that I thought was notable — and very much in line with a lot of the analysis I’ve been doing on Stratechery this year ( https://stratechery.com/2026/whos-afraid-of-chinese-models/?access_token=eyJhbGciOiJFUzI1NiIsImtpZCI6IkxzanFRcEhoMUJUdmU0djJQUUVuejIiLCJ0eXAiOiJKV1QifQ.eyJhdWQiOiJzdHJhdGVjaGVyeS5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUiLCJhenAiOiJIS0xjUzREd1Nod1AyWURLYmZQV00xIiwiZW50Ijp7InVyaSI6WyJodHRwczovL3N0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LmNvbS8yMDI2L3dob3MtYWZyYWlkLW9mLWNoaW5lc2UtbW9kZWxzLyJdfSwiZXhwIjoxNzk4MDIzNzMyLCJpYXQiOjE3OTU0MzE3MzIsImlzcyI6Imh0dHBzOi8vYXBwLnBhc3Nwb3J0Lm9ubGluZS9vYXV0aCIsInNjb3BlIjoiZmVlZDpyZWFkIGFydGljbGU6cmVhZCBhc3NldDpyZWFkIGNhdGVnb3J5OnJlYWQgZW50aXRsZW1lbnRzIiwic3ViIjoiMDE5NTE2ZjEtYTM0Ni03NjE0LTllOTYtYjYzYmQxMWZlODU2IiwidXNlIjoiYWNjZXNzIn0.-4goDIjBTIMS1VSfSXA129mt9oCqGldNIoKgANIfhlrFgBbUBOb9CrOSDOKid2cLCjCaHHAHChVgY-1Ia6eB9A ) — was Jassy’s implicit assumption that AI compute is on its way to being a commodity business; this was in response to a question about workloads running on Bedrock, AWS’s managed inference service:

Well, you’re right that the majority and most of Bedrock today is being run on top of Trainium. And we do that in meaningful part because we really like the price performance that we’re getting, and it allows us to both keep prices as low as possible for customers as well as we like the margins associated with it.

If you think about margins in the cloud space and you’re looking at different players and what their cost structure looks like, there are a few things to pay attention to. The first is for their data centers, how many of them are they actually buying or long leasing versus doing colo or neoclouds. I mean neoclouds are about 3,500 basis points worse in operating margin than if you long lease them. And then you look at what is the hardware on top of which they’re running, and if you’re using the hardware that everybody else is using, you’ll have a comparable cost structure to what everybody is doing. And if you have the ability to use hardware that’s different, you’ll have an advantage.

And so I think when you look at what we’re doing, most of our capacity is either owned or long leased which enjoys better operating margins than what you’ll find when people are mostly doing colo or neo or a big chunk doing colo or neo. And the fact that we’re running on top of our own chip that’s providing both advantaged price performance and it’s more cost effective for ourselves because we own it, I think, gives us some structural cost advantages that I think are going to be very helpful to customers as we pass them back to customers and helpful to the overall economics of AWS.

This is the bull case not for a time when compute is scarce, but for when compute is abundant, which, incidentally, is exactly what investors are freaking out about right now. In a commodity market price is not based on differentiation but rather marginal willingness to pay: how much is the last entity willing to pay for a unit of compute? And, if we were to enter a scenario where there simply aren’t enough entities for all of the compute that is available, then the price will fall to the marginal cost of the compute that clears the market; everyone whose marginal cost exceeds that will simply not sell. Everyone who can’t make enough operating profit to pay for their debt load will go bankrupt.

However, that doesn’t mean there won’t be real money to be made in such a market; profit will be a function of having a superior cost structure, which is exactly what Jassy is claiming AWS will have. Moreover, the entities with the superior cost structures will be the last to lose demand, precisely because they will have the most capacity to handle lower prices (indeed, they will attract it, because they will be safe to build on).

Meanwhile, Jassy continued to make the case — reasonably, I think — that inference benefits AWS broadly:

We have a compelling flywheel spinning in AWS. Borrowing from Jim Collins’ flywheel definition Good to Great, where if you do A, you almost can’t help but do B and if you do B, you almost can’t help but do C and so on. In AWS, having the leading functionality, security and operational performance can’t help but drive customers to run their apps and store their data in AWS, which can’t help but drive AWS AI demand because customers prefer their production inference to be near the rest of their data and apps, which can’t help but drive core AWS demand because AI uses core for work like reinforcement learning, posttraining and tool usage, which can’t help but drive reinvestment back into our broader AWS functionality.

This is a different model than primarily hosting GPUs or renting short-term data center capacity. This is a long-term platform strategy that broadly helps customers invent and evolve their customer experiences, operate stably and build a reliable partnership they can grow for many years. And it also yields very attractive long-term economics for us regardless of supply scarcity.

Or, per the point above, regardless of supply abundance! In short, Jassy’s overarching point seemed to be, “We are not the neoclouds”, and he’s right!

The Chip Question
-----------------

Jassy confirmed that Amazon will be selling Trainium chips:

Customers using our custom silicon saved more than $2 billion in Q3 alone, and we just open-sourced TorchNeuron so developers can run existing PyTorch code, which most already use on Trainium without rewriting a thing. As we launch new generations of chips, demand for prior generations remained strong. After our launch of Graviton5, backlog for prior generation Graviton instances still more than doubled quarter-over-quarter. And short-term capacity for prior generation Trainium instances sell at a premium compared to the first half of the year. With Trainium demand so strong, customers continue encouraging us to make it available outside of AWS. We intend to do so late in 2027. Overall demand for AWS capacity remains exceptionally strong.

An analyst reasonably asked why, if inference demand is so strong, AWS wouldn’t just keep Trainium for itself; Jassy’s answer wasn’t very convincing:

On the balance of chips and what you sell externally versus what you use inside your cloud, it’s a very good question. And it’s a question that we’re continuing to evaluate as we speak. I would say that the number of companies who have a significant cloud business and a chip strong enough to sell externally is effectively two. So there really aren’t many customers that have to grapple with the same type of problem at this type of scale.

And at this point, everybody is producing their advanced chips with the same provider. There’s only so many chips that provider can provide each year. Everybody gets an allocation. And you have to decide with that allocation how much of it you want to sell externally and how much of it you want to use in your cloud. And there’s a trade-off. If you sell externally, you’re able to realize more revenue in the short term because you get to recognize the revenue as soon as it leaves your inventory. And on the flip side, if you decide to use it inside your cloud in the form of EC2 instances or Bedrock, you actually will end up driving more absolute revenue and free cash flow from those chips because you get to use them over 5 to 6 years and drive revenue for a longer period of time. So you have to really think through what you want to do.

To date, I would say that we’ve taken a different approach in how we’ve used our chips than the other provider who has the same type of balancing act going on. And as I mentioned earlier, we believe we’re going to have a very significant business selling chips externally, and we’re going to start in late 2027. And we will be thinking through that balance, I think, probably every year. But they’ll both be very significant. Our ability to create price performance, scalable chips is going to be a very significant strategic advantage for us at scale, both in our own cost structure as well as building a separate business and selling external chips and selling external chips really is a very different business than your cloud infrastructure business.

I admit, posting that entire answer was kind of a waste of time, but it’s always funny when executives clearly don’t want to answer a specific question. And some of the points were accurate! Only Amazon and Google make their own LLM accelerators at scale, and both are dependent on TSMC in the end, and have to make hard allocation decisions. And, critically, both have to make the same allocation decision, which, I strongly suspect, is “Do we want to keep Anthropic as a customer?”.

Anthropic and OpenAI are the two companies with sufficient demand to have leverage over Amazon (and Google), and, in the long run, they don’t want to pay Amazon and Google inference margins; they also are not (yet) at a point where they can make a competitive accelerator, and obviously they don’t particularly want to pay Nvidia’s margins either. Amazon and Google (and Nvidia), meanwhile, definitely don’t want Anthropic building their own chips (which they are definitely trying to do ( https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/anthropic-to-build-inhouse-chip-design-team-for-claude-hire-engineers-4838219 ) ), so both are signaling the willingness to sacrifice some amount of internal demand in order to keep Anthropic hooked on their chips, even if the margins are ultimately lower than hosting Anthropic demand in their own data centers would generate.

About Agents
------------

Here is what Jassy had to say about agents:

Over 400 million customers have used Alexa For shopping, our agentic shopping assistant, in the last 12 months with customer interactions up 570% year-over-year. U.S. customers using Alexa for Shopping are spending 50% more per order than those who don’t. It researches products, compares options, checks a full year of price history, gets personalized gift recommendations, and tracks deals and price drops on customers’ behalf. Though we anticipate working with horizontal agents, we believe Alexa for Shopping is going to be the primary shopping agent customers use to shop Amazon.

Having a shopping assistant constantly by your side as you browse, get inspired, have questions on product detail pages and that you can simply ask to buy something based on your prior purchases that already has your history, your shipping address and payment info is uniquely helpful and convenient for customers.

Then, during the ad discussion:

A natural question is whether agentic shopping from external horizontal agents is meaningfully impacting Amazon Ads. And the answer is no. Few customers are doing much shopping this way yet, as the experience is still not great nor more convenient than coming to a retailer directly to research or one-click order there.

I’m going to write more about this soon, but Jassy’s perspective assumes that intent starts with the retailer: “I’m going to buy on Amazon, now let me research.” That may be the case for some use cases, in which case Alexa for Shopping is basically better search; buying something, however, is sometimes not the job to be done, but rather a necessary input to the job to be done. If that job starts in another agent, then clearly that other agent is going to be where people would prefer to do their research, and Amazon’s approach will just be annoying! I am, to be clear, speaking from personal experience (and yet, I must admit, I’m buying more than ever from Amazon). More soon.
