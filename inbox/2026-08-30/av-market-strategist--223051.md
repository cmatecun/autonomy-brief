Waymo Comes to Munich, Takes Aim at Tesla and Buys a Curb in Vegas
On Tuesday, Waymo announced it is laying the groundwork for a fully autonomous ride-hailing service in Munich. The sequence is the same one it has run in all eleven cities where it operates commercially today. First, vehicles drive manually across the city to build high-definition maps of the street network. Then testing with trained autonomous specialists behind the wheel. Then the specialists come out, and employees, media and invited guests ride before anyone else. Then limited public service, then open service, each step gated on approvals.
Waymo says it is working with the Federal Motor Transport Authority (KBA), state authorities and local officials, and expects to open commercial ride-hailing to the public towards the end of 2027.
The press release is thin on specifics, but the FAQ on Waymo’s German landing page is not, and it is where the actual operational commitment sits.
The footprint is much larger than the announcement suggests
Waymo names 17 Munich districts it will drive in, and describes the list as “including but not limited to”: Altstadt-Lehel, Ludwigsvorstadt-Isarvorstadt, Maxvorstadt, Schwabing-West, Au-Haidhausen, Sendling-Westpark, Schwanthalerhöhe, Neuhausen-Nymphenburg, Moosach, Schwabing-Freimann, Bogenhausen, Berg am Laim, Ramersdorf-Perlach, Untergiesing-Harlaching, Thalkirchen-Obersendling-Forstenried-Fürstenried-Solln, Hadern and Laim.
Munich has 25 city districts. Waymo has named 17 of them, and left the list open. That is not a pilot zone. It covers the historic centre, nearly all of the inner ring, and a substantial share of the outer residential belt, which is a materially more ambitious footprint than the phrasing of the announcement implies. Testing hours run 24 hours a day, seven days a week. 
The legal basis is stated plainly, which is useful because it removes any ambiguity about what Waymo thinks it is doing. Testing runs “under the guidelines set by the Federal Motor Transport Authority (KBA) and in accordance with the German Road Traffic Act (StVG), Autonomous Driving Act (AFGBV), and of course all other applicable laws.” On the commercial date, the FAQ says Waymo is “collaborating closely with authorities to secure the required permits.”
So the company’s own account confirms the position in the register below. It does not have them yet.
Two smaller details worth having. Waymo says Munich will not be its first winter-weather city, pointing to US markets that get annual snow, which pre-empts the obvious European objection. And on adapting the Waymo Driver, it names something more specific than the usual language about local conditions: teaching the system to read signage based on the 1968 Vienna Convention on Road Signs and Signals, along with local lane discipline and right-of-way rules for pedestrians and cyclists. The United States is not a party to the Vienna Convention and runs its own signage standard, so this is a genuine perception and rules re-learning task.
What Waymo still has not disclosed: fleet size, vehicle platform, and any date for removing the validation drivers.
The data question, and the part of it Waymo did not answer
Four of the ten FAQ entries deal with privacy and data. That ratio tells you what Waymo expects to be asked in Germany, and it is the right expectation.
The commitments are specific. Sensors are described as privacy-by-design. No facial recognition and no biometric identification. No use of the data for advertising, and no sharing with third parties. Full GDPR compliance claimed. German residents get a dedicated portal to exercise data protection rights over personal data potentially captured by the external cameras.
Then comes the question Waymo chose to put on its own page, and it is worth quoting in full because the answer is the interesting part:
“Waymo is a subsidiary of Alphabet, Google’s parent company. Can you guarantee that data collected on German roads will not be accessible to Google or US government authorities?“
The answer addresses Google thoroughly. Waymo is a separate company within Alphabet, operates its own data infrastructure, and will not share personal information with Google except with consent, in connection with providing services to Waymo, or as otherwise disclosed.
It does not address US government authorities at all.
Source: Waymo
I want to be fair about what that does and does not mean. No US company can straightforwardly guarantee otherwise, given the extraterritorial reach of US law over US-controlled data, so the honest answer would be uncomfortable to write. Waymo raised the question itself rather than waiting to be asked, which is more than most would do. But having raised it, it answered only half of it.
This is the thread we picked up in CW27, when Waymo registered four European entities in two weeks and the Dutch B.V. stood out because its stated purpose was computing infrastructure and data processing rather than robotaxi operations. We read that as the EU data-residency layer being built as corporate structure. The German FAQ is the customer-facing half of the same problem. 
The permit position, and what the register actually says
As in CW32, I parsed the KBA register again and pulled the current file this week, dated 18 August 2026.
251 entries. 156 Level 3, 95 Level 4. Filtering on the expiry column, because listed is not the same as live, 181 permits are still current. Of those, 70 are Level 4 with no geographic restriction, meaning they are valid across the whole Federal Republic, subject to the vehicle’s own capabilities.
Those 70 nationwide Level 4 permits are held by ten organisations:
Volkswagen holds 43 of 70, which is 61.4% of all unrestricted Level 4 authority in Germany. In CW32 that share was 69%, so the concentration is falling as new holders arrive, but one automaker still holds the majority of the country’s driverless testing scope.
Three things in that table matter more than the headline.
Waymo holds zero German permits. So does Nuro. Neither appears anywhere in the register, at any level, current or expired.
Mobileye’s permits have all lapsed. It holds four Level 4 permits, all geographically restricted, and every one has expired, three on 2 May 2026 and one on 8 July 2026. 
Stadtwerke München took three nationwide Level 4 permits on 14 August, two weeks before Waymo’s announcement. Munich’s municipal utility now holds more live unrestricted Level 4 authority in Germany than any foreign robotaxi developer operating in the city.
And the one that reframes the story: Momenta Europe GmbH holds one nationwide Level 4 permit, granted 8 July 2026 and running to July 2028. A Chinese full-stack developer is the only foreign robotaxi company on that list. Waymo is not on it yet.
Munich is filling up, and not with German companies
Waymo is the fourth serious stack to plant something in Munich inside twelve months.
Uber and Momenta confirmed Munich as the first continental European city for their robotaxi programme, initially with safety operators on board. Momenta has been testing since 2018, and its ADAS is already installed on 400,000 customer vehicles, including programmes with Mercedes-Benz, BMW and Audi. It is also, per the register above, the only foreign developer holding nationwide German Level 4.
Uber and Autobrains announced a Munich robotaxi programme built on NVIDIA DRIVE Hyperion, pitched as OEM-agnostic so it can scale across vehicle platforms rather than depending on one custom build. Autobrains decomposes driving into specialised agents rather than running one monolithic end-to-end model, holds more than 300 patents, and told us in EP18 of Autonomy Insiders [ https://substack.com/redirect/8787f955-338c-4a9f-92d8-f85ded0a7e6f?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ] that Europe is harder than the US and therefore the right place to prove a system. Worth noting against the robotaxi framing: in the register, Autobrains holds three Level 3 permits, all geographically restricted, expiring at the end of 2027.
Nuro established its first European presence in the Munich area in May, as the hub for engineering, operations and partner engagement. Nuro’s pitch is a universal Level 4 platform spanning personally owned vehicles, robotaxis and logistics, and it points to a zero-shot autonomy demonstration in Tokyo, operating in a new environment without prior training on local driving data, as evidence the approach travels.
Four stacks. American, Chinese, Israeli, and American again. That is the part the German government has noticed.
Christian Hirte, Parliamentary State Secretary at the Federal Ministry of Transport, used Waymo’s own press release to make the point:
“Waymo, the world’s leading provider of commercial robotaxi services, has now recognised Germany’s potential as a location offering legal certainty... We must now leverage the strengths of our economy and develop autonomous mobility solutions from within Europe. Our goal must be to ensure that we in Europe not only use these technologies but also develop and operate them here.“
Read that carefully. A German state secretary is celebrating an American company’s arrival and, in the same paragraph, arguing that Europe needs to stop being the place where other people’s autonomy gets deployed. Both halves are true, and the tension between them is the European position in one quote. Bavaria’s Florian Herrmann was more direct about what happens next: “Now we need to move quickly from testing to real-world deployment... cut red tape and remove regulatory barriers.”
There are two ways to read Munich. Read one, this is a hub forming: four stacks, the first-mover legal framework in Europe, deep engineering talent, political backing at state and federal level, and a municipal utility that has just taken nationwide Level 4 for itself. Read two, every stack named is foreign, the German contribution is the legal framework and the streets, and Hirte’s quote is a request for something that is not currently happening. I lean toward the second as a description of today and the first as a genuine possibility, because the AFGBV is a real asset. I hold that loosely. The binding variable is whether German capital follows German regulation, and so far it has not.
In a market that will launch before Munich, Waymo got a curb
While Munich is a 2027 story at the earliest, Las Vegas is a right-now story, and Waymo made a second move there this week.
Waymo announced a multi-year partnership with The Venetian Resort Las Vegas, naming it The Venetian’s first-ever official ride-hailing partner, covering arrival and departure at both The Venetian and The Palazzo. Waymo frames it around the guest journey: multi-language support, custom cabin controls, a consistent ride every time.
Note what the announcement does and does not say. It is a naming right and an operational integration at two connected properties. Waymo does not claim exclusivity anywhere in the post, and I am not going to assume it, because “first-ever official partner” and “only permitted operator” are very different commercial instruments.
But the direction is the thing. In CW34, covering Waymo’s Allegiant Stadium deal, we wrote:
“Allegiant Stadium is private property. So is the Sphere, where Zoox has the equivalent relationship. So is every casino in Las Vegas, each of which requires its own agreement before a vehicle can pick up on its land. The city is about to carry the deepest bench of autonomous operators in America, competing for a finite set of venue agreements.”
The Venetian is a casino. Two weeks later, the venue land-grab is underway, and Waymo has now acquired private access rights twice in a month, at a stadium and at a resort, in a market where it is not yet carrying public passengers.
The reason it is happening now is a supply and demand mismatch that has nothing to do with technology. As we covered in CW35, Nevada regulators approved roughly 7,100 robotaxis for Clark County in a single meeting: Tesla up to 5,000, Waymo 1,000, Uber 1,000 through Motional and Zoox, on top of Zoox’s existing 100. 
So four operators are provisioned for fleets that do not exist yet, in a city where every meaningful pickup point sits on private land and requires a separate negotiation. The permits are abundant. The vehicles are not. The curbs are finite. That is precisely the condition under which you sign venue deals early, and it is why this is a competitive move rather than a marketing one.
Waymo was not only expanding this week. It was also taking shots at Tesla.
On the same Tuesday, Waymo’s head of AI foundations, Srikanth Thirumalai, published a list of “10 AI lessons” drawn from more than 200 million fully autonomous miles. Tesla is not named anywhere in it. It does not need to be.
Lesson 10 is the sharpest:
“Simply improving a driver-assist system (L2) for full autonomy is a false summit. True L4 maturity can only be safely achieved by a purpose-built system, validated on closed courses and hardened by the uncompromising experience of driving without a human in the car.”
The argument underneath it is that neither billions of simulated miles nor millions of supervised miles with a human present can teach a system what it learns once there is no human backup at all. That is a direct description of Tesla’s method, which trains FSD on data from customer cars whose drivers remain legally responsible for the vehicle.
Lesson 1 states that cameras alone cannot deliver safe, full-scale autonomy, and that Waymo combines cameras, lidar and radar for redundancy. Tesla runs camera-only.
Lesson 4 warns against pure end-to-end neural networks converting raw camera input directly into steering commands: “You can’t build trust with a black box.” Tesla has spent two years selling exactly that architecture as the core of FSD.
Three of ten lessons, three specific Tesla decisions.
The confidence has numbers behind it. Waymo is serving more than 500,000 paid, fully driverless rides every week and targeting one million by year end. Tesla’s unsupervised fleet is estimated at 20 to 30 vehicles at any given moment across all markets, and the company confirmed 380,000 cumulative unsupervised miles as of its July earnings call. Waymo has logged over 220 million rider-only miles. Tesla’s entire cumulative unsupervised total is under 0.2% of Waymo’s, a gap of roughly 580x.
Waymo is not alone on this, and that is the part worth pausing on
The false-summit argument is not a Waymo idiosyncrasy. It is now the stated position of three of the leading global Level 4 operators.
Tony Han, CEO of WeRide, made the same case on our podcast in EP24 of Autonomy Insiders [ https://substack.com/redirect/09fc2bbd-8347-4176-acf2-f07b7f921d34?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ], and gave it the cleanest conceptual framing I have heard. Going from L4 down to L2 is a projection, like a four-dimensional creature describing a two-dimensional world. Going from L2 up to L4 is a lifting, like a three-dimensional creature imagining a fourth dimension. Both are hard. Lifting is the harder operation.
Lou Tiancheng, CTO of Pony.ai, has repeatedly argued that L2 and L4 are not two points on one path but fundamentally different problems.
All three of them are somehow talking their own books. Waymo, WeRide and Pony.ai are L4-first companies, and “the thing we did is the only thing that works” is exactly what you would expect them to say. That caveat is real and I am not going to bury it.
But the track record is also real. All three have removed the driver and run paid public service. And Han did something the others have not, which is to attach a falsifiable test to the claim. His bar for an ADAS company asserting a robotaxi path: at least 100 vehicles, available to the public, for at least 180 days, with no severe accidents and no suspension by a legal authority. 
Apply that yardstick to Tesla today and it fails on the first criterion. The unsupervised fleet is 20 to 30 vehicles, not 100. It does not get to the 180-day question or the incident question.
None of that settles whether Tesla eventually gets there. It simply locates Tesla against a public, checkable standard set by a competitor, and the useful property of that standard is that anyone can run it themselves next quarter and get a different answer if the facts change.
But is the timing an accident?
It might be. Waymo publishes research and safety material routinely, the post came from its head of AI foundations, and 200 million driverless miles is a natural milestone to write about.
Or it might have something to do with what happens next week.
On 22 August, Tesla confirmed a Cybercab event in Austin on 3 September, promoted with a poster reading “Exclusive Access: Cybercab.” It is invite-only, going to the top scorers in its Robotaxi rider sweepstakes plus mail-in entrants, winners announced 26 August, RSVP deadline 30 August, attendees 21 and over, livestreamed for everyone else.
Waymo published its lessons five days after that poster went up.
Why would Waymo care about a two-seater?
Because if the Cybercab economics land, they are a different sport.
Tesla’s Q2 release cites more than 125,000 units of installed annual capacity at Giga Texas, where pilot production began in February. Analysts forecast Tesla’s long-term bill of materials for the Cybercab at $25,000 to $30,000, against $65,000 to $100,000 for Waymo’s sixth-generation hardware and $60,000 to $70,000 all-in for typical US AV hardware.
Source: Platform Aeronaut  
Convert that into what it means. If Tesla builds a robotaxi for a third of what the leader pays, and can build 125,000 of them a year, then every mile Waymo drives carries a structural cost disadvantage that no amount of operational excellence closes. The cost curve becomes the moat. That is the bull case, and it is a serious one.
The Cybercab itself is genuinely impressive as an efficiency exercise. Tesla rates it at about 165 Wh per mile, the most efficient vehicle it has ever built, at 3,113 lbs with a 48 kWh battery, against 250 to 280 Wh per mile for a Model Y. For an operator running its own fleet, energy cost per mile is a real line item.
It is also a capacity downgrade. The Cybercab seats two, with no steering wheel, no pedals and minimal cargo space. The Model Y it replaces seats five. For anyone travelling with luggage, a stroller, a wheelchair or two other people, it is not an upgrade.
Why the cost curve is not the constraint yet
Until recently the Cybercab looked distant, and Tesla is the reason it looked that way.
Musk himself retired the decade-old fleet-data thesis by acknowledging Tesla must accumulate driving data specific to the Cybercab before scaling it, and Cybercabs have been retrofitted with steering wheels and pedals for calibration. 
Then there are the crashes. Tesla has filed 22 unique ADS incident reports with NHTSA, and three of them were caused by a human operating the vehicle remotely.
Be precise about what this does and does not show. Tesla does not disclose how often remote operators intervene, so no intervention-failure rate can be computed, and a sample drawn from crash reports is selected for crashes by construction. What can be said is that every remote intervention that has surfaced in a crash report ended in a collision, and that Houston had three active vehicles at the time.
The contrast is what makes it analytical rather than a gotcha. In a February 2026 letter to Senator Ed Markey, Waymo stated it “has not used remote driving or tele-operations where a human performs the Dynamic Driving Task.” Its remote agents advise; the Waymo Driver can accept or reject. Waymo does have one narrow recovery tool, and it describes almost exactly the Houston scenario: a trained agent can prompt a stuck vehicle forward at 2 mph at fixed steering angles to exit a travel lane. Waymo says that function has never been used outside of training.
Both companies built a tool for the same problem. One has never taken it onto a public road.
I do not think the timing of the AI lessons is a coincidence.
Waymo published the AI lessons five days after a rival’s launch poster, with three of ten lessons mapping onto three specific decisions that rival has made. That is a company shaping the interpretive frame before an event it cannot control, and doing it on the one axis where its lead is unambiguous: driverless miles actually driven.
But I could also be wrong.
What this means and what to watch
First, Waymo’s end-2027 Munich date forces an answer to the type approval question.  Waymo has now attached a date to the question. Commercial public ride-hailing is not a testing permission. Either Waymo becomes one of the first entities anywhere to complete a full European Level 4 type approval, or the date slips. There is no third path, and as Dirk Fratzke of TÜV SÜD told us in EP20 of Autonomy Insiders, no type-approved Level 4 vehicle exists anywhere in the world today.
Second, the data question is the one that could actually move the 2027 date, and almost nobody is pricing it. Waymo put “can you guarantee data will not be accessible to Google or US government authorities” on its own FAQ and answered only the Google half. German data protection authorities are among the most assertive in Europe, and as EP23 of Autonomy Insiders established, EU data residency in AV operations is treated as non-negotiable rather than negotiable. Watch whether Waymo routes German operational data through the Dutch entity it registered in June, and whether any German authority puts the US-access question formally. 
Third, the Las Vegas venue land-grab is now the leading indicator. Waymo has taken Allegiant Stadium and The Venetian in a month, Zoox holds the Sphere, and four operators are permitted for roughly 7,100 vehicles. Watch which properties sign next and whether any agreement is disclosed as exclusive rather than “official.” The first genuine exclusivity clause on a major Strip property will tell you more about the competitive structure of that market than any permit number.
Fourth, on 3 September, watch the fleet count rather than the reveal. The Cybercab is a new body on the same software stack, in the same geofence, run by the same service. The falsifiable test is simple and dated: if Tesla is still operating on the order of 20 to 30 unsupervised vehicles a month after the event, the launch was choreography. If that number moves materially, the cost-curve argument starts to bind.

CHINA CORNER: Beijing Writes Down Who Pays, and Xiaomi Writes Its Own Silicon
The manufacturer handles the ticket
On Tuesday, a draft revision to China’s Road Traffic Safety Law went to the Standing Committee of the National People’s Congress for initial review. Nine chapters, 170 articles, including a new dedicated chapter titled “Special Provisions for Autonomous Vehicles” covering public-road operation, traffic violations and insurance.
The headline provision: manufacturers or importers would handle road traffic safety violations committed while fully autonomous driving functions are activated.
Now the precision. This applies to the entity responsible for handling traffic violations. It does not mean liability for compensation in all traffic accidents automatically shifts to automakers. Those are different legal questions, and the draft addresses the first one.
What is covered: vehicles operating with fully autonomous functions activated. What is not covered: vehicles whose autonomous functions are not activated, and vehicles equipped only with assisted-driving features. Both continue to be regulated as non-autonomous, meaning the driver remains subject to conventional rules.
That distinction carries almost all of the practical weight right now. MIIT reported that L2 combined driver-assistance has reached 70.5% penetration in Chinese passenger vehicles this year, with navigation-on-autopilot at 34.2%. Those systems are explicitly excluded. So the provision affects higher-level autonomous vehicles approved for public roads, which today is a short list: China granted its first L3 market-access approvals in December 2025, to Changan’s Deepal and BAIC’s Arcfox.
One open question the published summary does not answer, and it is the one that decides whether any of this works: determining whether an autonomous function was activated will be critical to enforcement, and nobody has explained how vehicle operating data would be accessed or what evidentiary rules apply in a dispute. Everything turns on that.
This is the complement to the standard we covered in CW33. GB 44721-2026, the mandatory national safety requirement for L3 and L4 systems, was approved on 30 July and takes effect 1 July 2027. It requires autonomous systems to be at least as safe as a competent and attentive driver, requires L3 systems to monitor driver readiness, and builds a testing framework combining corporate capability checks, safety dossiers and third-party confirmatory testing.
So within roughly three weeks, China published a mandatory technical safety baseline and drafted the legal question of who answers for a violation. The standard says how safe the system must be. The law says who gets the ticket when it is not. Read alongside Dirk Fratzke’s observation in EP20 of Autonomy Insiders that China’s regulatory system is structurally “very close to Europe” with independent assessment built in, the picture is of a jurisdiction assembling a complete regulatory stack while others assemble parts of one.
Worth holding one comparison alongside it. As we have tracked on the Middle East thread, Saudi Arabia’s self-driving traffic law assigns legal responsibility for fully autonomous vehicles to the owner. China is proposing the manufacturer. 
And in the same week, Xiaomi announced its own driving chip
Which brings us to the other half of the Chinese build-out, because a regulatory stack is not much use without silicon to run it on.
On Monday, Xiaomi unveiled the XRing D100, its first in-house smart-driving chip, and claims it is China’s first high-computing-power smart-driving AI chip built on a 3-nanometre process.
What it is: a 20-core CPU and a 16-core NPU, supporting up to 160 GB of unified memory, which Xiaomi says lets it run large language models with as many as 200 billion parameters locally. Validation is complete and commercial use is scheduled for 2027. Before it reaches a vehicle, Xiaomi has installed it in an “AI Cube Prototype” desktop terminal running 120B and 3B models locally. It was announced alongside two siblings, the XRing O3 mobile SoC (debuting in the Xiaomi 18 Fold) and the XRing O100 AI accelerator, also slated for 2027.
What we do not know is more interesting than what we do. Xiaomi has not disclosed the chip’s computing power in TOPS. It has not named its foundry. It has not named the first vehicle to use it, and it has given no specific timetable for installing it in cars. Those four blanks make a direct performance comparison impossible, which is presumably part of why they are blank.
The commitment behind it is not ambiguous. Xiaomi says it has invested more than 21 billion yuan, about $3.1 billion, since restarting its chip programme, and now employs a chip team of nearly 3,000 people. Today Xiaomi’s vehicles run mainly on NVIDIA Thor, having previously used Orin.
The scale question is the whole story
Here is the frame I would apply, and it comes from Augustin Friedel of MHP in EP02 of Autonomy Insiders [ https://substack.com/redirect/68a0908c-d9fa-4534-90fb-60853a347ade?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ], which remains the most useful conversation this show has had about chips.
Friedel’s point was that in-house silicon is a volume decision long before it is a performance one. “At the end, it’s all about scale, which they would need to reach.” His example was Xpeng, which justifies its own silicon not on cars alone but across cars, humanoid robots and eVTOLs through its air-taxi subsidiary. Automotive alone is not enough to amortise a chip programme.
That is exactly the test Xiaomi faces, and it is the reason Xiaomi is a more interesting entrant than the others. It is the one company in this cohort with a consumer-electronics volume base sitting next to the car business, which is why the D100 arrived alongside a phone SoC and an AI accelerator rather than on its own. Whether the D100 enters vehicles on schedule in 2027 and reaches enough volume to amortise the R&D cost is the actual question, and it will not be answered by a spec sheet.
Friedel’s second point is the corrective to how this story usually gets written. TOPS is close to a vanity metric, and he said Chinese OEMs have gamed it deliberately: one manufacturer ran three NVIDIA Orin chips for 750 TOPS, so a rival specified four purely to win the comparison. The decision vectors that actually matter are memory bandwidth, thermal behaviour, energy draw, ASIL certification, and the one he called most neglected, tool-chain and ecosystem maturity. Level 4 systems generally sit in the 1,000 to 2,000 TOPS range, but the number alone tells you very little.
With that caveat attached, here is where the Chinese cohort stands:
Xiaomi’s 3nm process is the most advanced node in that table.
This is not only a Chinese story
The same logic is now running in the West, and it accelerated this month.
As we covered last week, Waymo confirmed its own custom ASIC, in production in its latest robotaxi generation, at more than 1,000 TOPS on TSMC’s 5nm process. But a Waymo spokesperson told TechCrunch, “It’s for the system, not a single chip.” Rivian ships the custom 5nm RAP1 at 800 TOPS. Tesla has self-designed longest of anyone, is on its fourth or fifth generation with a sixth in development, and committed $16.8 billion to the Terafab project in Texas.
Friedel called this in March, five months before Waymo’s announcement. Asked whether there was a single best Level 4 chip, he declined the question and described strategies instead: “If you look at Waymo... they are going for optimization on all the different layers. They optimized probably their own chips, their own software, they’re heavily involved in sensor design... they are all in on the self-driving stack.” Against that, “players like WeRide or Pony that rely more on NVIDIA chips or Qualcomm chips, and then try to optimize based on that.” That is a clean description of Waymo’s custom ASIC before it existed publicly.
Two constraints keep this from being a straight line, and both come from EP02.
NVIDIA’s moat is software, not silicon. “They have a very strong moat or lock-in which is the software layer... it’s very difficult for them to replace.” Designing your own chip does not free you from NVIDIA unless you also rebuild the toolchain, the middleware and the development environment your engineering organisation runs on. Xiaomi’s cars run on Thor today, and the D100 does not change that until the software around it is ready.
And nobody escapes the foundry. OEMs designing in-house still depend on TSMC, Intel or GlobalFoundries, because building chip production is, in Friedel’s words, “very capital intensive... you need super high volume to make the business case work.” They design the chip and hand the supply-chain risk to someone else. That is why Xiaomi declining to name its foundry is a real gap rather than a detail
What this means and what to watch
First, watch the enforcement mechanism, not the liability headline. The draft’s practical force depends entirely on how regulators determine whether an autonomous function was engaged, and that has not been published. Until there is a rule on data access and evidentiary standards in a dispute, this is a statement of intent. Watch for that detail in the subsequent deliberations, because the provision may still be amended and has not taken effect.
Second, the standard and the law are one system and should be read as one. GB 44721-2026 takes effect 1 July 2027. The manufacturer-liability chapter is moving through the NPC now. China is sequencing a safety baseline and a liability regime  to land in roughly the same window.
Third, for the D100 specifically, the number to wait for is not TOPS. It is the first vehicle. A chip with no named vehicle and no installation timetable is a validated prototype.
