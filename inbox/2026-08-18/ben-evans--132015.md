** In search of objects
------------------------------------------------------------

My friend came back from an experimental music gig recently with an audio cassette. There’s something of a trend for these, apparently, just as there is for vinyl (though without the claims about sound quality). The inconvenience is part of the point - they’re hard to digitise, which restricts circulation and means the creator retains a degree of control (this reminds me of a hip denim shop in Tokyo that called itself ‘Not Found’ so that you couldn’t Google it - you had to be in the know). They’re also a way to package up a set of tracks in a way that’s otherwise awkward without a tool like Soundcloud. And a little of it is really a pose, of course. But what’s interesting to me is that they’re something that you can actually give someone.

** Three trillion?
------------------------------------------------------------

‘Anthropic investors’ told the FT that it’s on track for run-rate revenue of $100-120bn by the end of 2026, which would be roughly the same growth rate it was at when it reported a $47bn run-rate in May (and implying maybe $70bn now?). On that basis, they propose an IPO valuation of $2tr (or perhaps $3tr, asserting a 30x revenue multiple). It would be kind of nice to see actual GAAP revenue (most reporting says the Anthropic revenue number is the gross paid by end-users to cloud providers, not the net revenue attributable to Anthropic), and getting a real sense of gross margins and the operating cost structure would be nice too.

The underlying valuation problem, as I’ve written a few times, is that we don’t know where supply and demand will get into balance (will there be a ‘dark chip’ glut?), nor how much pricing power and competitive leverage the model labs will have, nor how long we’ll keep pouring money into chasing the frontier, nor where we are in the cycle - indeed, in fairness, whether this is a cycle. If you bought Amazon in August 1999, you’d have been completely right about the future, but two years later you’d be down by 90% and you’d not be back in profit until the summer of 2007. Right now, no-one knows, and there are no certainties to anchor your analysis, which is why SpaceX share price is so volatile. ** Meta returns to open
------------------------------------------------------------

Meta’s AI reset continues - its new models are pretty close to the frontier, and perhaps more importantly on the ‘Paretto Curve’ of optimal price/performance. Now, just as for its Llama models last year, it’s started making them open as well, meaning that you no longer have to go to China to get a good open model. In particular, it released an open model optimised for running on a single PC.

It always seemed to be pretty clear that Meta’s strategy was for models to be commodity infrastructure sold at cost: it doesn’t have a cloud business to sell them at a profit (though that’s now being added as a backstop, at least in the current pricing crunch) but clearly it’s own business will be dependant on the capabilities that come from AI, so it wants this to be a cheap commodity, with the value coming from whatever you build on top - in Meta’s case, social and ads. Now Meta is back, but much more so than last year, releasing an ‘open’ model is also a statement about risk, and politics. Anthropic has alienated much of Silicon Valley by arguing that the risks of AI (even just things like security, without getting into AGI) means access to the frontier should be narrowly restricted, which might be a good-faith position but is also unavoidably an argument for ‘regulatory capture’ and reduced competition. Meta is pushing the opposite view - that the path to safety is to give the same
tools to everyone. Of course, that’s also the attitude it took with social. ON-DEVICE (https://us.list-manage.com/L9283O5CWhl?e=bc4ee99da9&c2id=e2956131aa985567d31359cb60722597) , STRATEGY (https://us.list-manage.com/nfZrbVcvw0L?e=bc4ee99da9&c2id=e2956131aa985567d31359cb60722597)


** The Bank of Jensen
------------------------------------------------------------

Two weeks ago the WSJ reported that Nvidia was going to guarantee the loans to build a $250bn OpenAI data centre; this week, apparently, that guarantee will only be for half the spend. But also this week, Nvidia announced a new $500bn AI financing JV with four giant investment firms (Apollo, Blackrock, Blackstone and KKR), plus Goldman Sachs, plus the giant developer Brookfield.

There are simple and complicated ways to explain this new JV: the simple way is that Nvidia wants to turn GPUs into fungible, tradable assets that can be used as security for loans. If you borrow money to build an AI cloud and then go bust, someone else can pick up the GPUs, so the credit risk to the finance providers is lower.

This is all very clever (see the complex explanations), but has two underpinning assumptions (as the cool kids say, ‘load-bearing assumptions’). First, are you sure that resale prices for GPUs are predicable and stable enough over the life of your loan to be collateral? These are not obviously long-lived assets. And second, are you sure that defaults won’t be correlated? The most obvious reason that someone would be unable to service a loan they took out to buy GPUs is that compute prices have collapsed, in which case, no-one else would be able to pay back the loan either. There are lots of problems with comparing the GPU boom to the fibre boom (which was built ahead of demand, not behind), but it would not necessarily have been sensible to lend money to fibre carriers and take fibre as collateral.

And more fundamentally, isn’t Nvidia own cash flow, that underpins these guarantees, subject to that same correlation? ** Claude watermarking
------------------------------------------------------------

Anthropic made a splash implementing the EU’s new requirement that text generated by AI have an identifying ‘watermark’ of some kind. Claude does this by looking at places in the text where there are multiple words that could be chosen with the same meaning: if it varies enough of these, it can record that as a pattern. This means the watermark won’t be added if you just use Claude to check for typos (it has to be able to choose which words to use, multiple times), and also means it will be degraded or removed if you rewrite or edit enough (theoretically you could also make a tool to identify those kinds of words and scramble them to new choices). It’s not clear there’s any way to make this foolproof in text: obviously it’s far easier for images, since you have so much more data to play with. ** Everything is hacked
------------------------------------------------------------

This story will be the first of many: someone asked their OpenClaw agent to get them an earlier gym booking, but there were no slots, so it achieved the task by finding a bug in the gym’s booking software and cancelling someone else’s appointment. This is the perfect illustration of two big problems: first, AI massively reduces the cost and effort required to hack things, making many more places vulnerable that wouldn’t have been worth the effort before, and secondly, an AI system will try to do what you tell if and often succeed, but that may not be quite what you think you told it. LINK (https://us.list-manage.com/14uqDzXLU6F?e=bc4ee99da9&c2id=e2956131aa985567d31359cb60722597)

At the other end of the scale, a group of bitcoin companies wrote an open letter complaining that AI safety rules (in part intended to prevent stories like the one above) mean that they have worse tools than their attackers (and of course hacking bitcoin has an inherent payoff). As I wrote last week, there isn’t an easy answer here - it’s trade-offs all the way down. LINK (https://us.list-manage.com/sSuzKxFZXIO?e=bc4ee99da9&c2id=e2956131aa985567d31359cb60722597)

** Ideas
------------------------------------------------------------

AT&T says that open models, including Chinese, are about 25% of its AI workload, and it expects that to rise to 70-80% (though no-one can really know this). This is the reality of real-world AI deployment at big companies: most use-cases (at least so far) don’t need the cutting-edge frontier mode, only the model that delivers a good enough result at the right ROI, and you really really want multi-sourcing, so that you’re not dependant on Dario or Donald.

AT&T also says it’s using 45bn tokens per day (a metric with questionable value). For comparison, back in April, at the peak of ‘tokenmaxxing’, one engineer at Meta managed to use 328bn tokens in 30 days (~11bn/day).
