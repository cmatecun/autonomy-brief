Pony.ai, WeRide, and Baidu Q3 Earnings Analysis
Three Chinese virtual driver companies reported their third quarter this week. We go through Pony.ai and WeRide in the same order, compare the two directly, and finish with the number Baidu didn’t publish.
Pony.ai Q3 26: the financials
Pony.ai beat expectations again. Total revenue rose 75% to $44.6 million, against an analyst consensus of $37.5 million.
By business line:
Robotaxi services: $17.8 million, up 165% year over year and 47% quarter over quarter. Now 40% of revenue, up from 26% a year ago, and the largest contributor to growth. Within that, fare-charging revenue grew nearly 400%
Robotruck services: $18.0 million, up 76% year over year and 35% quarter over quarter, mainly freight transport with Sinotrans. Also about 40% of revenue
Intelligent solutions: $8.9 million, broadly flat
Robotaxi and robotruck together now make up 80% of revenue, up from 66% a year ago. Pony is becoming the company its strategy describes.
165% is still the slowest robotaxi growth rate of the year. Growth is slowing in percentage terms as the base gets larger, which is normal, and the sequential 47% shows the business is still accelerating in absolute dollars.
On profitability:
Gross profit: $7.9 million, up 69%. Gross margin 17.8%, slightly better than Q2 (17.5%) and slightly below a year ago (18.4%)
Operating expenses: $76.6 million, up only 3%. Non-GAAP operating expenses fell 2% to $66.2 million
R&D: $60.1 million, flat year over year. As a share of revenue, R&D fell from about 238% to about 135%, because revenue grew and R&D did not
Operating loss: $68.7 million, slightly better than $69.7 million a year ago. The operating margin improved from minus 274% to minus 154%
That last line is the clearest sign of operating leverage in the print: revenue up 75%, operating expenses up 3%.
Now the headline that looks wrong. Pony’s net loss more than doubled to $136.3 million, from $61.6 million. That sounds like the business got worse, and it didn’t. The net loss includes a non-cash loss of $77.0 million from changes in the fair value of trading securities Pony holds. It says something about Pony’s investment portfolio and nothing about its robotaxis. Excluding that and share-based compensation, the non-GAAP net loss narrowed 14% to $48.9 million. The operating loss is the number to use.
Cash and runway. Pony ended September with about $1.15 billion in cash, deposits and wealth management products, down from $1.21 billion at the end of June. Non-GAAP free cash outflow was $78.2 million in the quarter. At that rate, the cash covers roughly three and a half to four years. Capital expenditure fell 45% quarter over quarter to $17.7 million, which management attributes to a disciplined pace of investment while the Gen-7 fleet keeps growing.
The market is not convinced yet: Pony’s US-listed shares are down 79% since the start of the year.
Pony.ai Q3 26: fleet and operations
The fleet reached 2,768 robotaxis at the end of September. The target for the end of the year is 3,500, so Pony needs to add about 730 vehicles in one quarter, roughly a quarter of its current fleet. Management says it is on track.
The robotruck fleet is harder to track. Pony’s quarterly releases only report robotaxis. The last truck number in its SEC filings comes from the annual report (20-F): 210 robotrucks as of 31 March 2026, a mix of Level 2++ trucks and Level 4 trucks with safety drivers, up from just over 140 at the end of 2025. Neither the Q2 interim report nor this quarter’s release gives an update. So unless Pony discloses it earlier, the next official number will most likely come with the next annual report, around April 2027.
In China, the operating milestones this quarter:
Guangzhou: the central districts and Nansha now form a single contiguous service area of more than 1,000 square kilometres, about nine times the size of San Francisco, with rides across five districts. Longer trips mean higher average prices per ride
Source: Pony.ai Q3 2026 Investor Presentation
Shenzhen: service to and from Bao’an International Airport is open to the public. Pony says it is the only company running a commercial, fully driverless robotaxi service at an international airport in a Chinese tier-one city
Shanghai: new permits for fully driverless operation in core areas of Pudong, with Zhangjiang Science City next
New cities: deployments started in Hangzhou, Chongqing and Hengqin (Zhuhai)
Source: Pony.ai Q3 2026 Investor Presentation
Internationally:
Zagreb, Croatia: from launch in April to fully driverless test rides in September, five months, more than 200,000 km and several thousand passenger trips, with Uber and Verne
UK: with Uber, road testing is underway in Manchester, with London to follow
Dubai: shown alongside the RTA at WETEX last month
Kazakhstan: an MoU with inDrive and NVIDIA, Pony’s first step into Central Asia
A pipeline of more than 4,000 robotaxis under agreements in negotiation internationally
The Toyota bZ4X joins the fleet. On 16 November, Pony.ai and Toyota launched fully driverless commercial service with their jointly developed bZ4X Robotaxi in Guangzhou’s Nansha district. It is one of Pony’s three Gen-7 models (with BAIC and GAC), and all three are now in fully driverless commercial operation. Some context:
Pony and Toyota have worked together on robotaxis since 2019
In 2023, Pony, Toyota Motor China and GAC Toyota formed a joint venture with more than RMB 1 billion ($150 million) of investment
The mass-production version rolled off GAC Toyota’s line in February 2026
The plan is around 1,000 bZ4X robotaxis across major Chinese cities

The difference we pointed out then still applies: Pony’s car comes from GAC Toyota in China, Waymo’s from Toyota in Japan. The same vehicle, built in two countries for two stacks, which matters given the politics around Chinese vehicle technology in the US.

My read, and I’d label it as a read: May’s move to a second OEM may partly be a response to exactly that. When your carmaker supplies Waymo and Pony.ai too, you are one customer among several, and a second source of vehicles makes sense.
Pony.ai Q3 26: insurance and unit economics at home and abroad
Insurers are pricing in the safety record. On renewal, Pony’s per-vehicle insurance premiums for the Gen-7 fleet fell another 19%, to about 40% of what an average human-driven taxi pays, down from about 50% in 2025. CFO Leo Wang pointed out that the cars are more expensive than taxis, which would normally push premiums up because repairs cost more, and the safety record more than offset that. I’d weight this more than most numbers in the release, because it comes from a counterparty with money on the line, not from Pony.
Source: Pony.ai Q3 2026 Investor Presentation
Unit economics: two models. Pony runs its robotaxi business in two ways:
In China, mostly a self-owned fleet, where Pony earns the fare
Overseas, a joint deployment model, where local fleet operators and platforms own the cars, and Pony earns from vehicle sales and a Virtual Driver licensing fee
In China, Pony says it has positive unit economics in Guangzhou and Shenzhen, and Wang laid out exactly what that means. Revenue is the net ride-hailing revenue after discounts and refunds. The cost side has seven items:
Vehicle depreciation, including the battery and the autonomous driving hardware
Electricity for charging
Salaries for remote assistance and ground support staff
Cleaning, service and maintenance
Insurance
Parking
Network communication
About half of the cost is vehicle depreciation, the other half is the other six items. What is not in it is R&D. That matches what James Peng told me on Autonomy Insiders three weeks ago, where he also put a number on the step from city-level to company-level break-even: roughly 50,000 robotaxis if Pony ran only robotaxis (Autonomy Insiders EP34 [ https://substack.com/redirect/2753c0ce-b057-4792-86bb-0a6e092a7e8d?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]). Pony runs 2,768 today.
Overseas is meant to be the higher-margin business. Wang gave two reasons. On the cost side, Pony’s staff-to-vehicle ratio and energy efficiency matter more in Europe, where labour and electricity are expensive. On the structure side, the joint deployment model is asset-light: Pony doesn’t carry the vehicle capex, and depots, charging, cleaning and maintenance sit with the partner. In Peng’s terms from the podcast, of the four roles in a taxi business (user acquisition, fleet ownership, driver, servicing), Pony keeps the driver and can hand the other three to partners. Pony expects joint deployment to become a high-margin business that scales.
All of this and more about Pony’s business model and unit economics is already in our company deep dive that you can find here: Pony AI Deep Dive. Pony.ai Q3 26: targets and robotrucks
Pony set three targets at the start of the year, and says it is on track for all three: robotaxi revenue at 3.5 times the 2025 level, a fleet of 3,500 vehicles, and 20 cities worldwide.
On robotrucks, the more interesting line is about next year. Pony unveiled a new Gen-4 heavy-duty robotruck with GAC Commercial Vehicle, and is starting operations at Jiangmen Port and Mawan Port in Guangdong, China’s largest provincial economy and biggest export hub. After proving the first two ports, the target is hundreds of vehicles across ports next year, with a pipeline of global port operators. Light-duty robotrucks have entered production for urban logistics, with three to four times the cargo capacity and two to three times the speed of the low-speed robovans common in China today.
Pony.ai Q3 26: why its CTO thinks the model can’t be copied
The most interesting part of the call came from CTO Tiancheng Lou, and it is worth taking slowly.
The comparison with large language models. Like frontier LLMs, physical AI is trained in two stages: pre-training on large amounts of data, which teaches basic driving, and post-training through reinforcement learning, which is where Lou says 90 to 95% of the real work happens. Pre-training alone is bounded by human driving data. To get beyond the level of a human driver on rare, dangerous situations, the model has to be trained on them, over and over.
That happens in PonyWorld, Pony’s world model, which Lou calls a closed-loop virtual driving school. It generates 10 billion kilometres of training every week.
Source: Pony.ai Q3 2026 Investor Presentation
Why it has to mirror reality. Here is the point that separates driving from coding. When an AI learns to write code, the training environment and the real environment are the same. In driving, the simulated world has to reproduce the probability distribution of the real one. If edge cases appear too rarely in simulation, the model never learns them. If they appear too often, the driving policy becomes overly cautious. The simulation must match reality, and Pony calibrates PonyWorld continuously with what its fleet encounters on public roads.
What that means for retraining. Because the model is trained on generalisable scenarios rather than specific roads, Lou says it needs zero retraining wherever the traffic rules and driving styles stay the same. Expanding Guangzhou to more than 1,000 square kilometres, and to tens of thousands across cities next, is then just a matter of deploying more cars. New countries need localisation (traffic law, left- or right-hand driving, local behaviour), which PonyWorld handles with simulated local road users before launch. That is how Lou explains Zagreb going fully driverless in five months, and why he expects it to be repeatable internationally.
Why it can’t simply be distilled. LLMs can be distilled: a competitor queries a strong model through its public API and trains a smaller one on the answers. Pony’s models have no public API. They run inside a closed loop of world model, reinforcement learning and a driverless fleet that feeds real edge cases back into the simulation. To copy the result, you would have to build the whole loop, including a large driverless fleet, from scratch. Lou’s conclusion: a deeper moat than LLMs have, and marginal R&D costs that keep falling as the same engine covers more area, more countries and more vehicle types (robotaxi, heavy truck, light truck).
Source: Pony.ai Q3 2026 Investor Presentation
That is a strong argument, and the falling R&D share of revenue supports it. The fair counterweight: the moat is only as deep as the fleet feeding it, and Waymo, Baidu and WeRide run the same kind of loop with fleets of similar or larger size. It protects Pony against latecomers more than against its current peers.
Now let’s switch to WeRide.
WeRide Q3 26: the financials
WeRide reports in RMB. To make it comparable with Pony, I’ve converted at the rate WeRide uses in its release, RMB 6.7045 per US dollar.
Revenue rose 88% to RMB 321.5 million ($48.0 million), up 38.8% quarter over quarter, a record and ahead of expectations. So WeRide and Pony now report almost the same revenue: $48.0 million against $44.6 million.
The overseas share is the headline. Overseas revenue rose 171.5% to RMB 146.9 million ($21.9 million), now 45.7% of total revenue. That is up from close to 40% in Q2.
By business line:
L4 (robotaxi, robobus, robosweeper): RMB 168.8 million ($25.2 million), up 47% year over year and 35% quarter over quarter
Robotaxi, within L4: more than RMB 100 million (over $14.9 million) for the first time, up 204%, about 60% of L4 revenue. More than 90% of robotaxi revenue came from overseas, led by the Middle East
L2++/L3 (driver assistance): grew nearly fivefold
AI infrastructure: nearly doubled
Together, the last two came to about RMB 152.7 million ($22.8 million)
The other way to cut it: service revenue rose 102% and is now 58% of total revenue. That includes the mileage-based fees WeRide earns on every overseas robotaxi ride, which grow with the fleet without WeRide owning the cars.
Gross margin reached 43.0%, up 10.1 points year over year and 5.5 points quarter over quarter (37.5% in Q2). Gross profit was RMB 138.1 million ($20.6 million). The CFO was open that this quarter benefited from a favourable mix that may vary. Two drivers: overseas robotaxi fees, recognised on a net basis, and driver assistance licences with a high double-digit margin.
A note on comparability. WeRide’s 43% and Pony’s 17.8% are not the same kind of number. WeRide books overseas robotaxi income as net fees on cars it doesn’t own. Pony’s robotaxi revenue includes fares from its own fleet, with all the costs of running that fleet in cost of revenue. Both margins are correct. They just measure different businesses.
WeRide Q3 26: costs, and one line to read carefully
R&D: RMB 465.3 million ($69.4 million), up 47% year over year but only 7% quarter over quarter. R&D as a share of revenue fell about 40 percentage points, from roughly 185% to roughly 145%
Selling expenses: RMB 26.5 million, just 8.2% of revenue
Operating loss: RMB 332.9 million ($49.6 million), narrower by 12% year over year and 21% quarter over quarter
Net loss: RMB 310.0 million ($46.2 million), narrower by 22.6% quarter over quarter and roughly flat year over year
The R&D comments were the most interesting part of the cost discussion. Li said the R&D team is now largely in place and WeRide does not plan large additions to R&D headcount. From here, the focus is on efficiency and output. Part of this quarter’s R&D was one-off (summer campus hiring, first-half bonuses) and part is planned investment in the next-generation robotaxi, which is meant to bring the hardware cost down further. That said, R&D still grew 47% year over year. The ratio fell because revenue grew faster. The next few quarters will show whether the headcount freeze holds.
And the line to read carefully. Administrative expenses were only RMB 13.9 million, against RMB 100.4 million a year ago. That includes a non-cash reversal of about RMB 49 million in share-based compensation, related to employees who left. It is a one-off, and it flatters this quarter’s loss. On an adjusted basis, excluding share-based compensation and fair value changes, the net loss was RMB 336.5 million ($50.2 million), essentially flat quarter over quarter. 
WeRide Q3 26: cash
WeRide ended the quarter with about RMB 4.7 billion ($695 million) in cash, deposits and wealth management products. That is about RMB 700 million less than at the end of June.
Li attributed most of the decline to timing rather than run-rate burn: vehicles and kits bought ahead of large overseas robotaxi deliveries in Q4, receivables still open at quarter end, a prepayment for the next-generation robotaxi, and first-half bonuses. She expects collections and customer prepayments to keep cash roughly stable in Q4.
The plan from here:
Cash burn of about RMB 1 billion ($149 million) in 2027, and lower again in 2028
First quarter of positive free cash flow in 2028
Positive free cash flow for the full year 2029, with more than RMB 1 billion still in the bank at that point
So in WeRide’s own metric, current cash fully funds the path to self-sustaining cash flow. WeRide has bought back more than $95 million of shares since March and says it will not issue new shares at the current valuation. Revenue guidance for 2026 was raised to at least RMB 1.1 billion (about $164 million), up more than 60%.
WeRide Q3 26: driver assistance and Jingshuo
L2++/L3. Cumulative deliveries of WeRide’s driver assistance system WRD 3.0 passed 100,000 units, which means WeRide hit its full-year target ahead of schedule. It won 10 new mass-production design wins this quarter. The target is 500,000 units by the end of next year. WeRide is paid through engineering fees and royalties, so the business carries a high double-digit gross margin at low marginal cost.
AI infrastructure, and the new subsidiary Jingshuo. Revenue in this segment nearly doubled. It sits in Jingshuo, the subsidiary WeRide spun out, which turns the data tooling WeRide built for its own robotaxis into a product. It provides pre-training data, post-training environments and verification to dozens of companies working on foundation models, autonomous driving, embodied AI and robotics, with standard and custom datasets, licensed data processing software and simulation environments. It has orders under framework agreements worth several billion RMB, and Li said some customers who bought millions of hours of data are now considering tens of millions of hours over the next two years.
The product stack has three layers:
EGOK: a wearable device that collects multimodal data, with all data streams synchronised at the hardware level
SkillForge: turns first-person task data into skill sets that can be used for training, testing and licensing
Crucible: an environment hub that turns tasks into interactive environments models can run in, connected to post-training systems
Asked whether Jingshuo is a distraction from L4, CEO Tony Han said no: it monetises data and tools WeRide already built. Physical AI companies need exactly the kind of real-world data that a driverless fleet generates as a by-product. Whether “several billion RMB” in framework agreements turns into revenue at that scale is the open question. Framework agreements are not contracts.
WeRide Q3 26: fleet and overseas
The L4 fleet reached about 4,100 vehicles, of which more than 2,300 are robotaxis, up 26% quarter over quarter. WeRide now operates in 13 countries and holds permits in 9.
Middle East: the engine. The fleet there reached about 600 vehicles, up about 50% from roughly 400 at the end of July. Li expects 800 to 1,000 by the end of the year and the fleet to roughly double again next year, with full coverage of the core areas of Abu Dhabi and Dubai by year end. The partners are platforms and local taxi operators, including Uber and TXAI, who own the vehicles and handle cleaning and daily operations. WeRide says it is now Uber’s largest driverless partner outside the US by fleet size.
Europe:
Madrid: Spain’s first national L4 operating permit, with commercial launch planned by the end of the year, with Uber
Zurich: public robotaxi service with SBB and Swiss Transit Lab, and fully driverless robobus operation at Zurich Airport
France: an autonomous driving partnership with public transport operator Keolis
Belgium, Slovakia: robobus routes, and road testing in Slovakia under the national ELEVATE programme
More than 100 L4 vehicles in Europe by year end, which WeRide says will be the largest such fleet in Europe
Plus Singapore with Grab (more than 20,000 rides) and a new insurance partnership with Allianz in Singapore and Ping An.
What limits the scaling. Morgan Stanley asked when deployment will accelerate. Li’s answer was clear: the main constraint is the timing of permits, not the technology. In major cities, going from testing with a safety driver to fully driverless operation usually takes at least a year. And then the number: WeRide has binding contracts for about 3,000 robotaxis globally, signed with several partners, for delivery as soon as permits come through.
How WeRide earns on each of those cars: it sells the robotaxi up front, bundled with about six years of recurring, mileage-based revenue sharing. So each vehicle brings upfront revenue and a long tail of recurring income, while the partner who owns the car has every incentive to maximise its utilisation. Han’s long-term target: grow the operating fleet roughly tenfold every three years.
WeRide: unit economics in Guangzhou
In China, WeRide said Guangzhou reached positive unit economics at city level for a full quarter for the first time. The fleet averaged 21 rides per vehicle per day, with a single-day peak of 33.
Source: WeRide/LinkedIn
Asked how it defines unit economics, Li listed the cost side: vehicle depreciation including the battery and autonomous driving hardware, charging, salaries for remote and ground support staff, cleaning, maintenance, insurance, parking, and network and telecom fees. Slightly less than half of the cost is depreciation. Revenue is net ride-hailing revenue after refunds. R&D is not included.
The three drivers she named: high utilisation, longer average trips, and a more efficient staff-to-vehicle ratio. Overseas, she said, the structure is even better because partners carry the vehicle capex, and WeRide has already improved the remote assistance ratio in the Middle East to one operator for more than 10 vehicles.
Pony and WeRide, side by side
Here is what makes this quarter different from previous ones. For the first time, the two companies define unit economics almost the same way. Put the two answers next to each other:
Revenue: Pony counts net ride-hailing revenue after discounts and refunds. WeRide counts net ride-hailing revenue after refunds
Costs: both list depreciation (vehicle, battery, AD hardware), charging, remote and ground staff, cleaning and maintenance, insurance, parking and network
Weight of depreciation: Pony about half, WeRide slightly less than half
Excluded: R&D, in both cases
Where it is positive: Pony in Guangzhou and Shenzhen, WeRide in Guangzhou
That matters, because until now the comparison was harder. In CW34 we pointed out that WeRide’s overseas per-vehicle number was a licensing measure, a different financial object from an owner-operator P&L. In Guangzhou, both are owner-operator P&Ls, with the same cost lines, in the same city. The claims are comparable.
And neither definition includes R&D, which is where most of the money goes. The rest of the picture, side by side:
Revenue: Pony $44.6 million (+75%) / WeRide $48.0 million (+88%)
Robotaxi revenue: Pony $17.8 million (+165%) / WeRide over $14.9 million (+204%)
Where robotaxi revenue comes from: Pony mainly its own fleet in China / WeRide more than 90% overseas, on partner-owned cars
Gross margin: Pony 17.8% / WeRide 43.0%
R&D as a share of revenue: Pony about 135% (from about 238%) / WeRide about 145% (from about 185%)
Robotaxi fleet: Pony 2,768 / WeRide more than 2,300
Liquidity: Pony about $1.15 billion / WeRide about $695 million
Remote assistance: Pony 1:30 in China / WeRide 1:10 in the Middle East and 1:40 in China
Positive unit economics in one city means a car covers its own running costs there. Company-level break-even means the whole fleet also covers R&D. 
Baidu: the number that isn’t there
Baidu also reported Q3. Total revenue fell 1% to RMB 30.8 billion, while AI Cloud Infra revenue grew 94% to RMB 8.2 billion. But for us, the most interesting number in the robotaxi section is the one that isn’t there.
For two years, Baidu has led its Apollo Go update with the number of fully driverless rides per quarter:
Q3 2025: 3.1 million
Q4 2025: 3.4 million
Q1 2026: 3.2 million
Q2 2026: about 1 million
Q3 2026: not disclosed
Q2 was the Wuhan quarter. At the end of March, Apollo Go vehicles stalled on a busy elevated road in Wuhan with passengers inside, three ministries froze new AV licences for every operator nationwide, and Baidu’s largest market went dark.
In September, I wrote that I expected Q3 to bring the recovery, with one warning: the fully driverless count would lag the real operational recovery for as long as safety drivers stayed on board, so that was the metric to watch. This release doesn’t settle it, because the metric is missing.
What Baidu reported instead:
Shenzhen: fully driverless rides up about 170% quarter over quarter. It is now one of Apollo Go’s largest markets
Dubai: a larger operating area and a doubled fleet
30 cities, up from 27 in the spring
About 34 million autonomous kilometres in Q3, and more than 390 million cumulative as of 31 October
London testing with Uber and Lyft, and fully driverless testing on Hong Kong’s Airport Island
There are two ways to read the missing number.
Read one: the recovery happened, just not in this metric. Some cars returned to Wuhan with safety drivers on board. Kilometres count those trips. Fully driverless rides don’t.
Read two: the recovery hasn’t happened yet. Shenzhen grows fast, but from a frozen base, and one city doesn’t make up for the rest.
Baidu’s cumulative mileage gives a rough way to compare quarters, even without a ride count:
Q1 release (May): more than 330 million autonomous kilometres in total
Q2 release (August): more than 350 million
31 October: more than 390 million
So between the May and August releases, the fleet added roughly 20 million kilometres, in a period that includes the second half of the licence freeze and the first weeks of recovery. Between August and the end of October, a slightly shorter period, it added roughly 40 million. Q3 alone came to about 34 million. These are rounded “more than” figures tied to release dates rather than quarter ends, so treat it as rough. But it points clearly in one direction: the fleet is driving much more again than it was during the freeze.
I lean towards read one. The operating signals (Shenzhen, Dubai, 34 million kilometres in a quarter, and now the mileage pace) point that way. That would also explain why the count is missing: with safety drivers still on board in Wuhan, it would probably look weak next to 3.2 million. And no company puts a weak-looking number in its release when a better one is available.
So my read: the recovery I expected in September is happening. Baidu just chose the metric that shows it. For two years it led with rides. This quarter it led with distance.
I’ll call the recovery complete when the ride count is back in the release.
What this means and what to watch
First, overseas is where the margin is, and it’s asset-light. WeRide makes more than 90% of its robotaxi revenue abroad, and Pony expects its joint deployment model to be the higher-margin business too. Both hand the cars, depots and cleaning to partners and keep the driver. Watch WeRide’s Middle East fleet against the 800 to 1,000 target at year end.
Second, permits are now the binding constraint, not the technology. WeRide has 3,000 robotaxis under binding contracts waiting for permits, Pony has a 4,000-vehicle pipeline. Watch Madrid’s commercial launch by year end, and how fast Pony’s UK testing turns into a London permit.
Third, unit economics are now comparable, and still exclude the biggest cost. Both companies count the same costs in Guangzhou and both leave out R&D. Watch for a margin per vehicle, and for the R&D line: WeRide says headcount is done, Pony’s R&D is flat. If both hold while revenue keeps growing, the gap between city-level and company-level break-even starts to close.
Fourth, read past the headline loss. Pony’s net loss doubled because of a securities revaluation, WeRide’s narrowed partly because of a compensation reversal. For Pony, the operating line tells the real story. For WeRide, the reversal sits in operating expenses too, so the adjusted loss is the better guide, and it was flat.
Fifth, Baidu’s ride count is the recovery test. If it returns in Q4, read one is confirmed. If Baidu leads with kilometres again, the question gets louder.

Waymo leaves the freeway, Tesla goes big in Germany, and Uber opens in Munich
Waymo: off the freeway, again
Waymo robotaxis are no longer allowed on California freeways. The state’s Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV), which regulates the testing and deployment of autonomous vehicles in California, issued the restriction on 11 November. It only became public on Tuesday, after the San Francisco Business Times reported it.
According to the DMV, two things triggered the order:
Three Waymo vehicles entered an active construction closure on U.S. Route 101 near Burlingame
In a separate incident, a Waymo tried to pass California Highway Patrol vehicles on Interstate 280 in San Francisco while they were running a traffic break (officers weaving across all lanes at low speed to slow traffic ahead of an accident)
The DMV’s language is unusually sharp. It said Waymo’s driverless freeway operations pose an “imminent hazard to public safety”, because of recurring incursions into active construction zones, despite a prior restriction and corrective actions taken after similar incidents on 18 May. The restriction stays in place until Waymo can show it has taken “effective corrective actions”.
Waymo complied, and went one step further. It has stopped freeway driving in all 15 US cities where it operates commercially, not only in California, to evaluate its performance. Its statement stresses that none of the US-101 encounters involved injuries or collisions, and that the service keeps running on surface streets with more than half a million trips a week.
That last point matters, because some headlines this week read as if Waymo had shut down. It hasn’t. It has lost one road type.
The history makes this one heavier. Waymo started freeway rides in November 2025 in San Francisco, Phoenix and Los Angeles. In May 2026, robotaxis were filmed driving through cones in construction zones and Waymo quietly stopped using freeways. In June, it recalled its entire fifth-generation US fleet, 3,871 vehicles, over driving at speed into closed freeway sections (CW26). After a software update, freeway rides came back gradually from July (CW32). Now the same failure mode has brought the same restriction back, this time imposed by a regulator.
Phil Koopman , who has written about AV safety for years, puts it into a broader pattern in a post this week: school buses, flood water and highway construction zones are three areas where Waymo filed a recall and incidents continued afterwards. That includes the NHTSA school bus query we covered last week (CW47), where most of the incidents happened after the fix. His proposal is simple: if a company says a pattern is fixed, it should publish a dashboard showing how often it still happens. I think that is a fair ask. In our own coverage, we have called transparency regulatory currency, and this is a case where it would pay.
What is new is what the DMV is asking for. Rob Grant, on the Road to Autonomy podcast, made the key point: in a typical recall, the company certifies that its fix addresses the issue and keeps operating. Here the regulator wants to be shown that it works before cars go back on the road. Nobody outside the DMV and Waymo knows yet what that evidence looks like: simulation, test track miles, supervised freeway miles, or a statistical case.
Grant’s own estimate is a pause of 12 to 15 weeks, so Waymo back on freeways sometime around late February. That is his estimate, not the DMV’s. The DMV has published no timeline and no criteria.
Why highways turned out to be hard
For most of the last decade, the industry assumed highways would be the easy part: no pedestrians, no unprotected left turns, barriers between the directions, clean lane markings. Rob Grant, who worked at Cruise, recalled on Road to Autonomy that Cruise deliberately started in dense cities on exactly that theory.
This week suggests the opposite, at least for robotaxis. As Grayson Brulte pointed out on the same show, the vehicles going driverless on US highways are trucks:
Aurora is today the only company running regular operations on trucks with no driver on US highways
Kodiak has told the SEC it will follow by the end of the year
Bot Auto has already completed driverless runs
Volvo Autonomous Solutions told the show it plans to go driver-out in Q1
On the robotaxi side, it’s now zero. Waymo was the only operator carrying passengers driverless on US freeways. 
Why are trucks ahead? Grant points to two factors. Speed leaves far less time to react to an unexpected construction zone than city traffic does. And trucking companies, whose 80,000-pound vehicles cause severe damage even at low speed, built their safety case around the highway from day one, while robotaxi companies built theirs for the city. His open question is whether that city-first way of proving safety needs a revision before freeways.
I’d add a third: trucks drive a handful of fixed corridors, hub to hub, that can be validated one by one. A robotaxi on freeways has to handle every ramp and every lane closure across a whole metro area, wherever riders want to go. 
Grant also makes the point that matters most for everyone else: whatever Waymo has to show to get back on California freeways becomes the bar for every operator in the state. Zoox will face the same questions at the CPUC, and Tesla when it applies for driverless operations. This week's order may set a new standard for highway operations, without a vote in any legislature.
What the pause costs
The direct cost is airports and long trips. SFO is offline for Waymo while the pause lasts, and a ride from the airport down to Menlo Park or Palo Alto on surface streets takes about an hour and a half or more. With freeways, the same trip took about 45 minutes.
The indirect cost is utilization. Waymo is building towards a fleet of about 8,000 vehicles by the end of this year. Without freeways, trips across the Bay Area take longer, the longest and most valuable ones like the airport drop out, and cars spend more time driving empty between rides. More cars on the same surface-street network means a higher cost per ride. That pressure was already building: Grant notes that deadhead miles rose in Q3, according to the CPUC data, in a quarter when freeway rides were only gradually coming back. The pause adds to a trend that fleet growth had already started. In CW35 we read Waymo’s Q2 ride dip mainly as the effect of the summer freeway pause. If the pause runs into February, expect the same effect in Q4 and Q1.
In the short term, those trips go back to Uber and Lyft drivers. No competing robotaxi in the US is permitted or ready to pick up Waymo’s freeway and airport demand.
Tesla: a Cybercab factory in Grünheide
Last week was already a big Cybercab week for Tesla. This week it continued, this time in Germany.
Tesla announced it will invest €3 billion ($3.3 billion) to expand its gigafactory in Grünheide, just outside Berlin. The new building is meant for the Cybercab:
Capacity of up to 1 million Cybercabs a year, according to André Thierig, the site’s senior director of manufacturing
Building permit application before the end of this year
Construction start in the first half of 2027
Production start in 2029
Several hundred new jobs for the first phase of construction and production planning
According to Reuters, that capacity would make Grünheide Germany’s largest car plant by capacity, ahead of Volkswagen’s Wolfsburg headquarters. It lands in a time when Volkswagen, Mercedes-Benz and BMW are all cutting jobs and discussing plant closures. Tesla has hired more than 2,000 people in Grünheide in recent months to ramp up Model Y production and plans to start battery cell production there from the middle of next year.
But analyst Matthias Schmidt raised the obvious question for Germany: how much of a factory built for an automated product ends up creating long-term jobs.
There is also a regulatory catch. Under EU Regulation 2022/1426, a fully driverless vehicle type can be registered at most 1,500 times a year across the EU, and Germany's national route allows 250. Grünheide is planned for up to a million. The cap is not meant to last: in June, UNECE published the first global rulebook for automated driving systems, and it sets no volume limit. But it only applies in Europe once the EU writes it into its own rules, and there is no date for that yet. The plant is also a bet on Brussels, with 2029 as the deadline.
Context: the Cybertruck makes room. As we reported in CW47, Tesla will wind down Cybertruck production in Austin at the end of summer 2027, and Musk has said the space goes to more Cybercabs. Read together, Austin and Grünheide are the same decision on two continents: Tesla is reallocating manufacturing capacity from cars people drive to a car nobody drives.
A Model Y without a steering wheel
The Cybercab has two seats. Musk addressed that at the New York Times DealBook Summit on Wednesday: for groups of four to six, or a whole family, you still need a bigger autonomous vehicle, and Tesla plans an autonomous version of the Model Y with no steering wheel and no pedals, for six or seven people.
There was no timeline, no price and no word on where it would be built. Six or seven seats points to the stretched three-row Model Y L, which Tesla launched in the US in July at $61,990, or to the regular Model Y with its $2,500 seven-seat option.
The real constraint is regulatory: Tesla self-certified the Cybercab as compliant with federal safety standards, NHTSA responded with a Special Order in September, and Zoox took the slower route of a Part 555 exemption, which caps it at 2,500 vehicles a year. A seven-seat Model Y without controls would walk into the same question.
TAD in Europe: stricter rules than at home
There is also news on Tesla Assisted Driving (TAD), the European name for what is still called FSD (Supervised) in the US.
The Dutch vehicle authority RDW, which granted Tesla’s original European approval in April, signed off on an update on 16 November that tightens driver monitoring:
Earlier attention warnings
A strike immediately if the driver looks away during a manoeuvre
Hands on the wheel above 130 km/h
Setting a speed above what the system displays has to be confirmed by scrolling the wheel, and the speed limit sign pulses red when the car exceeds it
This comes on top of the condition Germany set in October: the speed margin was cut from 50% to 10% over the limit, and the product was renamed.
Why does Europe get this and the US doesn’t? Because the two systems work in opposite order. In Europe, driver assistance software has to be approved before it reaches customers, so the regulator can set conditions first. In the US, nobody pre-approves it. Tesla self-certifies, and NHTSA steps in after complaints and crashes. Electrek reports that Tesla has in fact loosened driver monitoring in the US recently.
The fair way to read this: the US model gets new features to customers faster and relies on enforcement afterwards. The European model is slower and puts the burden of proof up front. What this week shows is that Tesla can build stricter monitoring whenever a regulator asks for it, which will make it harder to argue in the US that it can’t.
Italy says yes
On Friday, Italy’s transport ministry granted provisional approval for TAD, as Deputy Prime Minister and Transport Minister Matteo Salvini announced on X. Italy recognised the RDW approval under Article 39 of the EU type-approval regulation, after what Salvini called a thorough technical feasibility check. Tesla Europe followed with a one-liner and a video of a car driving itself through Rome, and says rollout begins soon.
Italy is the 12th European country to allow TAD, after the Netherlands and ten others (Belgium, Croatia, Czechia, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Latvia, Lithuania, Slovakia and Slovenia). It is also the first of the EU’s big markets.
Source: Reuters
Why that matters for December, in population terms:
The Netherlands plus the ten others: about 70 million people, roughly 15% of the EU
Italy alone: about 59 million
With Italy, the share of Europeans who can legally use TAD nearly doubles, to close to 30%
The EU committee that decides on bloc-wide recognition (the TCMV) moved the Tesla vote to December, at the earliest. A yes needs a qualified majority: at least 15 of 27 member states, representing at least 65% of the EU population. Germany said in October it would support approval after the rename and the speed cap. Germany plus the twelve countries that have recognised TAD come to about 47% of the EU population, if all of them vote yes.
So the gap is still about 18 percentage points. France refused a national approval in July, and Spain has not recognised the Dutch approval. One of the two, plus a few smaller states, is what December comes down to. As in CW42, I think the vote turns on the feature, not the name, and Italy has just made the math considerably easier for Tesla.
Zoox arrives on Uber in Las Vegas
Since last Monday, Zoox robotaxis are available through the Uber app in Las Vegas. It is the first time the Amazon-owned company has opened its service to a third-party platform.
How it works:
You can’t select a Zoox directly. If you request UberX, Uber Comfort, Uber Electric, Uber Comfort Electric or Wait & Save, you may be matched with one
You can raise the chances by opting into autonomous rides under “ride preferences” in the Uber app settings
Uber plans a dedicated autonomous option later, “as availability and reliability improve”
Uber will not provide maintenance or other fleet services for Zoox in Las Vegas
Uber and Zoox also signed a multi-year partnership with MGM Resorts, whose properties serve as pickup points
Next: Los Angeles, with initial operations for employees and friends this year, commercial service in 2027, and rides on Uber next year as well
Earlier this year, the Nevada Transportation Authority approved a permit allowing Uber to deploy up to 1,000 robotaxis in Clark County through Motional and Zoox. Dara Khosrowshahi called Las Vegas the first US city where riders can reach two fully driverless partners through Uber (Dubai already had two, CW46), and described the Zoox ride as “an absolutely delightful experience.”
Not everyone agrees. Grayson Brulte, who rides every service in the market for Road to Autonomy, reported a ride in Las Vegas this week with several near-misses and said he would not ride again unless something fundamentally changes. That is one rider’s experience. The structural point behind it is more important.
Zoox is still not comparable to a Waymo service, because it runs on fixed stops. In August we counted 15 fixed pickup points in Las Vegas (CW34). You are picked up and dropped off at predefined places, such as the MGM Grand or the airport, and can’t be taken anywhere inside the operating area. Waymo, in the same city, drops riders anywhere in its ODD, including curbside at the airport. So on Uber, a rider plays what Brulte calls “autonomy roulette”: they may get a Zoox, and if they do, it may not take them where they wanted to go. For a tourist attraction, that is fine. For a ride-hailing product, the number of stops is the product.
There is more Uber news this week, and it comes from its partnerships with Chinese virtual driver companies.
Uber and Momenta start in Munich
We start in my home country. Uber and Momenta have launched a supervised robotaxi pilot in Munich, the first time riders in Germany will be able to book a supervised robotaxi ride through the Uber app. The details, from the announcement and Handelsblatt’s reporting:
Where: northern Munich, to start in a radius of about 4 kilometres around the Olympiapark, with plans to expand gradually across the city
Booking: potential riders can pre-register in the Uber app now. Registered riders requesting UberX, Electric or Comfort in the zone may be offered an autonomous ride, with access granted in phases. The first passengers are expected in the coming weeks
Price: free
Stops: pickups and drop-offs at designated locations
Safety: a trained safety operator on board every trip
Vehicles: the three test cars approved by the Federal Motor Transport Authority (KBA) are from Chinese manufacturer SAIC. No German cars, at least to begin with
The permit belongs to Momenta. In July, Momenta became the first Chinese company cleared to test Level 4 vehicles on urban roads across Germany without city-by-city applications (CW32). That fits what we found in the KBA register in CW47, where Uber itself held no permits. And because Momenta’s permit covers all of Germany, expansion beyond Munich now depends more on demand and local coordination than on a new national approval.
This puts Uber and Momenta ahead of the other two players pushing into Munich. Uber’s second partner in the city, Autobrains from Israel, only has Level 3 test permits so far. Waymo announced Munich at the end of August (CW36) and is driving without passengers. Khosrowshahi had already told Handelsblatt last month that the pilot would start this year.
Some detail worth knowing about Momenta itself: its driver assistance systems are fitted to more than 1.4 million vehicles, including the China models of Mercedes-Benz and BMW, and it works with Audi and Volkswagen. It trains its Level 4 models on the data its Level 2 systems collect, most of which comes from Chinese cities, which is one reason Bavaria matters to it. Uber increased its stake during Momenta’s Hong Kong IPO in July.
So the first Uber robotaxi in Germany is a Chinese car with Chinese software, and its route passes BMW’s headquarters.

Uber puts $50 million into Dubai
 The company committed $50 million over the next two years to expand autonomous services in the city, with new services available by the end of 2027:
Door-to-door autonomous rides in Jumeirah and Umm Suqeim, for example from Kite Beach to Madinat Jumeirah, without fixed pickup and drop-off locations
New service areas in Business Bay, DIFC and Downtown Dubai
An on-board specialist at first, before fully driverless commercial service
All subject to regulatory approval
Uber’s Pradeep Parameswaran described the money as going into operational infrastructure, local partnerships and a hybrid fleet. The RTA framed it as a step from pilots and limited operations towards wider commercial deployment.

Bolt picks Helsinki, snow included
Bolt announced a partnership with the Helsinki Region Transport Authority (HSL), the cities of Helsinki, Espoo and Vantaa, and Fintraffic to pilot fully driverless (Level 4) shared vehicles in the Helsinki region, starting in 2027. 
The planned areas:
Central Helsinki
A corridor between the city centre and the airport
Ring roads connecting parts of Espoo and Vantaa
Two things make this one interesting.
The weather. Bolt’s Charlotte Eisner named snow, ice and long dark winters as a real test for autonomous driving. That is not a small point. Even Waymo’s broadest California approval this year, covering all speeds, rain, fog and hail, carved out widespread snow or ice. Denver only became Waymo’s first cold-weather commercial market in September. Helsinki in January is a different league.
The role in public transport. The pilot is designed to sit inside the public transport system, from first and last mile connections to on-demand service in the suburbs. One of the city partners described the goal as getting people from home to the closest rail station, so they can leave their own car behind. That is the feeder model we have discussed several times on Autonomy Insiders, with Holo [ https://substack.com/redirect/72309b97-f61f-4b4a-b951-a680f0d90d28?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ], which runs autonomous services for Ruter in Oslo, and with Henriette Cornet on how transit agencies should approach robotaxi operators [ https://substack.com/redirect/775d1cce-bb17-4708-8a7e-c46ee8ce76e1?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]. 
What the release doesn’t say: which autonomous driving technology Bolt will use. Bolt chose NVIDIA’s Alpamayo stack and Lucid vehicles for its main European programme and works with Pony.ai in Luxembourg. For Helsinki, no driver has been named yet.
What this means and what to watch
First, California may be setting a new bar without a new law. The DMV wants to see proof that a fix works before Waymo returns. Whatever evidence satisfies it will be asked of Zoox and Tesla next. Watch whether the DMV publishes its criteria, and whether other states keep waiting for California.
Second, the pause is a utilization story. Waymo’s fleet keeps growing while its network shrinks to surface streets. Watch Q4 ride numbers, airport availability and deadhead miles in the next CPUC report.
Third, Tesla is building for a rulebook Europe hasn't finished yet. A million-car Cybercab plant only makes sense if the EU lifts its 1,500-vehicle cap before 2029. Watch the building permit filing in Brandenburg, any move by the Commission to adopt the UN rules, and, separately, France's and Spain's positions before the TCMV vote on TAD in December.
Fourth, Uber is collecting autonomous supply faster than it can turn it into a product. Zoox in Las Vegas and Momenta in Munich both start with matching by chance, fixed stops and limited areas. Watch when Uber switches on a dedicated autonomous option in Las Vegas, and how quickly Munich moves from free rides with a safety operator to paid ones.

In its Q3 business update, Swedish autonomous trucking company Einride reported contracted annual recurring revenue of $167 million, up 76% since June, more than 40 customers (eight new since June) and about 8,500 hours of contracted operations without safety drivers, up from 5,400. It holds driverless permits in four countries and five US states, including the KBA permit for its cab-less truck running daily for Lidl on a German public road. Q3 revenue was $18.3 million, up 27% quarter over quarter. Einride runs more than 260 electric and 10 autonomous trucks today, plans to deploy most of its 500 Tesla Semis in 2027, and targets 1,500 to 2,000 vehicles and cash flow break-even by 2028. Fitting for this week: highway autonomy is only planned from the first half of 2028, with its next-generation driving system being developed with NVIDIA.

Driverless mining trucks at 5,400 metres in Tibet
SANY has deployed 60 of its 70-tonne battery-electric SKT105ED haul trucks, running autonomously on Eacon’s technology, at the Zhibula Copper Mine in Tibet, at an elevation of about 17,700 feet. At that altitude, the argument for autonomy is not only labour cost: Eacon cites research showing that most workers at high-altitude mines suffer low blood oxygen during night shifts, and a remote operations centre can supervise the fleet from lower ground. Eacon says it runs more than 3,100 autonomous trucks across more than 40 mines.

NTT Mobility tests a bus without HD maps in Japan
NTT Mobility will start public road tests of an autonomous electric bus (built by eVersum) in February 2027, using the AI driving system of Israel’s Imagry, which works with cameras and without high-definition maps or LiDAR. Testing starts at Level 2 with a driver supervising, around NTT’s Musashino R&D Center, with performance evaluations from April 2027. NTT says it is the first mapless approach of its kind in Japan.

Mercedes gets KBA approval for Level 2 point to point
Mercedes-Benz says it is the first manufacturer in Germany to receive KBA approval for assisted point-to-point driving under UN Regulation R171. Its MB.DRIVE ASSIST PRO supports the driver along the entire navigation route, including in city traffic, lane changes, turns at intersections and stopping and starting at traffic lights. An early access programme for selected customers starts in December in Stuttgart and Munich, with market launch planned for the first half of 2027 and over-the-air updates for cars with the right hardware. The price isn’t known yet. The interesting part is the route. Mercedes got its approval under R171 itself, from the German authority, so it needs no EU vote. Tesla's TAD went through an exemption under Article 39 of the EU type-approval regulation, which is why it depends on individual countries recognising it and on the TCMV in December. If both clear their gates, German drivers could compare the two systems in 2027.
