Leverage

As we all try to work out what AI will do to different industries, it's obviously useful to look back at the internet, and I think one framing that's useful is to suggest you can split the impact into three.

There were some industries where we all know that the Internet changed everything (recorded music, or retail, say), and there were also industries where the internet really was just a useful chain or tool, and changed very little. If you were in construction or mining, email was great, but you didn't have new internet-enabled mining companies. The interesting part, I think, is in the middle. If you were an airline or a bank, the Internet changed a great deal about how you went to market and presented yourself to your customers, ut ultimately, the business of an airline is renting the planes, buying the fuel, earning the landing slots, and selling the seats, and the business of a bank is making a spread between deposits and loans, and the internet did not fundamentally change what those were or how they were done. It was fundamentally incremental.

** Wall Street questions
------------------------------------------------------------

The two fundamental changes in AI this year so far have been firstly the emergence of agentic coding with explosive product-market fit, and secondly that capex has outgrown the cash flows of the hyperscalers, pushing them to the capital markets to raise funds (and giving the capital markets the decision on how far this goes). Indeed, the scale of spending is now a conversation for macroeconomists. That’s a particular issue for the neoclouds, which claim (following Jensen Huang) that they are completely safe investments because the chips underneath have massive demand and act as gold-plated collateral. But if you’re worried that the whole segment could have a price collapse, then that argument doesn’t work. Spreads widen. However, it does strike me that there’s a circular argument here: if Wall Street stops funding the capex because it’s worried there’ll be a glut, then the capex stops and there won’t be a glut 🙃. ** Anthropic numbers
------------------------------------------------------------

Anthropic’s upcoming IPO is starting to look like the big test - is it too big to fail? Revenue (gross) was apparently $16.8bn in Q3, up from $11.5bn in Q2, $4.8bn in Q1 and $4.85bn in all of 2025, and the company claims to have a positive operating profit before SBC, but that slowing growth means the target market cap might pull back from $2tr to $1.5tr. Stepping back, as I’ve said repeatedly, the current unit economics of these companies really aren’t a great guide to what will happen next: we don’t know where supply or demand or model training will go. ** Tesla 2017
------------------------------------------------------------

Way back in 2017, Tesla showed a ‘Roadster’ supercar, launched the empty shell of one on a SpaceX rocket as a stunt, and took a bunch of $50,000 deposits. The car never actually shipped. This week, Tesla held another event to launch another completely different Roadster supercar. Again, there was a stunt, but a much more impressive one: Tesla rigged a mock-up of the car with a bunch of nitrogen gas thrusters and put on a stage show with this mock-up doing backflips and somersaults. This time it’s taking deposits of $250,000 - again with no promise of when or even whether you’ll ever actually get a car. The company claims that it will eventually sell a version of the car with those thrusters, by invitation only, and obviously not road-legal.

I once described Elon Musk as a bullshitter who delivers, sometimes, and this is the perfect example - a bunch of very cool stunts to promote the relaunch of a product that was promised and never delivered, and this one might never be delivered either - but meanwhile there is the car company and the self-landing rockets. He reminds me a lot of Henry Ford, who was also both a brilliant industrialist and a racist. LINK (https://us.list-manage.com/eMwrNCkuxoS?e=bc4ee99da9&c2id=e2956131aa985567d31359cb60722597)


** Apple’s kitchen computer
------------------------------------------------------------

I sometimes put things at the end of the news section that I think are both interesting and irrelevant, and Apple’s HomeView, a $300 HomePod mini with a screen fits both. This is Apple’s entry into the kitchen computer segment, which has been a tar pit for 30 years: tech companies keep making things because it fits their strategy and it looks like there’s a space in the market map, but none of them ever get used as much more than a kitchen timer, because the gap on the market map does not line up with a gap on any actual consumers sense of what they need. This one looks the same.

Apple’s pattern is to take spaces that haven’t worked and do them right, but I can’t see that here. The launch video is one of the worst pieces of product marketing I’ve seen in years. This is supposed to tell us why the product is great and how it solves our problems, but here the HomeView makes things worse every time it appears, and every use-case would be done better with a phone. The crowning absurdity is when the teenage girl messages her secret boyfriend that the family doesn’t know about… from the shared screen in the kitchen, surrounded by her family.

Meanwhile, the device itself needs a power cable, so in this video there’s a hole drilled in the middle of the kitchen counter to plug it in. Nothing about this product makes much sense, except as the output from internal strategy and politics.
