# Waymo's Secret Plan for Berlin & Bolt's 25,000 Lucid Vehicles for Europe

👋 Welcome back everyone,
Waymo had another strong expansion week, and this one was the broadest in the company’s history. Las Vegas opened to the public as the fifteenth city. Tokyo got a 2027 date and two named partners. Singapore got a 2028 date and a government. Berlin turned out to have been in preparation all along, which nobody outside the Berlin Senate administration knew. So this is one story, and it runs from Las Vegas to Wolfsburg: what it takes to put a robotaxi on a road, and who can currently afford to.

Waymo’s big expansion week, Bolt’s 25,000 vehicles for Europe and another SPAC arrives
Waymo opens Las Vegas, and it becomes one of the most contested robotaxi markets in the US
On Monday, Waymo began opening its robotaxi service to the public in Las Vegas, a few weeks after receiving final approval from Nevada transportation regulators. That is a fast turn from approval to riders.
The launch follows the same playbook as the previous fourteen cities. Public riders are invited on a rolling basis, so members of the public request an invite and wait for an access code. Waymo typically opens the service to everyone within a month or two of the official launch.
It starts with “dozens” of the new Ojai minivans, serving an area that covers the Strip south of Highway 589 and into Boulder Junction. Fleet and service area are both expected to scale from there.
Source: Waymo
Las Vegas is Waymo’s fifteenth market, alongside Austin, Dallas, Denver, Houston, Los Angeles, Miami, Nashville, Phoenix, San Diego, Tampa and the San Francisco Bay Area, plus San Antonio.
Source: Waymo
Now the part that makes Vegas interesting. Zoox is already running a commercial service there. Tesla and Motional are both planning to launch one. That makes Clark County one of the only markets in the United States where four separate robotaxi operators are either live or committed.
And the paper capacity is extraordinary. The Nevada Transportation Authority approved commercial robotaxi operations for Waymo, Tesla and Uber. Waymo is authorised for up to 1,000 autonomous vehicles over the next year. Tesla is allowed up to 5,000. Uber is allowed up to 1,000, which it will run through its partnerships with Motional and Zoox. Together that is up to 7,000 robotaxis approved for a single county over twelve months.
Nobody expects those numbers to be reached. Tesla’s own Cybercab chief engineer, Eric Early, told state regulators in August that the company would be “extremely happy and satisfied if we could get ourselves up to 2,500, maybe a bit higher than that in the next year.” Waymo is starting with dozens. 
One more detail, and it is the one I keep coming back to. The fleet operations in Las Vegas are run by Moove, Waymo’s long-standing operator partner. That lands one week after Nashville went live on the Lyft app with Flexdrive running the fleet end to end, which we covered in CW38.
Two launches, two cities, two operators, two completely different operating models. Moove as a contracted specialist. Flexdrive as a demand platform’s own subsidiary. 
I remain genuinely curious about which of these operators, if any, convinces Waymo to make it the operator of choice, and whether Waymo ever wants such a thing. If you want the full map of that layer, who does what, which deal structures exist and who works with whom, our deep dive on the fleet operations layer is here: Fleet Operation Overview [ https://substack.com/redirect/85dfee76-a10f-4899-8379-2b9a67cfa97a?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]
But Waymo was not only busy in the United States. The next stop takes us to Japan.
Waymo, Nihon Kotsu and GO have officially agreed to prepare the commercial launch of an unmanned Tokyo ride-hailing service in 2027
On the same day as the Las Vegas opening, the three companies announced they have formally agreed to prepare for a commercial, fully driverless ride-hailing service in Tokyo in 2027.
Waymo arrived in Tokyo in 2025, and the partnership itself was announced in December 2024, when the company began adapting its technology to left-hand traffic and dense urban conditions. Over the past year, Waymo vehicles have been driving autonomously through the city supervised by trained Nihon Kotsu crew members, learning narrow neighbourhood roads and dense crowds.
What changes now is the commitment. The partnership is actively expanding local operations and working with government ministries to secure the permits and licences a commercial service requires, while validating the Waymo Driver and the joint operations under the same safety framework used across the fifteen US cities.
The service will be available through both the GO app and the Waymo app. That structure should look familiar, because it is the one we covered in CW38 in Nashville, the first market anywhere where Waymo vehicles were available on both the Waymo app and a partner app, one vehicle pool with two front doors. Tokyo would make it the second, and the first outside the United States, although the announcement does not say whether a single fleet gets dispatched dynamically across both networks the way it does in Nashville. It launches with an initial fleet and expands gradually to around 100 vehicles across key Tokyo neighbourhoods.
Each partner brings a distinct piece. Waymo brings the driver. GO brings the mobility platform and rider reach, and Hiroshi Nakajima, GO’s president, was explicit that users will hail both conventional taxis and autonomous trips through the same app, and that this launches as a full commercial service rather than a trial. Nihon Kotsu brings nearly a century of Tokyo taxi operations, and its president Yasuharu Wakabayashi framed the initiative as coexisting with human-driven taxis while addressing future driver shortages.
The timeline is explicitly conditional. Waymo’s stated intent is to serve its first fully autonomous trips to public riders in Tokyo in 2027, contingent on securing regulatory approval from national and local authorities and on completing validation. Japan has made this easier than most: Tokyo has already designated zones for the early introduction of Level 4 services, and the demographic argument, an ageing population and a structural driver shortage, is doing real political work in a way it does not in the US.
We set a standard for announcements like this one. An international expansion is an ambition until the cities, the regulators and the local fleet partners are disclosed. Madrid cleared that bar in CW38. Tokyo clears it too: named partners with operating track records, named regulatory process, named distribution, named fleet trajectory, named year.
What it does not clear is scale. Around 100 vehicles across Tokyo is roughly what Waymo runs in Nashville today, in a metro area with about fourteen million people. That is not a criticism of the plan, it is a description of how early this still is outside the United States.
And Tokyo is not Waymo’s only stop in Asia.
Waymo goes to Singapore
Three days later, Waymo announced Singapore, in partnership with the Ministry of Transport and the Land Transport Authority, with a fully autonomous, all-electric commercial ride-hailing service planned via the Waymo app in 2028.
The roadmap is phased, and the details are all published.
In the coming months, an initial fleet of all-electric Jaguar I-PACE vehicles arrives while Waymo establishes local operations.
In 2027 comes the readiness phase. Trained autonomous specialists conduct initial manual driving, adapting the Waymo Driver to local road geometry and monsoon weather patterns. The FAQ is more specific than the blog post: Waymo intends to manually drive and map all regions of Singapore, collecting the data needed to seek Land Transport Authority approval for its autonomous driving system in 2027. Only after that period of manual driving does it begin autonomous driving with trained local specialists behind the wheel, and only then fully autonomous operations.
Operations will run 24 hours a day, seven days a week, to ensure the technology can handle all road conditions. Data from testing will be processed under Singapore’s Personal Data Protection Act.
The public commercial launch is intended for 2028, through the Waymo app.
The vehicles are the detail most coverage skipped, and the fine print matters. Singapore starts with the all-electric Jaguar I-PACE running the fifth-generation Waymo Driver, with future platforms added as the fleet expands. Read the announcement carefully and the I-PACE is the mapping vehicle, not the service vehicle.
Grayson Brulte of Road to Autonomy draws the inference and I think he is right: Singapore is likely to become an IONIQ 5 market. Singapore is home to Hyundai’s innovation centre, and it is the plant Motional’s line-fit IONIQ 5 robotaxis already come out of. A market where the vehicle is built locally, in a country that has just made you its newest AV operator, is not a market you serve long-term with a Jaguar shipped in from somewhere else.
Source: LinkedIn/Motional
Now the thing worth pausing on, and it is in this week’s own Quick Takes.
Grab is expanding its Singapore autonomous fleet from 10 to 50 vehicles over the next six months. Its current fleet has driven more than 110,000 kilometres autonomously and served more than 12,000 unique riders. It is preparing an on-demand, point-to-point service in Punggol which opens to trial riders in the coming months and to the general public in the fourth quarter of 2026, at which point commercial fares apply. The technology partner is WeRide. Pony.ai is also carrying public riders in Singapore through ComfortDelGro’s Zig app.
So Waymo’s commercial launch is 2028. A Chinese virtual driver starts charging fares in the same city this year, roughly two years earlier.
Jeffrey Siow, Singapore’s Minister for Transport, chose his words precisely: “Singapore welcomes Waymo’s entry as our newest autonomous vehicle operator.”
Newest. Not first.
That is the pattern I think is worth naming, because it inverts the story everyone knows. In the United States, Waymo arrives first and everyone else follows it into the market. Outside the United States, Waymo is arriving into markets other people already operate. Singapore has WeRide and Pony carrying riders. London has Wayve and Uber. Tokyo is the one place where Waymo genuinely got there early.
Waymo’s technology lead is not in question. Its first-mover position outside the US mostly is.
But Waymo was not finished with Europe either.
Waymo’s secret plan for Berlin
At the end of August, Waymo announced Munich as its first EU market. 
This week we learned the plan was larger than that.
According to documents from the Berlin Senate administration obtained by Manager Magazin and SPIEGEL, Waymo has been intensively preparing a robotaxi operation in Berlin in parallel. Originally, market entry in the capital was meant to happen at least as fast as Munich. Until a few weeks ago, Waymo planned to map the Berlin city area this year with up to 50 vehicles in test mode, and to launch commercially in 2027.
A Senate administration memo from the end of May states that Waymo would “probably be the first provider of a commercial service (robotaxi) in Berlin.”
An administration spokesperson confirmed that talks with Waymo are indeed already “far advanced,” and that from the administration’s point of view, a start of test operations this year is “possible.” Waymo said only, in general terms, that it would “very much like to bring the service to more places across Germany,” but that it has nothing to announce at present.
Two details from the reporting are doing more work than the headline.
The first is that Waymo was apparently the first company to approach the Berlin administration with a concrete vehicle-scaling plan and a business plan detailed enough to work with. On permitting questions, the parties were far advanced and had found solutions for all open points. A spokesperson for the Senate administration for transport: “We are technology-open and have our own interest in testing such robotaxis in Berlin’s street space.” Other providers active in Germany, including Volkswagen’s MOIA in Hamburg running Mobileye technology, have not yet reached the threshold of being able to offer a service without a safety driver.
The second is the surprise. The Berlin administration reportedly only learned shortly before the end-of-August announcement that the Americans intended to focus on Munich first. Waymo is said to have explained the decision with internal processes.
Which means the plan we are now reading about is one Waymo has already changed once. That is worth stating plainly, because it is easy to read this reporting as “Berlin is coming.” What it actually evidences is that Waymo ran two German cities in parallel, got both to an advanced administrative stage, and picked one late.
And then there is the wall, which is the part that matters most and which no amount of ambition solves.
To run a commercial robotaxi service in Germany, Waymo needs an operating permit for Level 4 vehicles from the Kraftfahrt-Bundesamt, an approval from the responsible state authority for the specific operating area, and a commercial licence under the Personenbeförderungsgesetz, the law governing taxis and rental cars. The PBefG currently contains no clause for robotaxis. If the reform under discussion does not arrive in time, Waymo would have to operate as a rental-car provider, as Uber partly has, unless it receives an exemption, which would be limited to five years.
So a lot more than algorithms will decide whether Waymo really gets going in Germany. Which brings us to the piece of the German problem Waymo can actually solve on its own.
Waymo partners with Allianz Partners for insurance in Europe
On 15 September, Waymo announced a partnership with Allianz Partners to build the insurance, claims and safety research foundation for its European expansion, beginning with its initial deployment plans in London and Munich.
Three workstreams, all disclosed:
Insurance and risk solutions. Tailored coverage and risk management for Waymo’s autonomous fleet, including fleet insurance, liability protection, and new insurance models designed specifically for autonomous mobility.
A claims ecosystem. Digital claims handling and management.
Research and safety collaboration. Joint research with the Allianz Center for Technology across crash analysis, safety modelling and risk evaluation.
The companies will also explore operational solutions to streamline vehicle recovery tailored for autonomous fleets, which sounds minor and is not. Recovering a vehicle with nobody in it, from a road you do not control, is one of the genuinely unsolved operational problems in this industry.
The safety data underneath the deal is the 16x reduction in serious injury crashes compared with human drivers in the cities where Waymo operates.
Tomas Kunzmann, CEO of Allianz Partners, framed it as combining Waymo’s technology leadership with Allianz’s insurance, assistance and vehicle safety expertise. Tilia Gode, Waymo’s Head of Risk and Insurance, gave the sentence that explains the timing: “As we advance our commercial rollout in Europe, building the right operational foundation is just as critical as the technology behind the Waymo Driver.”
This is a bigger asset than it looks, and the reason comes from Episode 10 of Autonomy Insiders [ https://substack.com/redirect/4b24883b-26ea-4329-8195-a57939331e12?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]. Chris Moore of Apollo ibott, who underwrites this market, put it about as bluntly as it can be put: you can build the safest self-driving car in the world, but if you cannot insure it, it does not go on the road.
The counter-intuitive finding from that conversation is the one that applies here. The United States is, in underwriting terms, easier than Europe and the UK, despite nuclear verdicts and a hostile plaintiff bar, because European motor liability is unlimited. An insurer taking on a driverless fleet in Europe is accepting an uncapped tail. Which makes a named European insurance partner a materially harder thing to secure than a US one, and a reasonable proxy for how seriously a company is treating the continent.
Allianz has been here before, incidentally. It insured Singapore’s first driverless bus. The same insurer now appears in Waymo’s European risk layer and in the Asian market Waymo announced two days later.
I find it genuinely good to see that Germany sits high on Waymo’s expansion list rather than being an afterthought behind a dozen US cities. I am already looking forward to sitting in a Waymo in Germany. 
To run any of these expansions, though, Waymo needs vehicles. The answer so far has been the Ojai from Zeekr, which comes with constraints because it comes from China. Everyone has been asking when the Hyundai IONIQ 5 arrives. This week we got the answer.
The first IONIQ 5 Waymo robotaxis will be delivered in the fourth quarter
Hyundai confirmed it at its 2026 CEO Investor Day, in a single line that most of the coverage of that event skipped over.
The first IONIQ 5 Waymo robotaxis will be delivered in the fourth quarter. They are assembled at Hyundai Motor Group Metaplant America in Georgia, using a local supply chain, drawing on the group across manufacturing, design, software and finance.
Then the sentence that tells you what this is for: “These vehicles will enable an international expansion of robotaxi services as early as 2027.”
Hyundai also confirmed that its own Motional will use the robotaxi-ready IONIQ 5 when its commercial service launches later this year as driverless. Motional’s target for that launch is the Uber network in Las Vegas. So the same vehicle line, out of the same Georgia plant, supplies both Waymo’s international expansion and one of the three competitors preparing to launch in the market Waymo opened on Monday.
The wider Hyundai picture is worth thirty seconds because it is the context for the robotaxi line. The group reaffirmed a 2030 global sales target of 5.55 million units, raised its consolidated operating profit margin target above 9%, and is growing North American manufacturing capacity by 500,000 units while lifting its 2030 local parts sourcing target from 60% to 80%. José Muñoz, President and CEO of Hyundai Motor Company, described the strategy as “becoming a physical AI company which will produce and deploy robots and robotaxis.” On the software side, Hyundai is standardising its sensor architecture around the NVIDIA ecosystem so that data from Hyundai, Kia, 42dot and Motional can be integrated to a single consistent standard.
Now, what this actually changes, because it is not a new deal.
We covered the deal itself in February, under the headline “The Supply Breakthrough”: Hyundai supplying Waymo with 50,000 IONIQ 5 by 2028, for a reported figure of around $2.5 billion, built at the Georgia Metaplant. What arrived this week is the delivery date.
Start with cost, and let me be transparent about where the numbers come from, because neither of them is official.
Divide the reported $2.5 billion by the 50,000 vehicles and you get roughly $50,000 per unit. Neither Hyundai nor Waymo has published a per-vehicle price. That figure is arithmetic on a reported contract value and a reported volume, and either could be wrong.
The other side of the comparison is just as constructed. Back in CW15, when the US imposed 100% tariffs on China-built EVs, we worked through what that did to Waymo’s Zeekr plan. We took the pre-tariff estimate of roughly $50,000 for the Zeekr RT6, applied the tariff, put the landed cost at around $100,000, and concluded it broke the expansion math. Waymo has never disclosed what it pays Zeekr, so that was our estimate too.
Hold both caveats and the comparison is directional rather than precise. A Georgia-built IONIQ 5 plausibly lands at something close to half a tariffed Chinese-built Ojai. I would not defend the exact figures to anyone. I would defend the direction, and the direction is the argument.
Line-fit, and why it matters 
The detail I would actually lead with is how the vehicle gets built. It turns out to be the thing neither company will confirm.
The Ojai arrives from Zeekr as shells, stripped of Chinese connected-car technology, and receives the sixth-generation Driver in Arizona, where Magna handles the upfitting at its Mesa facility. A line-fit vehicle would be a different object: the integration would happen on Hyundai’s own production line in Savannah, and the car would arrive ready.
Grayson Brulte of Road to Autonomy put that question directly to Hyundai’s corporate communications this week. Line fit, or upfit at Magna in Mesa? Hyundai declined to comment.
So we do not know. 
Here is why the answer matters more than the delivery date does.
When we retired the vehicle-supply framing in CW35, on MoffettNathanson’s shipping-receipt work showing 725 Ojai entering the US in July against roughly 300 in commercial service, we named the replacement constraint: upfit throughput. One month of imports was more than twice the entire deployed Ojai fleet. Waymo had solved getting vehicles. It had not solved turning vehicles into working robotaxis at the same rate.
If the IONIQ 5 is line-fit, it does not add a second lane into that bottleneck. It routes around it entirely.
If it is upfit at Mesa, it joins the same queue, and the constraint sits exactly where CW35 left it.
What we do know is that Hyundai can build these cars line-fit, because it already does. Motional has been showing line-fit IONIQ 5s arriving from Hyundai’s Singapore plant with no retrofit required. The capability exists. Whether Waymo gets it out of Savannah in Q4 is the open question, and Walter Piecyk’s read, which seems reasonable to me, is that the first vehicles are upfit and line-fit comes later.
That is the thing worth measuring in Q4.
Then there is the part of the Ojai problem that is domestic rather than international.
The 2025 connected-vehicle ban is the reason those cars arrive as shells stripped of Chinese technology in the first place. That is a US rule, written for US roads. And a Chinese-built robotaxi carrying paying passengers is a considerably more awkward object in Texas, Florida, Tennessee, Arizona and Nevada, which is most of where Waymo actually operates, than it is in California. Industry observers have called the Ojai a political liability in red states before, and nothing since has made that less true.
A Georgia-built car, assembled with a local supply chain, simply does not carry that exposure. For a company operating in fifteen US markets, several of them in states where the politics of Chinese technology are live, that is worth real money and real trouble avoided.
While Waymo keeps laying the groundwork for its expansion, other players with a German focus have entirely different problems.
MOIA cannot find investors, and its future is uncertain
As recently as mid-July, MOIA chief Sascha Meyer was enthusiastic. Volkswagen’s shuttle service had just hit its next milestone. The ID. Buzz vehicles, upgraded with Mobileye technology, had begun carrying passengers through Hamburg in autonomous test operation. 
In the background, things had stalled badly.
Manager Magazin reported previously that Volkswagen CEO Oliver Blume had been searching since last year for co-investors for the shuttle service. This week it reported that the first attempt at that search has failed.
Several parties confirm that the last two interested companies, Uber and Lyft, have walked away.
That leaves MOIA and the autonomous driving unit ADMT, led by Christian Senger, with what amounts to hope for a complete reset: a new technology partner beyond the Israeli company Mobileye, who would ideally also take a stake in MOIA as an investor to rescue the project. At group level the response was carefully non-committal: “We are examining all options.”
Here is what the deal was supposed to look like, and where it broke.
Several parties had looked at the VW package over the past year. Talks went furthest with Uber and Lyft. For the end of 2026, VW even announced a joint shuttle offering with Uber in Los Angeles, although with a safety driver aboard and not fully autonomous.
The investors were supposed to come in, according to people around Wolfsburg, with “a few hundred million euros” up to an agreed next milestone. From Uber, fresh money would then have come around 2027. Volkswagen would have benefited additionally because the partners would also have paid for the use of the vehicles.
The visions of the shared future were, in places, far apart. Do Uber or Lyft pay per kilometre or per vehicle? How long are they tied to the VW vehicles? Which vehicle model do you use at all? Contentious too was how Volkswagen would participate in further financing. Volkswagen apparently wanted the partners to gradually compensate the more than €2 billion Volkswagen has invested so far. Only then would the partners contribute new money of their own.
“Their expectation, though, was that Volkswagen should keep investing too,” an insider said. “The brand boards in particular shook their heads at that.”
MOIA has been loss-making since the project started in 2016 under Blume’s predecessor-but-one, Matthias Müller. With Mobileye supplying the complete self-driving technology for the electric buses, everything took longer than wanted. Which is why, a year ago, Blume quietly started test drives in shadow mode with a second partner: the British AI start-up Wayve.
And the timing of all this is brutal. While Waymo comes to Munich and pushes ahead with Berlin, nobody invested in MOIA.
Getting out will be expensive
Going it alone is not the plan either. There is a “very positive perspective” for the business model, says one person at the top of the company, but it only becomes genuinely lucrative in the 2030s. The company would probably have to invest at least another billion euros to make MOIA commercially ready, and it could be more. Volkswagen needs that money elsewhere right now.
So a new idea is circulating. Cariad, Volkswagen’s software subsidiary, which was supposed to develop autonomous driving systems for the group’s passenger cars independently of MOIA, has broken off its cooperation with the supplier Bosch and is currently also looking for new partners. The favourites, according to Manager Magazin, are the newcomer Wayve and the most valuable company in the world, NVIDIA. The US semiconductor giant Qualcomm has also been mentioned at times. Why, the thinking goes, should they not also take a stake in MOIA? They could come in with a significantly higher share than was ever envisaged for Lyft and Uber.
Giving up MOIA is regarded inside the group as a humiliation to be avoided. Extreme solutions are now being considered, including a complete handover to Uber. If that does not work, and if Blume and his people cannot find a solution with the possible technology partners either, it could get expensive. An exit threatens a write-down in the billions.
I have been fairly sceptical about Mobileye for some time. Across all the projects running Mobileye technology, I am not aware of a single one that has demonstrated genuine driver-out operation.
The ID. Buzz AD itself is a different story. I have heard a great deal of good about that vehicle, and it lands well with the people who have ridden it. Which is exactly why I have been wondering for a while when MOIA, or Volkswagen, would put a different technology partner behind it.
According to Manager Magazin, shadow test drives with Wayve have already happened. A Wayve and Volkswagen combination would create a European solution, and some would find that very appealing as a sovereign answer. 
And if you then put those vehicles onto Bolt, you would have a complete European chain, which obviously has a lot of charm.
Which brings us to Bolt, and to the other European vehicle announcement of the week.
Bolt and Lucid partner to deploy 25,000 robotaxis in Europe
On Thursday, Lucid Motors and Bolt announced a partnership that could bring robotaxis built by the California company to the continent.
Bolt “aims to deploy at least 25,000 fully autonomous vehicles” built on Lucid’s upcoming mid-size EV architecture, which is meant to be smaller and cheaper than its current models.
The division of labour, as announced: Bolt intends to own and operate the fleet. The two companies will work together from the product-development stage on an autonomous-driving-system-ready vehicle platform. The vehicles will use NVIDIA’s autonomous vehicle compute and sensing architecture, Hyperion.
Markus Villig, Bolt’s founder and CEO, gave the framing: “Autonomous driving in Europe requires data, software, vehicles, and operations to work as one system built for European roads and regulation. Lucid brings a world-class platform, and together, we will co-design the vehicle and software based on our data and more than a decade of operating experience across more than 850 cities.”
Now the context that makes this more than a press release.
What is happening here is that Lucid is building a second business as a robotaxi OEM, on top of its premium vehicle business, with support from Bolt and NVIDIA.
Look at what the companies actually said they will do. They will work together from the product-development stage on an autonomous-driving-system-ready vehicle platform. That is a vehicle designed for autonomy from the beginning rather than a retrofit. NVIDIA supplies the compute and sensing architecture underneath it. Bolt supplies the demand, the operations and the balance sheet for the fleet.
Napoli has been fairly direct about why Lucid wants this. He has said the robotaxi business would carry margins that “vastly” exceed those of “the traditional retail model.” That is a chief executive describing a second business line, not a partnership announcement.
And Bolt is not Lucid’s only robotaxi programme. Lucid has spent the past year working with Uber and Nuro on a robotaxi version of the Gravity SUV, due by the end of 2026, and Uber’s commitment on Lucid vehicles runs to as many as 35,000 units as part of its $10 billion autonomy programme.
Put the two together and the picture changes shape. Lucid now has stated demand of up to 35,000 vehicles from Uber in the United States and at least 25,000 from Bolt in Europe. Roughly 60,000 robotaxis, two demand partners, two continents.
That is the kind of order book that justifies building a purpose-built robotaxi platform in the first place.
Bolt’s own side is still worth noting. Its target of 100,000 autonomous vehicles on its platform by 2035 is not new, and this announcement is explicitly described as a “major step toward” it. That target came from Charlotte Eisner on the very first episode of Autonomy Insiders, where she also gave the shape of the curve, four-digit fleets in four to five years and six figures after 2030, and where she was clear that Bolt is willing to hold the asset rather than rent it. As she put it, “we don’t hide away from CapEx.” The press release confirms Bolt intends to own and operate the fleet.
Bolt already has the other pieces around it: Pony.ai as its first virtual driver partner, though explicitly not an exclusive one, Stellantis on the vehicle side.
What we do not know is at least as interesting.
No software partner was named. The companies said the vehicles will use NVIDIA Hyperion for compute and sensing, and then said nothing at all about who supplies the driver. Lucid declined to offer anything beyond the press release. Worth knowing that Bolt has its own unit in this space, Bolt AD Solutions, a dedicated autonomous driving division that works with vehicle manufacturers and autonomous driving technology providers to co-design, integrate and commercialise autonomous mobility for Europe.
The most common speculation I have heard this week is Nuro, and it is the obvious guess. Lucid already works with Nuro in the United States on the Gravity robotaxi with Uber, so the relationship, the integration work and the validation history all exist.
I do not think it will be Nuro.
I am not going to pretend to more certainty than I have on this one, and I am deliberately not naming who I think it will be. But the obvious answer is rarely the one that survives contact with a deal this size, and the next few weeks and months are going to be genuinely interesting on that question.
Whoever it turns out to be tells you what this partnership actually is.
We also do not have a timeline. No money has changed hands, and Bolt has not placed an order for the vehicles.
My own read: Bolt is very well positioned, and this announcement underlines something I think is under-appreciated about them. They want to be more than a demand platform, and they back themselves to sit at the table while the vehicle is designed rather than taking whatever an OEM offers. That is a harder and more valuable position than aggregating rides.
The team supports it. Bolt has hired experienced people from the AV world, including from Zeekr. Eisner herself helped develop the purpose-built Waymo vehicle during her time at Geely and Zeekr before joining Bolt, which is about as direct a piece of relevant experience as exists for co-designing a robotaxi. And from my own conversations with these people, they know exactly what they are doing.
I am genuinely curious to see how this develops.
In the United States, meanwhile, it is SPAC time again.
May Mobility to go public via SPAC
May Mobility is merging with ACP Holdings Acquisition Corp, a special purpose acquisition company established by the Houston-based investment manager Atlas Credit Partners. The deal could raise more than $300 million for May Mobility at a valuation of $1.4 billion.
The money comes from two instruments: a $120 million private investment in public equity, and up to $217 million from the trust account ACP Holdings maintains.
That second number carries a caveat worth stating clearly, because it is the one that gets glossed over in every SPAC announcement. Shareholders in the SPAC may choose to redeem their stock at the time of the merger. If they do, the amount actually reaching May Mobility falls. The $300 million figure is a ceiling, not a floor.
Once the merger completes, May Mobility says it will be the first public company in the United States focused entirely on autonomous ride-hailing. That is the company’s positioning, and it is meant to separate it from the other public companies working on autonomy, including Tesla, Rivian, Alphabet through Waymo, and the trucking-focused Aurora, Einride and Kodiak. 
The business model is the more interesting part. May Mobility pitches itself as asset-light and partnership-first. Rather than owning and operating the robotaxis, it sells its autonomous vehicles to its fleet partners over time while keeping control of remote supervision and software updates. In exchange it receives either fixed fees or per-trip licensing fees.
The operating base underneath that: founded in 2017, autonomous Toyota Siennas in three US locations, a partnership with Lyft in Atlanta, and rides in Eden Prairie and Grand Rapids in Minnesota. More than 550,000 paid autonomous rides to date, covering more than one million miles, with three cities running driver-out, though those remain relatively limited deployments. A first trial deployment in Japan. Commercial launches planned in Arlington, Texas with Uber, at the end of this year or in early 2027.
And the financials, which are the reason this listing is worth your attention at all: around $10 million in revenue last year, against a cash burn of around $93 million. The filing also shows roughly $450 million raised since 2017.
The roughly $300 million ceiling, at last year’s burn, is a bit over three years of runway, assuming no redemptions and no increase in spending. Neither of those is a safe assumption, and the stated use of proceeds points the other way: more R&D, particularly on removing its safety drivers, supply-chain investment to reduce bill-of-materials cost, and new geographic deployments.
The single most interesting number in the filing, though, is the one about the vehicle.
May Mobility’s bill of materials is $270,000 per vehicle, and roughly half of that is the autonomy kit. The company is targeting a 50% reduction by 2028, which would bring it to about $130,000.
Set that against the rest of the field, using the comparisons Walter Piecyk drew on Autonomy Markets this week. Tesla is believed to be under $30,000. Waymo, with the Ojai and its sensor stack, is probably under $100,000. So May Mobility is currently building a robotaxi that costs roughly nine times a Tesla and close to three times a Waymo. Grayson Brulte’s summary: it “makes the Jaguar look like a bargain for Waymo.”
The fair caveat, and Piecyk makes it himself, is that $270,000 across a small number of vehicles largely reflects development cost rather than a steady-state production number. That is true. It is also exactly why the 2028 target is the thing to hold them to.
It does explain the use of proceeds, though. When the company says it will spend on supply-chain investment to reduce bill-of-materials cost, we now know what it is reducing from and what it is aiming at.
This is also not the first AV company to take this route, and the roster is instructive. Aurora went public via SPAC in 2021. Kodiak followed at $2.5 billion. Einride completed its de-SPAC and now trades on Nasdaq as ENRD. And Plus is attempting it again, in what is actually its third attempt rather than its second: Hennessy in 2021 seeking around $3.3 billion, then Churchill Capital IX at roughly $1.2 billion, terminated in April 2026, and now Texas Ventures III at $800 million.
May Mobility at $1.4 billion belongs on that ladder, and time will tell where.
For what it is worth, I welcome these listings, and I have said so consistently. Public markets force disclosure. We know May Mobility’s revenue, its burn, its ride count and its mileage this week only because a listing obliges someone to publish them.
What this means and what to watch
Las Vegas is the market that tests the commissioning thesis in public. Four operators either live or committed, 7,000 vehicles approved on paper, and Waymo starting with dozens against a national fleet of 4,000. The constraint is not permission. Watch how fast Vegas goes from dozens to hundreds, because that is the CW35 question being answered in a market where three competitors are watching the same clock.
Watch whether Tokyo confirms the two-app structure. In CW38 we said Nashville was the only market anywhere with a single Waymo fleet dispatched across two apps, and that a second city would make it a template rather than a one-off. Tokyo is now the candidate, and it would establish that template internationally before it has been repeated at home.
Germany is a legislative problem, not a technical one. The thing to watch is the PBefG reform. If the reform lands with a robotaxi clause, Waymo can run a real service. If it does not, Waymo runs as a rental-car provider or on a five-year exemption. And watch whether Berlin comes back at all. What was reported this week is a plan Waymo already revised once, and the Berlin administration found out late. A second German announcement inside twelve months would tell us the Munich-first decision was sequencing. Silence into 2027 would tell us it was a choice.
Track the IONIQ 5 commissioning rate through Q4, not the delivery announcement. Deliveries starting in Q4 is the easy part. The question CW35 left open is how fast vehicles become revenue-earning robotaxis. The specific thing to watch is whether these cars arrive line-fit from Savannah or go to Magna in Mesa for upfitting, because Hyundai will not say, and the answer decides whether the Arizona bottleneck gets relieved or simply gets a longer queue. And watch where the Ojai stops appearing: if new US markets open on the IONIQ 5 rather than the Ojai, particularly in the states where Chinese hardware is a live political question, that tells you the vehicle’s origin was doing more work in the decision than either company has said.
Watch whether MOIA’s technology partner and its investor turn out to be the same company. That is what Volkswagen is now hoping for, and it is a materially harder deal than the one that just failed. Uber and Lyft were being asked to buy distribution. NVIDIA or Wayve would be asked to buy a loss-making operator and supply its stack, and the reason to do it is strategic rather than financial. 
The ADS provider is the announcement. Until we know who supplies the driver, we do not know what Bolt and Lucid actually signed. The consensus guess is Nuro, on the strength of the existing Lucid relationship in the US. I do not think it will be Nuro, and I would watch for that name before watching for the vehicle, because it decides whether this is a conventional supply deal or something structurally new.
Watch May Mobility’s redemption rate at the merger, not the headline valuation. Money on the slide is not money in the bank, and at a $93 million annual burn the difference between $120 million and $337 million is the difference between eighteen months and three and a half years.

💡 Quick Takes
Zoox’s 100-robotaxi cap in Nevada expires this month
The Nevada Transportation Authority permit that limited Zoox to 100 robotaxis in the state expires on 25 September, confirmed to TechCrunch by an NTA official and a Zoox spokesperson and set out in an updated permit. Zoox currently runs about 100 of its custom-built, steering-wheel-free robotaxis across four US cities, with the bulk in Las Vegas and San Francisco, and Robotaxi Tracker estimates more than 30 of them are operating in Las Vegas. The company declined to say how many it will add, saying only that it plans to increase the fleet steadily over the next several months “to meet the high demand in Las Vegas and other markets.” Two things make this worth more than its word count. First, Las Vegas is the only city where Zoox charges for rides, so the market that just lost its ceiling is also its only revenue market, and California approval to charge is still outstanding. Second, look at what the real constraint now is. Zoox began charging in Vegas last month under a temporary NHTSA exemption from certain federal motor vehicle safety standards, needed because its vehicle has no steering wheel or pedals, and that exemption permits up to 2,500 vehicles per year for two years. The state cap that just disappeared was 4% of what the federal exemption already allows. Zoox’s binding constraint moves from a regulator to its own production line, in the same month Waymo opened and while Tesla and Motional prepare. That is what a contested market looks like from the inside.

At IAA Transportation 2026 in Hannover, Pony.ai revealed a Level 4 autonomous heavy-duty truck developed with GAC Commercial Vehicle, built on GAC’s T9 battery-electric platform with a fully redundant drive-by-wire chassis. Volume production begins later this year, for long-haul freight, dedicated-route logistics and port transportation. The sensor kit runs nine lidars, three millimetre-wave radars and 13 cameras, and uses the same domain controller as Pony’s seventh-generation robotaxis. The numbers that matter: the bill-of-materials cost of the autonomous driving kit is down 70% versus the previous generation, transportation cost per ton-kilometre is expected to fall 30%, and energy consumption is expected to drop 10%. Pony signed the cooperation agreement with GAC on 16 April and completed testing and validation within five months. The company plans to take the robotruck business into Europe and the Middle East over the next two years.
