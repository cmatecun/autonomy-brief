Waymo opens three cities to everyone, and arrives at Nashville’s airport
Since Wednesday, anyone in San Antonio, San Diego and Tampa can download the Waymo app and hail a fully autonomous ride. Until now, Waymo let riders in on a rolling basis. According to Waymo, the three cities have welcomed “hundreds of thousands of riders” since service opened earlier this year.
In San Antonio, that includes the airport. As we reported in CW15, San Antonio International became Waymo’s fourth airport and its first in Texas back in April. What changes now is who can use it: anyone with the app, rather than a selected group of riders.
Nashville got its airport this week too. Waymo now offers fully autonomous rides to and from Nashville International Airport, with pick-up at the Ground Transportation Center at the Central Terminal. Waymo is ramping up gradually and will tell Nashville riders in the app when they have access.
Airport rides matter more than their share of the map suggests. Airport trips make up roughly 15% of Uber’s bookings, at two to three times the revenue of an average ride, and the demand runs from the first flight to the last. Travellers often have no car with them and luggage in hand. For any ride-hailing business, these are some of the most valuable routes there are.
Denver opened alongside San Diego and Tampa, but Waymo’s announcement does not mention it. Denver is Waymo’s snow market and runs only on the Ojai, so I’d read the slower pace as caution ahead of its first winter. Waymo hasn’t said.
Winter is also the theme of Waymo’s next step, because it is heading north.
Waymo comes to Toronto
Waymo is coming to Canada, starting with Toronto. 
Its new Canada page answers most of the questions you’d ask, so here is what it says.
The permit. Waymo has been approved for Ontario’s Automated Vehicle Pilot Program and has received a provincial permit from the Ministry of Transportation “to begin manual driving and testing.” So for now, trained specialists sit behind the wheel.
The area. Testing will run across select neighbourhoods, including St. Lawrence Market, Harbourfront, Roncesvalles Village, Parkdale, Liberty Village, Don Mills, Yorkdale, Leaside, East York, Summerhill, Yonge & Eglinton, Weston, Mimico and Etobicoke.
The hours. Testing “typically” runs 24 hours a day, seven days a week, “to ensure our technology can safely handle all of Toronto’s road conditions.”
The timeline. Waymo is “working hard” to launch its fully autonomous service in 2028, hailed through the Waymo app.
The vehicles. Waymo starts with “a limited number” of all-electric Jaguar I-PACEs and will scale gradually.
The weather. Toronto is another winter market, and Waymo’s FAQ spends a lot of space on snow. It says Toronto will not be its first city with winter conditions, pointing to Denver and Detroit and to years of testing in Upstate New York, Michigan’s Upper Peninsula and the Sierra. The sixth-generation Waymo Driver has sensor-cleaning mechanisms that scrape and clear snow from the sensors. In a blizzard, Waymo says it will “abide by local guidance,” because there are conditions in which no driver, human or autonomous, should be on the road.
One detail is worth pausing on. The winter capability Waymo describes belongs to the sixth-generation Driver, which runs on the Zeekr-built Ojai. The cars arriving in Toronto are I-PACEs, which carry the fifth generation. So I’d expect the Ojai to follow well before 2028.
The other thing that stands out is the runway. In Denver, Waymo went from removing its backup drivers to public service in three weeks. In Toronto, it is giving itself two full winters before the first paid ride. For a new country, a new regulator and a new climate at the same time, I think that is the right call.
Last week we listed Montreal among the northern cities Waymo is exploring. Toronto is the first Canadian city with a name, a permit and a date.
Element will run Waymo’s fleet in Toronto
Thanks to a press release from Element Fleet Management, we also know who will run the fleet there.
The deal is an extension. Element and Waymo announced a multi-year partnership in June, when Element began supporting Waymo’s operations in San Diego. Toronto is “the next phase,” and it is Element’s hometown.
Through Element Mobility, its intelligent mobility division, Element will manage on-site operations for Waymo’s Toronto fleet. That means coordinating “the people, facilities, infrastructure, and vehicle operations required to keep the fleet ready, available, and operating efficiently.” The release names fleet lifecycle management, vehicle readiness and maintenance, charging infrastructure, energy management and connected intelligence. In short, Element makes sure the cars are charged, maintained, cleaned, serviced and ready to go out.
Element manages more than 1.5 million vehicles worldwide. Waymo’s Nicole Gavel said that together with Element and other partners, Waymo will “invest in hundreds of roles over time spanning fleet maintenance, validation driving, customer service, and other operations functions.”
Waymo can remove the driver. It can’t remove the work, and in Toronto it named who does that work two years before the first paid ride.
Element is also now a repeat Waymo operator, as Moove already is in Phoenix, Miami and Las Vegas. Once an operator has proven itself in one market, Waymo appears happy to take it to the next.
A lot has happened in the fleet operator layer since our last update. Flexdrive opened its Nashville depot, Moove took Las Vegas, and Element now has a second Waymo market. Tesla had other problems this week than expansion.
NHTSA weighs whether Tesla’s Robotaxis speed past schools
A new defect petition accuses Tesla’s Robotaxis of speeding through active school zones, and NHTSA has opened a file to decide whether to investigate.
What it is. NHTSA’s Office of Defects Investigation opened the file, DP26010, on 9 October. The petition reached the agency on 23 September and targets model year 2026 Robotaxis running with Tesla’s Automated Driving System engaged. The evidence is thin so far: three social media videos linked in the petition, and four incidents in NHTSA’s own tally.
What it is not. It is not an investigation, and nobody has found a defect. No crashes, injuries or deaths are part of the file. Under federal rules, NHTSA has 120 days to grant or deny a petition, which puts the deadline around 21 January 2027. If it grants it, a formal defect investigation follows, and those can end in a recall.
It is the third federal action aimed at Tesla’s driverless fleet in about five weeks, after an audit query and a Special Order asking Tesla to prove under oath that the Cybercab, with no pedals or steering wheel, meets federal safety standards. Tesla’s answers were due on 30 September and have not been made public.
The petition also gave us something Tesla never has: a federal number for the size of the fleet. NHTSA puts it at about 1,000 vehicles, Model Y and Cybercab, and labels that figure an estimate. I’d read it as an upper bound. The Texas AV Tracker counts 803 autonomous Teslas registered in Texas. Registered is not operating, and the 1,000 most likely counts every registration rather than every car on the road. 
Speeding is a big topic for Tesla in Europe as well. One of the main objections to an EU-wide approval of FSD, now called Tesla Assisted Driving, is that the system can drive up to 50% above the detected speed limit. As we wrote in CW41 and CW42, that is the feature the December vote is most likely to turn on.
But the two cases need to be kept clearly apart. FSD is a Level 2+ system. The driver supervises, chooses the driving profile and is responsible for the car. A Robotaxi runs at Level 4 with nobody behind the wheel. As Electrek put it: “When the ADS decides how fast to go past a school, Tesla made that decision.”
Waymo has had its own problems around schools. Late last year, the Austin school district reported Waymo vehicles illegally passing stopped school buses around 20 times. Waymo filed a voluntary software recall in December 2025, and the NTSB opened an investigation in January.
Incidents like these draw particular attention, and rightly so. They involve children, one of the groups we most want to protect on the road. Waymo answered with a recall. We’ll see whether Tesla does the same, or waits for NHTSA to decide for it.
But that wasn’t all.
Tesla’s remote operators crash two more Robotaxis
Tesla’s latest crash reports to NHTSA add two more Robotaxi crashes in which a remote operator, not the autonomous system, was driving. Electrek found them in NHTSA’s September data release. Both happened in August.
In Dallas, a Model Y was stopped on a residential street, wedged between a concrete mixer truck and a parked SUV. “The remote support operator remotely took control and while moving forward, the vehicle made contact with the SUV at approximately 0.40MPH,” Tesla wrote.
In Austin, a remote operator “was in control” of a Model Y driving through an alley, with a utility pole on one side and a dumpster on the other. It hit the dumpster at about 1 mph.
Neither car had passengers on board. Tesla now has 36 unique Robotaxi incident reports on file, up from 22 in July.
In CW36 we counted three crash reports in which a human was driving the car remotely. Now there are five. Every remote takeover that has shown up in Tesla’s crash data has ended in a collision. Five for five.
To keep this in proportion: nothing serious happened. We are talking about bumpers, a dumpster and a tree stump, at walking speed, with nobody hurt. And because crash reports only contain crashes, five for five says nothing about how often remote takeovers go well. Tesla does not disclose how many remote interventions it has.
Still, I think the pattern shows the capability gap quite clearly. Waymo has a tool for the same situation. In a February 2026 letter to Senator Ed Markey, Waymo wrote that it “has not used remote driving or ‘tele-operations’ where a human performs the Dynamic Driving Task.” Its remote staff give the Waymo Driver guidance it can accept or reject. The one narrow exception is a function that lets a trained agent nudge a stuck vehicle forward at 2 mph at fixed steering angles. Waymo says it has never been used outside of training.
Waymo runs more than 4,000 vehicles and over 500,000 trips a week. It has never needed that tool on a public road. Tesla’s fleet is a fraction of that size, even if you take NHTSA’s 1,000, which probably counts registrations rather than cars on the road. And it has already been linked to five crashes with a human driving remotely.
Tesla doesn’t share its remote intervention numbers, so we cannot calculate a rate. But I think this is a pattern worth highlighting.
Let’s switch continents and head to Europe.
Uber, Pony.ai and Verne take robotaxis to Manchester
Uber and Pony.ai have started testing robotaxis in Manchester. The vehicles are mapping the city’s streets with safety drivers behind the wheel. No passengers are being carried yet, and the companies have not said when a public service might launch or how many vehicles are involved.
Manchester is the first UK city outside London where robotaxi testing has been announced. Pony.ai announced it a week after revealing plans to bring its seventh-generation robotaxis to London with Uber. “With nearly 6 million journeys made by Greater Manchester residents and visitors every day, and a strong ambition for more connected, accessible mobility, the region presents an exciting opportunity for robotaxis,” the company wrote.
It is early. Transport for Greater Manchester confirmed it has received no applications to operate driverless taxis in the city region. The UK government opened applications for its pilot scheme for driverless taxi and bus services in May, and Pony.ai and Uber have not said whether Manchester will run under it.
In London, the local politics are already visible. About 15 members of taxi and private hire unions protested outside Wayve’s King’s Cross office this week, after a London Assembly committee warned that robotaxis could worsen congestion and threaten jobs, and TfL made clear that any automated passenger service “can only operate with TfL’s consent.”
What makes Manchester interesting is who else is on board. Manchester reunites the Zagreb trio: Verne is part of the deployment too. The roles are the same as in Croatia:
Uber provides the passengers through its ride-hailing platform.
Pony.ai supplies the autonomous driving technology.
Verne contributes its operational framework.
In Zagreb, the three already offer autonomous rides that customers can book in both the Uber and the Verne app, and since September Verne and Pony.ai have run fully driverless rides for invited riders there.
Last week, writing about London, I said a Verne-style operator behind Pony.ai’s fleet would confirm the Zagreb template. In Manchester, Uber brought the original. Whether Verne is part of the London plan as well, the companies haven’t said.
Let’s go from L4 to ADAS.
Mercedes-Benz and NVIDIA deepen their partnership, and Volvo does too
Days before NVIDIA’s GTC in Berlin next week, Mercedes-Benz and NVIDIA announced that they will extend their strategic partnership on the next generation of automated driving systems. (I’ll be at GTC as well, so if you’re there too and want to talk, send me a message on LinkedIn [ https://substack.com/redirect/9151be54-fc83-4219-9ae5-5756e1e118b2?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ].)
“NVIDIA is our most important strategic partner in automated driving,” said Mercedes-Benz CTO Jörg Burzer. “Together, we developed the first Mercedes-Benz assistance system for urban point-to-point driving for the western world.”
Here is what Mercedes will use from NVIDIA:
Hyperion, NVIDIA’s in-vehicle computing and sensor architecture, working with Mercedes’ own operating system, MB.OS.
Alpamayo Stack, full-stack driving software with AI reasoning, supporting urban point-to-point assistance, active safety and automated parking.
Halos, which adds a parallel safety stack with redundancy and guardrails on top of Mercedes’ own safety foundation.
According to Mercedes, these technologies support advanced driver assistance and “the development of future SAE-Level 4 applications, including robolimousines.” Beyond driving, Mercedes also uses NVIDIA for vehicle engineering simulation and in MO360, its digital production system.
Two things stand out to me.
First, Mercedes now has two partners for urban point-to-point driving. In CW40, Mercedes signed a production agreement with Wayve for Level 2 point-to-point driving within two years. This week it calls NVIDIA its most important partner in automated driving, with a software stack that covers the same function. Mercedes hasn’t said how the two will divide the work.
Second, the Level 4 line. In CW40, I wrote that the signal to watch was a European carmaker signing an end-to-end AI supplier for Level 3 or above, because that would show the black-box concern about AI driving decisions had been answered. The release stops short of that. It speaks of “the development of future” Level 4 applications, and a production agreement would be the real signal. But the release combines an AI reasoning stack, a Level 4 goal and a parallel safety stack acting as a guardrail. That last part is exactly how you would try to address the black-box concern.
Mercedes was not the only one. In the same week, Volvo Cars announced it is expanding its partnership with NVIDIA, which goes back to 2017. Volvo will use the same three building blocks: Hyperion, Halos OS and the Alpamayo Stack. The goal is for future Volvos to drive door to door, from highways to busy city streets to parking, “with a gradual introduction of eyes-off and mind-off driving.” The technology debuts in a next-generation Volvo.
Put that next to last week. According to Der Spiegel, Volkswagen picked Wayve over NVIDIA and about a dozen other providers for the AI software in its driver assistance. So within a month, NVIDIA reportedly lost the software contest at Europe’s largest carmaker and deepened its software role at two of its premium ones.
One player that also bets on L2++ but wants to reach L4 is Wayve, and this week we made a small find.

What this all means and what to watch
First, airports keep moving up Waymo’s list. San Antonio is now open to every rider and Nashville has its airport. The highest-value curb in ride-hailing is being won city by city, and I expect every new Waymo market to have an airport on its roadmap from day one. Also watch Denver: if it opens to everyone before the snow, Waymo is confident in its winter Driver.
Second, Toronto has a date: 2028. Before that come two winters of testing. Watch for the Ojai to appear in Toronto, because the sixth-generation Driver is the one Waymo built for snow, and the I-PACEs arriving now are not.
Third, Waymo now names its fleet operator long before launch, and it goes back to operators it already knows. Element has two Waymo markets, Moove has three. Our updated fleet operations overview is coming soon.
Fourth, NHTSA has until about 21 January 2027 to grant or deny the school-zone petition. If it grants it, a formal investigation follows. Watch whether Tesla files a voluntary recall first, as Waymo did with its school buses issue.
Fifth, Tesla reports Q3 results on Wednesday, 21 October. I don’t expect Tesla to disclose its remote intervention numbers on Wednesday, or anytime soon. Until it does, the crash reports remain the only window we have, and every new one will be read the way this week’s were.
Sixth, Manchester is at the mapping stage. The first application to Transport for Greater Manchester will be the real start. And watch whether Verne also appears in Uber and Pony.ai’s London plans.
Seventh, GTC Berlin. After Mercedes and Volvo, watch which other European carmakers stand on NVIDIA’s stage next week, and whether Volkswagen names a technology partner for MOIA. I think it will be Wayve.

A US appeals court blocks class actions over Tesla’s self-driving claims
The 9th US Circuit Court of Appeals has thrown out an order that let Tesla buyers in California pursue a class action claiming Tesla misled them about the capabilities of its full self-driving technology. The court found too many individual questions about what each buyer actually saw before purchasing. It did not rule on the merits, and the case continues. At its centre is Tesla’s claim that cars sold since October 2016 have the hardware needed for full self-driving, the same promise I described in CW42 as the real obstacle to Tesla ever adding lidar. For now, buyers have to pursue that claim one by one rather than together.

Baidu starts Hong Kong’s first large public robotaxi trial
Baidu’s Apollo will invite more than 1,000 residents of Tung Chung to ride its robotaxis from 21 October to 3 November, the first large-scale public trial in Hong Kong. The route is a 5.1 km loop of about 15 minutes with no stops, and each vehicle takes up to two passengers. Every car carries a backup operator with an average of ten years’ driving experience and at least 100 hours of autonomous driving training, while Baidu also monitors the fleet from its control centre. Hong Kong has moved step by step: a pilot licence in 2024, testing extended from Airport Island to North Lantau, Kowloon East and the Southern district, and driverless tests without an operator on board at Airport Island since July. Baidu says its vehicles have covered more than 280,000 km in the city. The stated goal is to join Hong Kong’s public transport system and move towards regular operations.

Bot Auto puts its autonomy hardware at $50,000 per truck
Houston-based Bot Auto has picked Lenovo’s AD1 domain controller, built on NVIDIA DRIVE Thor, as the production computer for its driverless trucks from the first quarter of 2027. It expects the full autonomy hardware, compute and sensors included, to cost $50,000 per truck. Bot Auto calls that three to eight times less than other systems, though the comparison rests on a trade-publication cost guide rather than on competitors’ own figures. Today its trucks run on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 per server, which founder Xiaodi Hou says is “not the architecture we want to replicate across hundreds or thousands of commercial trucks.” Bot Auto now moves about 350 loads a month across Texas, says revenue grew 270% from May to August, and has deliberately held back fleet expansion “until the compute platform itself is scalable.”

Waabi adds Warrior Logistics in Texas
Waabi and the asset-based carrier Warrior Logistics have announced a commercial partnership on the Dallas to Houston corridor. Warrior has integrated a fleet of Waabi Driver trucks into its network for recurring shipments in food and beverage, consumer goods and other freight. Both companies report 100% on-time pick-up and delivery and zero safety incidents since operations began earlier this year (company figures). Warrior’s CEO frames the trucks as capacity on “the routes that are less desirable for our drivers,” not as a replacement. It is Waabi’s second customer announcement in two weeks, after Volvo and Warp, and Waabi says its load volumes on the route have grown more than 80% this year.

TIER IV and TUM open an Autoware research center in Munich
TIER IV, the company behind the open-source driving software Autoware, and the Technical University of Munich have set up the TUM Autoware Research Center, a multi-year collaboration across three TUM research groups. The work covers a hybrid architecture that combines deep-learning driving models with a verifiable rule-based “safety guardian”, a benchmarking and verification framework, and a repeatable process for adapting Autoware to new cities. TUM’s research vehicle EDGAR already runs on Autoware and was deployed in Berlin within a week to test a new Level 4 assessment method with TÜV-Verband for the German government. The pitch is aimed at European carmakers: an open stack, at a time when the European Commission is working on a concept for an open European software-defined vehicle platform.

TomTom and DeepRoute.ai team up on assisted driving
TomTom and the Chinese company DeepRoute.ai have signed a memorandum of understanding and integrated TomTom’s location data into DeepRoute’s driving stack for L2+ and L2++ functions. A demonstration vehicle drives on open roads in Paris during the Motor Show. DeepRoute frames the deal as part of its global expansion: map data that captures local road layouts and rules helps it adapt to “unfamiliar regulatory and physical environments abroad.” A Chinese AI stack with a Dutch map underneath is a practical way into Europe, and a reminder that location data still matters for AI driving stacks.

JAECOO and Momenta take on the Arc de Triomphe
During the Paris Motor Show, Chery’s brand JAECOO and Momenta drove an 11.4 km route from La Défense through Neuilly-sur-Seine and around the Arc de Triomphe without a single takeover, in a JAECOO 7 test vehicle running Momenta’s R7 World Model. The roundabout is a hard test for any driving system: twelve avenues converge, there are no lane markings, and traffic already circulating has to give way to cars entering from the right. A safety driver monitored the drive throughout, the system is L2++, and the car is still in testing and validation, so this is a demonstration rather than a product. The context makes it interesting. JAECOO only entered France in April and reached a 1.6% market share in September, and the company says the JAECOO 7 was the UK’s best-selling car that month. Momenta already supplies Mercedes in China and works with Uber in Munich. With TomTom and DeepRoute.ai on the streets of Paris in the same week, two Chinese assisted-driving stacks were demonstrating for European customers at the same show.

Dubai tests driverless pods on their own tracks
Dubai’s Roads and Transport Authority has started a four-week trial of Glydways’ automated transit system on Bluewaters Island, together with Dubai Holding. The small electric pods carry four to six passengers at up to 50 km/h on narrow guideways separated from regular traffic, and use 20 lidar sensors plus radar and cameras. According to the RTA, the network could carry more than 20,000 passengers per hour in both directions and cut capital costs by up to 90% and operating costs by up to 70% compared with similar transport modes. Those are projections, and the trial is there to test them. A 2.8 km link from National Paints Metro Station to Bluewaters is among the routes under study. Running on dedicated tracks makes the autonomy problem much simpler than mixed traffic, which is exactly why it may scale faster.

Forterra and Hypercraft show an autonomous vehicle for contested environments
Defense companies Forterra and Hypercraft unveiled the Razorback, Hypercraft’s ground vehicle running Forterra’s AutoDrive software, at the Association of the US Army’s annual meeting in Washington. It offers 300 horsepower, a 500-mile range and up to 38 kW of power for onboard systems. AutoDrive is integrated on more than 200 vehicles in active service, and Forterra says it was the first US ground autonomy company deployed in Ukraine, where it has completed 88 casualty evacuation missions. In August, the US Army chose Forterra as prime contractor for its autonomous mine-breaching programme. After Overland AI’s Marine Corps contract last week, defence keeps producing some of the most concrete autonomy contracts around.
