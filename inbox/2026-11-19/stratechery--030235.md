Good morning,

This week’s Stratechery Interview is with longtime Stratechery guest Eric Seufert ( https://x.com/eric_seufert ). Seufert is, in my opinion, the world’s pre-eminent digital advertising analyst, and Stratechery’s most frequent interview subject ( https://stratechery.com/?access_token=eyJhbGciOiJFUzI1NiIsImtpZCI6IkxzanFRcEhoMUJUdmU0djJQUUVuejIiLCJ0eXAiOiJKV1QifQ.eyJhdWQiOiJzdHJhdGVjaGVyeS5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUiLCJhenAiOiJIS0xjUzREd1Nod1AyWURLYmZQV00xIiwiZW50Ijp7InVyaSI6WyJodHRwczovL3N0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LmNvbS8_cz1lcmljJTIwc2V1ZmVydFx1MDAyNmFtcDtjYXRlZ29yeT1pbnRlcnZpZXdzIl19LCJleHAiOjE3OTc2NzgxNTUsImlhdCI6MTc5NTA4NjE1NSwiaXNzIjoiaHR0cHM6Ly9hcHAucGFzc3BvcnQub25saW5lL29hdXRoIiwic2NvcGUiOiJmZWVkOnJlYWQgYXJ0aWNsZTpyZWFkIGFzc2V0OnJlYWQgY2F0ZWdvcnk6cmVhZCBlbnRpdGxlbWVudHMiLCJzdWIiOiIwMTk1MTZmMS1hMzQ2LTc2MTQtOWU5Ni1iNjNiZDExZmU4NTYiLCJ1c2UiOiJhY2Nlc3MifQ.Xs1E1cOWDlp2bBQaOmG2Ovgk41c8Zu89ybzyCqVFgeqs88rs3t9-JShILf3KPF9eCPn3s1kGvrEcCzMAXZSYlQ&s=eric+seufert ). Seufert writes full-time at MDM.co ( https://mdm.co/ ) , and is well-worth the subscription.

This was one of my favorite interviews I’ve done with Seufert. We see eye-to-eye when it comes to digital advertising, but have some divergences when it comes to agentic commerce. Seufert is adamant that the retailers will make agents that own the experience; my experience argues that consumers will ultimately go to the retailers who welcome agents with open arms.

To that end, in this interview we discuss Seufert’s view — already vindicated! — on agentic shopping, why Amazon is an advertising behemoth across the web, the Meta versus Amazon battle, and how he defines agentic commerce — and where my personal experience disagrees. Then we get into the inevitability of ads in agents, our shared Google optimism, and the current state of OpenAI and Netflix’s ad businesses.

Agentic Shopping

*Eric Seufert, welcome back to Stratechery. As usual, I’m preparing, I’m like, “How has so much happened in six months? We have so much to talk about” — but before we get there, I went to Mobile Dev Memo to prepare for this interview, MDM.co, is this is a rename?*

*ES:* Yeah, you call it a rename, a rebrand. You know, like Prince going into the symbol, a shorter domain. That’s right.

*Shorter domain, also, if you’re talking about advertising, I feel like using an acronym really fits the genre. So there you go.*

*ES:* That’s right, apropos.

*We are, I know you’ve been chomping on the bit for this, we are going to talk about agentic shopping. We’ve talked about agentic shopping for a long time. I think you in particular have been very ahead of the curve, you’ve had a very strong opinion on agentic shopping going back a year. We’ve mentioned this article before, Agentic Commerce Is a Mirage ( https://mobiledevmemo.com/agentic-commerce-is-a-mirage ). This sort of kicked off this whole series of takes from you. I know we’ve talked about it, but given the context of Muse, of Instinct, all these things that are happening, and the fact that we’re going to spend a long time on the topic, why don’t you start from the top? This is your chance to sort of restate your case, and then we’re going to jump ahead to some of the stuff you’ve written more recently.*

*ES:* Yeah, so let me set the stage on the original article, I published that September 2025. And the context of that piece was not Muse, it was not Instant Checkout, those came later, this was Amazon blocking Perplexity.

Now, remember, Perplexity launched probably the OG agent. I’m sure it wasn’t the first, but maybe it was the first that got any sort of attention. And I said, “Of course they did”, why wouldn’t they? Doesn’t that serve their interests? First of all, Amazon has historically had a pretty hostile relationship with third-party browsing agents and it just kind of made sense.

*Right, which has always been like an eternal battle for them. Those things that stick in a coupon code or look for a comparison site, all those sorts of things.*

*ES:* Yeah. And so I made the case that, of course, they would block Perplexity and of course, they would want to protect their first-party relationship with consumers, because that is the source of a lot of the commercial value that is generated by the company, because a lot of the net income is generated from advertising. So if they got disintermediated by an agent, then no one would see their ads and they would lose that source of high-margin revenue, which would be disastrous for the business.

By the way, that revenue funds a lot of other parts of the business that are really good for consumers and so if consumers lost access to those parts of the business, then they’d be worse off.

*If it all went away they would have to raise prices, which would decrease volume, which would actually just make everyone unhappy.*

*ES:* Yeah, exactly. In the agentic commerce outcome that some people are championing for — but I think also that the tenor of this has changed a lot in the last few months.

*Yep, so let’s fast forward. You accurately predicted this in this context — to your credit, you had written this before ChatGPT’s commerce initiative came out, you immediately said it was going to fail. Fast forward a year, is Muse and Instinct more of the same? Give me the path from the Perplexity story to where we are today.*

*ES:* (laughing) Well, so the series is on part five ( https://mdm.co/agentic-commerce-is-a-mirage-part-5/ ) after a year, so there have been a number of twists and turns in the last 13 months. But what you see is Amazon very stridently digging its heels in and saying, “We will not open our platform to third-party agents, that does not serve our interests”.

There was one point — in February, Jassy had made this comment at Davos saying, “We might find a way to work with third-party agents, but it would have to make sense for us, and there’d have to be a value exchange”, and he made a similar comment in the Q1 earnings, or maybe the Q4 earnings, shortly thereafter.

But I wrote another one of the entries ( https://mdm.co/amazon-openai-advertising-not-agentic-commerce/ ) from the series in response to those comments and I said, “The way to do that is with ads, the value exchange is with ads”. This was actually around the same time that there were rumors that Amazon was going to invest in OpenAI, and I said, look, maybe they do invest in OpenAI — they hadn’t committed to that yet — but if they do, I bet the reason will not be because they want to orchestrate some sort of agentic commerce exchange where people can search Amazon through ChatGPT, I bet it’s so that they can insert ads into ChatGPT. And that got announced just recently ( https://stratechery.com/2026/openai-ads-amazon-ads-in-chatgpt-walmart-to-accept-apple-pay/?access_token=eyJhbGciOiJFUzI1NiIsImtpZCI6IkxzanFRcEhoMUJUdmU0djJQUUVuejIiLCJ0eXAiOiJKV1QifQ.eyJhdWQiOiJzdHJhdGVjaGVyeS5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUiLCJhenAiOiJIS0xjUzREd1Nod1AyWURLYmZQV00xIiwiZW50Ijp7InVyaSI6WyJodHRwczovL3N0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LmNvbS8yMDI2L29wZW5haS1hZHMtYW1hem9uLWFkcy1pbi1jaGF0Z3B0LXdhbG1hcnQtdG8tYWNjZXB0LWFwcGxlLXBheS8iXX0sImV4cCI6MTc5NzY3ODE1NSwiaWF0IjoxNzk1MDg2MTU1LCJpc3MiOiJodHRwczovL2FwcC5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUvb2F1dGgiLCJzY29wZSI6ImZlZWQ6cmVhZCBhcnRpY2xlOnJlYWQgYXNzZXQ6cmVhZCBjYXRlZ29yeTpyZWFkIGVudGl0bGVtZW50cyIsInN1YiI6IjAxOTUxNmYxLWEzNDYtNzYxNC05ZTk2LWI2M2JkMTFmZTg1NiIsInVzZSI6ImFjY2VzcyJ9.BHT9ZyvL76ILDGrro-h3ZLUvWQuUmcuSM1hODuh7sOTtQXKoZdlxb-QGqCmxC7tOeiuG5ph4QFRxRUVJA6WWLA ) , and that, I think, is really meaningful and really telling.

I think there are two clues as to what form this will ultimately resolve to. One of them is this ads deal that hasn’t really taken shape yet, but we can kind of hypothesize as to what that might look like, it might just be Amazon inserting ads via its DSP into ChatGPT. But that is win-win — that’s win-win-win, actually. The consumer wins, ChatGPT wins, and Amazon wins. Amazon wins because it gets more surface area to broadcast its advertisers’ ads to.

*And as we’ve talked about, there is no bigger bullish signal for an advertising platform than increased inventory. You actually want the price-per-ad to be going down, because there’s so many more places to put it, that’s how the marketplace dynamics work for these ads, so that is a great outcome for Amazon.*

*ES:* It’s a great outcome for Amazon, because they’re inventory-constrained, they’re not data-constrained, they’ve got more data than anybody else — they’re inventory-constrained and they don’t have a social surface. They’ve infamously failed to generate real traction with any sort of social product outside of maybe Twitch, and the advertising product there has not really gained too much steam.

So everyone wins, ChatGPT wins because they get more demand, so the price is going to go up. Amazon wins because they get to broadcast their advertisers’ ads elsewhere using their data, which means they’ll be very well targeted, because I would assume there’s going to be some sort of account linking — but even if there isn’t, they still get identity, we can talk about that in a second.

And the consumer wins. Why? Previously they got no access to Amazon products in their ChatGPT conversations, now they do. Now they can discover Amazon products. And guess what? They get to discover the most relevant product. Why? Because it was priced, and it was bid against, and it was served by Amazon. That’s win, win, win.

The Amazon Identify Layer

*By the way, a couple questions on this, just in passing — we’re going to continue on this thread. Whatever happened to the Amazon-Meta partnership, where you were going to be able to sign into Amazon on Meta platforms and there would be ads coming straight from Amazon? Did that just peter out completely?*

*ES:* I think so, that was always a test, they did the same thing with Snap. That was the genesis of my Walled Compound ( https://mdm.co/first-party-data-and-the-new-walled-compounds/ ) idea. But the idea persists, I don’t think it went anywhere with Meta, and it didn’t go anywhere.

*Maybe it just makes more sense because OpenAI is a nascent ad business, so Amazon can provide them a lot more value, whereas the hurdle rate for Meta to allow someone else to advertise on the platform is extraordinarily high.*

*ES:* Yeah, it’s just really high, and also keep in mind, in that partnership Amazon was going to select the ad, so Meta was effectively giving up control over what appeared in the feed. But I think what’s interesting about that idea is that it does persist.

The thing about Amazon that you have to recognize, especially in the context of agentic commerce, but even more broadly, is that Amazon is the identity layer for the Internet ( https://mdm.co/amazon-is-ctvs-data-engine/ ). It is the identity backbone for the entirety of the open Internet. That is the open Internet, it’s Amazon, it’s Amazon’s bid, it’s Amazon identity.

*Really? Explain this. Why is it Amazon and not Google or Facebook, which people would assume?*

*ES:* Because they are walled gardens. Amazon has deterministic identity on 90% of US households. And so when you see a deal that Amazon does with Roku, with Disney+, with Netflix, with Tubi, with OpenAI, with Pinterest, the reason that those other parties are willing to allow somebody to inject demand into their bid stream, into their ecosystem, is because Amazon has the identity, they know who they are. And so when Amazon can connect that person to their data set from Amazon Prime, they’re going to target ads better than almost anyone else could.

*Wasn’t this formerly Google’s domain? Are you saying they’re taking over what Google Networks used to be?*

*ES:* Google Networks still exist, but they weren’t doing the targeting — they were effectively allowing the ads to be served, the ads would be served against cookies. But what Amazon is doing is different, because Amazon says we don’t need a cookie.

*Because you’re signing into the Amazon account and linking it to that other service?*

*ES:* You might be. In some cases, you are. In some cases, you aren’t. Amazon says we don’t need cookies, we don’t need device IDs in order to do that identity, we just have all these other signals that we can place the user against.

*Fascinating.*

Muse

*Let’s go back to the question with agents. The angle from Amazon is super clear, especially the serving ads angle — they want to own the user, they want to own the relationship, they collect all the data. The angle for OpenAI makes a ton of sense, they’re building an ad business, and if Amazon can inject basically tons of money into their business by offering way better ads than they ever could, that makes total sense. But then we run into Muse. When I asked about what happened to that partnership, could it be resuscitated in this case? Right now it looks like they’re just in a face-off. What’s your take on what’s happening there?*

*ES:* Here’s my take. I think that Meta internally recognizes that Muse will be monetized with ads at some point. I think if they came straight out of the gate and said, “Hey, we’ve got this totally new consumer paradigm, it’s called an agent, it does work on your behalf, and by the way, you’re going to give it a lot of information about your preferences and your purchasing habits, and then we’re going to monetize your usage of it with ads”, I think there would be some reluctance to adopt it, I think there would be maybe a little bit of a consumer revolt.

*Do you think so, though? Because they came out and said, we’re not going to use our advertising, there’s going to be no linkage of data into Muse, it’s going to be totally separate. And from my perception, no one cared. Everyone seemed to use Muse assuming all their data was going to Meta and it almost seems like this Silicon Valley psychosis about ads claimed its final victim, where the greatest ad platform in the world willingly cut itself off from its hot new product because they assumed users cared, and actually no one cared, and all they did was back themselves into a corner.*

*ES:* It could be, but we don’t know the counterfactual — what would have happened if they had monetized it with ads out of the gate? It may not have been a consumer revolt.

*No, not monetized it. It’s the fact they promised not to. They didn’t promise not to have ads ( https://stratechery.com/2026/amazon-blocks-muse-amazons-moat-aggregator-v-aggregator/?access_token=eyJhbGciOiJFUzI1NiIsImtpZCI6IkxzanFRcEhoMUJUdmU0djJQUUVuejIiLCJ0eXAiOiJKV1QifQ.eyJhdWQiOiJzdHJhdGVjaGVyeS5wYXNzcG9ydC5vbmxpbmUiLCJhenAiOiJIS0xjUzREd1Nod1AyWURLYmZQV00xIiwiZW50Ijp7InVyaSI6WyJodHRwczovL3N0cmF0ZWNoZXJ5LmNvbS8yMDI2L2FtYXpvbi1ibG9ja3MtbXVzZS1hbWF6b25zLW1vYXQtYWdncmVnYXRvci12LWFnZ3JlZ2F0b3IvIl19LCJleHAiOjE3OTc2NzgxNTUsImlhdCI6MTc5NTA4NjE1NSwiaXNzIjoiaHR0cHM6Ly9hcHAucGFzc3BvcnQub25saW5lL29hdXRoIiwic2NvcGUiOiJmZWVkOnJlYWQgYXJ0aWNsZTpyZWFkIGFzc2V0OnJlYWQgY2F0ZWdvcnk6cmVhZCBlbnRpdGxlbWVudHMiLCJzdWIiOiIwMTk1MTZmMS1hMzQ2LTc2MTQtOWU5Ni1iNjNiZDExZmU4NTYiLCJ1c2UiOiJhY2Nlc3MifQ.4P8StyQzN9ynpa0HLpBWukFumtiYdrFhlcGOi-WH90OXJBStekqNhdJ2pZ17dpz435obJr2MPK_bzTyR949oAA ) , they promised not to link your Muse data to your Meta data.*

*ES:* Let me address the question first and then circle back to that, because I think there’s a little bit of a sleight of hand there that we could interpret with proper context.

We don’t know what the consumer reaction would have been if they did monetize it with ads out of the gate and it may not be consumer fear that they were worried about — it may have been the media fear. They’re trying to launch this new product and get traction right out of the gate, and they’re doing it in an environment that’s frankly very competitive. We have a number of agents that have reached some traction — I wouldn’t say critical mass for any of them — but we have a number of agents that have meaningful financial support. We’re going to have more coming soon, I would argue that Google has one, by the way, but we can come back to that and then a commerce agent. So they needed to gain — I don’t know that they need to gain consumers’ trust, but I think they wouldn’t want to do anything to subvert it.

*For sure they won’t launch it with ads, though. But I’m asking specifically — there’s one line in their announcement that says your data will not be passed there. Did they need to do that? I think your point is actually probably right, they avoided a lot of bad media stories, maybe that was the answer.*

*ES:* I don’t know that they need to connect the data in the first place, they get all the data on the CAPI [Meta Conversions API]. If you buy something via Muse and the retailer is an advertiser with Meta, which it will be, it’s going to send the data back to Facebook with the CAPI anyway so they don’t need to actually connect. They don’t need to have one continuous pool, these could be segmented in that way, because anything you do in Muse is going to get sent back to Meta regardless.

*Right. And is this a payoff of how they had to rebuild everything post-ATT, where they actually don’t need direct linkages between everything? They can do good-enough matching, and that gives them actually more freedom of movement?*

*ES:* Yeah, exactly. My understanding is, you know, post-ATT, Meta’s operating strategy was,
“We need redundancy”. We need multiple ways to connect data, and there can never be a total reliance on any single one of them, we need to make sure that we’ve got total coverage. If one of these gets revoked through some legal process or some regulatory process, that we still have coverage with other tools and that’s what they have now.

*Got it. Do you think there was ever a possibility that Muse or Meta and Amazon would have ever revisited their deal or gone the OpenAI route? To be like, “Hey, we said we wouldn’t advertise there, but Amazon, if you want to sign in with your Amazon account, Amazon gets the commands there”, and we’ll sort of backdoor our way into advertising that way?*

*ES:* I have a feeling they will, I don’t know what’s going to be specific to Amazon, but let me talk about — I said that there’s one prototype for what I think this is ultimately going to look like, and that’s OpenAI doing the deal with Amazon.

Here’s the other prototype, it’s Universal Cart from Google. Universal Cart is essentially Google’s agent, it’s a shopping agent, it’s embedded into Google Search, it’s going to be embedded into YouTube, it’s going to be embedded into Gmail.

And what it allows you to do is track prices across a multitude of retailers, they’ve got connectivity with the UCP [Universal Commerce Protocol]. And then when you want to buy something through the retailer’s integration with Universal Cart, they can say the user’s allowed to buy it right then and there. They can buy it through YouTube, they can buy it through Google Search or AI Mode. If that’s more convenient for them, they can do that and then here’s three API endpoints that you ping us with, and we’ll make sure we take care of the transaction.

Or they can say, “No, they’ve got to come to my website”. Why? Because I’ve got ads. I’ve got to fill the cart out, I’ve got to show ads to this person, and so they can collect all the information they want through your tool. But when they’re ready to check out, they’ve got to come to my website. And how is Google monetizing that? Ads. They’ve got Direct Offers ( https://blog.google/products/ads-commerce/agentic-commerce-ai-tools-protocol-retailers-platforms/ ) , they’ve got an ad unit that the retailers can buy to nudge their suggestions up. That’s the form this is going to take.

Now, they don’t call it an ad, and I don’t think Meta is going to call it an ad, they’re going to call it something else. They’re going to call it agentic recommendations, or agentic nudges, or agentic hints — but it’ll be an ad. It’ll be something that someone pays for that nudges that product up in the suggestions and that will be an ad and I do think that’s how they’re going to monetize this.

*Why would retailers agree to the Universal Cart?*

*ES:* You mean checking out from the Google site?

*Why would they integrate with that? Don’t they want the users on their site, putting it in the cart on their site? What’s the game?*

*ES:* Because they’re just going where the people are, they’re getting potentially incremental demand. So they should more or less be totally indifferent if the user discovers their products on Google, added it to the cart in Universal Cart, and then checked out on their page, because they still got the opportunity to show the ad, they still got the opportunity to do upsells and collect all the information, they still maintain the first-party relationship.

Now, the question is, do they get more from let’s say increased conversion of the user checking out right then and there with Google than they would if the user went to their site and checked out? For some retailers — small retailers, DTC retailers, long-tail retailers — the answer is yes. So that’s the question. Where is the trade-off? Where’s that boundary?

For Amazon, it’s absolutely no, we need them coming to our website, we need to maintain the first-party relationship and show the ads. For some small retailer that serves some long-tail thing? Yes, we probably get more with the increased conversion rate than we would if they came to our website and checked out there.

*Is there a problem in a lot of this debate where e-commerce is getting lumped into one big pile, and in fact we need to make very clear distinctions? Amazon is like its own category, then there’s a larger circle that’s like Amazon and Walmart and then there’s kind of like Amazon and Walmart versus everyone else. Is that a good three-circle categorization?*

*ES:* Yeah, so I look at it as Amazon as its own kind of thing and then Amazon and Walmart lump together in the sense that they’re the dominant retail platforms, they’re the dominant sort of e-commerce CPG platforms in the US.

*Right. And the CPG point is interesting. They’re the only two platforms where you can legitimately have a good chance at buying everything that you want.*

*ES:* Right, including grocery, which needs almost immediate fulfillment, which no one else can provide. This isn’t a disciplined hierarchy, but then I’d put Stripe and Shopify kind of in the same bucket as pipes. They sort of do fulfillment, now they’re different, but they sort of do fulfillment in the retail context there, I’d put them in the same category, and they’re willing to work with anybody.
