What The FAA
The timeline was in turmoil over the weekend after the FAA issued a nationwide ground stop that froze flights across the country for more than four hours. And it seems like the entire thing started with a routine router upgrade at an air traffic control communications facility in Arkansas.
When the ground stop began, 3,500 planes were already in the air, more than 7,000 flights were delayed, and by the end of Friday, over 9,000 flights had been canceled. Here’s a minute-by-minute breakdown of what happened over the weekend:
3:10 p.m. ET — Problems start in Chicago. The FAA begins ground stops for flights heading to O’Hare and then Midway as controllers report communications issues.
3:36 p.m. — The problem spreads. Radar feeds and communications start becoming unstable across FAA facilities. Investigators would later trace the problem to network equipment being upgraded by L3Harris in El Dorado, Arkansas, where a router was reportedly flooding the FAA network with roughly 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds.
3:54 p.m. — The FAA stops departures nationwide. Every U.S. airport is told to hold departures until further notice. Roughly 3,500 aircraft already in the air continue flying and landing, but new departures are largely frozen.
Around 5:00 p.m. — Officials rule out an obvious attack. Homeland Security says there is no indication of a national-security threat, while intelligence agencies find no evidence that the disruption was malicious.
5:46 p.m. — The first flights start moving again. The FAA allows recovering control centers to request permission to release individual flights, beginning a very gradual reopening.
8:15 p.m. — The nationwide ground stop is lifted. Investigators isolate the problematic network traffic, block the Arkansas and Texas locations involved, and the system stabilizes enough for the FAA to reopen departures.
Saturday, 11:00 a.m. — The apparent cause is revealed. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy says maintenance work by L3Harris, which was replacing old copper connections with fiber, appears to have triggered the failure. The FAA is still investigating whether the issue came from a faulty router, an installation mistake, or something else.

Jordi: It's immediately a shell of its former self. The culture is very strong and it will endure for some amount of time. But everyone that talks about NVIDIA is saying, "This person will be this part of Jensen and this person will play this other role that Jensen played." What has made NVIDIA so successful is running it in a dictatorship-style. I don't believe there is a CEO out there that can do the dealmaking, navigate the supply chain relationships, manage all of the downstream customers, the hyperscalers, the neoclouds, application companies, open source, and then also do all the engineering and product leadership that Jensen was so good at. Completely irreplaceable.
John: I firmly disagree with this. I think if you find the Tim Cook of NVIDIA to Jensen's Steve Jobs, you can see serious appreciation in the value of the business.
Jordi: If Steve Jobs was running Apple and you still had Tim Cook doing his thing, you might be looking at a $10T company right now. I think the same thing for NVIDIA. Sure, the stock price can appreciate, but 5-10 years from now, there will be a Jensen discount.
