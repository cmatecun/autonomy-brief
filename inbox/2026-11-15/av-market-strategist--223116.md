# Tesla Wants to Sell You a Cybercab & Robotaxi Goes 24/7

👋 Welcome back everyone,
Tesla had one of its busiest weeks of the year, with news on its map, its hours, its Cybercab and its plans abroad, plus a fresh update on the merger with SpaceX. Waymo got an uncomfortable letter from its regulator, Zoox grew in two cities, and NVIDIA released a driving model small enough to fit in a car. In Europe, a robotaxi operator we know well started hiring in Berlin, and the Volkswagen ID. Buzz picked up a new autonomous driver.
We also checked the German testing register again, and some of the names we were looking for are missing.

Tesla had a busy week. So did Waymo, Zoox and NVIDIA
Tesla has caught up with Waymo on the clock and, on paper, on the registry. What it hasn’t matched yet is the proof.
A lot came from Tesla this week, so let’s go through it step by step.
San Antonio: Tesla’s robotaxi city number eight
Tesla opened its Robotaxi service in San Antonio this week, its eighth US city with unsupervised rides. Five are in Texas (Austin, Dallas, Houston, Fort Worth, San Antonio), three in Florida (Miami, Orlando, Tampa). Las Vegas, Phoenix and Jacksonville are listed as coming soon, without dates.
Source: X
The San Antonio map is big:
About 275 square miles, according to EVwire’s reading of the map
About 19 miles across at its widest, east to west and north to south
Centred on downtown, reaching Loop 1604 in the north and beyond Loop 410 in the west, east and south
That makes it the largest launch area Tesla has had so far. For comparison, Fort Worth opened two weeks ago with about 143 square miles (CW46), so San Antonio is almost twice that on day one. In the same week, Tesla also expanded Austin to about 443 square miles, nearly three times the area Waymo serves in the city.
San Antonio is also a Waymo market. Waymo restarted service there in September after a five month pause, which followed incidents in which its vehicles got stuck on flooded roads.
Robotaxi now runs 24/7
A day later, Tesla posted the next step on X: “Robotaxi now runs 24/7. Every city we’re available in.” Musk added: “Robotaxi works 168 hours a week.”
It is the end of a step-by-step extension that has run since the summer:
26 August: 6 a.m. to 10 p.m.
12 October: 6 a.m. to 11 p.m.
21 October (Q3 update): 6 a.m. to midnight in Austin
End of October: 6 a.m. to 2 a.m.
11 November: around the clock, in every city
On the Q3 call, Ashok Elluswamy had said Tesla would “unlock 24/7 operation” over the rest of the year, with the hours “driven by the capability of the AI software.” Musk explained the night-time challenge in early October with a line I think is worth keeping: Tesla is “literally trying to avoid grey kittens on grey tarmac in the dark.”
With 24/7, Tesla now matches Waymo.
Two more data points from this week round out the picture. Tesla’s driverless service areas now cover about 4.2 million people, or 1.2% of the US population, according to Tesla shareholder Sawyer Merritt. And on Thursday, the Texas autonomous vehicle registry listed 1,303 Tesla vehicles (573 Cybercabs and 730 Model Ys) against 1,305 for Waymo, according to the Robotaxi Tracker, which reads the state’s records.
That second number needs a caveat right next to it. Registrations run ahead of the cars riders actually see in service. Austin has more than 400 registered Teslas, while the other cities have between a handful and a few dozen (Fort Worth 24, Miami 5). So the registry counts the cars Tesla has signed up. How many of them drive at 3 a.m. is a different number.
And that is the more useful way to look at 24/7. A schedule says the app takes bookings at night. How many cars answer depends on density. In Austin, Tesla has a little over 400 registered vehicles on about 443 square miles, so roughly 0.9 vehicles per square mile. Waymo runs around 250 cars in Austin, according to its fleet partner Avomo (CW46), on roughly a third of that area, so about 1.7 per square mile. These are rough numbers, and Tesla’s registrations overstate its active fleet. But they show the trade Tesla is making: a much bigger map, with cars spread thinner across it.
How much of the map is real?
One criticism comes up every time Tesla draws a bigger map: drawing a large geofence is easy, serving all of it is not. As I wrote last week, how many cars actually serve those maps is what will tell us how far along Tesla really is.
This week we got the first independent attempt at an answer. Koby Pierce built a Geofence Tracker that compares each service’s advertised area with the area where pickups actually work. He entered real addresses into the app, one at a time, and logged each one as a door-to-door pickup, a walk-to spot nearby, or a declined ride.
The headline result, across six cities (Houston, Dallas, Austin, Fort Worth, Miami, Orlando):
Tesla advertises 810 square miles and offers door-to-door pickups in 555 square miles. That is 69%.
Measured in people, about 2.63 million of the 3.15 million living inside the advertised maps get a car to their door (based on 2020 Census blocks).
Counting walk-to pickups, most cities come close to their full advertised area.
By city, the door-to-door share ranges from 77% in Dallas to 59% in Austin, where coverage was split into 28 pieces with 89 holes. Austin was tested on 4 November, before this week’s expansion.
Source: EVwire via Geofence Tracker
So the critics have a point: almost a third of Tesla’s advertised map does not get you a car at your door.
But the comparison with Waymo cuts the other way. In Houston, Tesla’s real door-to-door area is about 190 square miles, more than three times Waymo’s 55.3 square miles. Waymo’s real area came in at 113% of its advertised 48.9 square miles: its app allowed pickups almost everywhere inside the map, and about 6 square miles beyond it. Waymo offers no walk-to pickups at all.
Two honest caveats. This is one tester, checking addresses by hand, with Census-based population estimates, and Waymo’s advertised shape comes from a third-party map. Treat it as the best independent data so far, and as an approximation. My read: Tesla advertises more than it serves, and still serves more ground than Waymo in Houston. Both are true, and the gap between them is density, which is exactly what the map does not show.
A smaller battery, and the end of the Cybertruck
Musk also talked about the vehicle itself this week. Efficiency gains, he said, will let Tesla use a 40 kWh pack in the Cybercab, about 16% smaller than today’s 47.6 kWh. A smaller pack means less weight and less cost per vehicle, on a car that already is one of the most efficient production vehicle so far, at 6.18 miles per kWh. There will also be a “liquid silver” version, so the fleet isn’t too monotonous.
Tesla also confirmed this week that it will wind down Cybertruck production at Giga Texas. Musk said the line will run through the end of summer 2027 to work through the order backlog, and will then be dismantled to expand Cybercab output. Tesla’s Q3 update already listed installed Cybercab capacity of more than 125,000 vehicles a year.
And Musk floated one more idea: a fully autonomous Cybertruck optimised for deliveries, which he said “could be very useful” for moving cargo within cities. No timeline, no details. But it shows where the factory space is supposed to go: into vehicles that drive without a person.
Cybercabs for private buyers
Tesla also wants Cybercabs on the road outside its own fleet.
On Friday, Tesla invited select customers to order a Cybercab, as a limited release in supported service areas. 
Source: X
Until now, there had only been an interest form for fleet operators, not for individuals. The president of the Tesla Owners Club Austin posted his order email: $29,990, plus a $299 monthly subscription required to operate the vehicle. Elluswamy added that the Cybercab will work unsupervised in all the same locations as Tesla’s robotaxi fleet.
What that means in practice: a car without a steering wheel or pedals that drives itself only where Tesla’s robotaxi service already runs. Outside those maps, there is no fallback, because there is nothing to drive it with.
The contract is unusual. According to the purchase and subscription terms, which were posted on X and analysed by Brad Templeton in Forbes and by Electrek:
No subscription, no driving. “Your Cybercab will not operate without an active Autonomy Subscription.”
The car is for personal use only. Without Tesla’s written permission, it can’t be put on Tesla’s robotaxi network, Uber, a delivery app or a rental platform.
The owner arranges their own insurance and covers most running costs, such as cleaning, charging and parking. Basic maintenance is included, and the car drives itself to the service centre.
Misuse earns “strikeouts.” After three, autonomous driving is suspended for a week, without a refund. Tesla can also suspend or cancel the subscription for “unauthorized or inappropriate use,” or if the owner refuses covered maintenance.
Tesla can remotely locate, restrict or control the car, put it into a “safe state” or end the owner’s access, and the owner has no remedies beyond those in the terms.
A cabin camera above the rear-view mirror collects image data, kept for 30 days by default.
Tesla can change the subscription price with a month’s notice, and can “expand, reduce, relocate, or otherwise modify” the operating area at any time.
Deliveries could start as early as next month. Tesla hasn’t said how many people are on the invite list.
So you buy the car, and Tesla keeps the keys.
It is also a different plan from the one Tesla described in October. On the Q3 call, Ashok Elluswamy said customer cars would get unsupervised driving through the v15 software, as part of Tesla’s geofences and fleet management: “We suspect somewhere around Q2 next year is when we can turn on some of the first customer vehicles to be part of the Robotaxi fleet.” Lars Moravy added that all Cybercabs were going to Tesla’s own fleet for now, with most staying Tesla-owned. What arrived first is the opposite route: a private Cybercab that stays out of the fleet. So Tesla is now preparing two models side by side, private cars that are meant to join the network from 2027, and a private robotaxi that is not allowed to.
One detail makes that sequence interesting. The Cybercab runs on the same AI4 computer as today’s Model Y, and according to Elluswamy on the same software. So hardware isn’t what keeps unsupervised driving out of private Model Ys until 2027. Tesla’s explanation is the fleet side: on the Q3 call, Elluswamy said the only additional requirement is that customer cars can be part of the geofences and the fleet management. Electrek reads it differently: a new car, sold on new terms, lets Tesla start fresh instead of delivering the self-driving it has promised existing owners since 2016. For now, I’d give Tesla’s explanation more weight, because geofenced fleet control is exactly what the Cybercab contract sets up. But if private Model Ys don’t follow in 2027, Electrek’s reading gets stronger.
What does $299 a month buy? For comparison: since February, Tesla only sells FSD as a $99 monthly subscription, and that version still needs an attentive driver. The Cybercab subscription costs three times as much, for driving without supervision, inside the map. Templeton runs the numbers. A typical car drives about 12,000 miles a year, but only around 2,300 to 4,000 of them on the urban streets of its home city, and that is the only place the Cybercab can go today (no freeways, no trips out of town). Call it 250 miles a month, or roughly 15 hours of driving. That puts the subscription at about $20 per hour of driving. If the service area one day covers all 12,000 miles, around 42 hours a month, it drops to about $7 an hour. Over five years, Electrek puts the total at $47,631 for the car and 59 months of subscription, before insurance, registration and charging. And neither side of that calculation is fixed. The price can change on a month’s notice. And the map it pays for can grow, or shrink.
Insurance is the open question. Tesla hasn’t said what it will cost. Without a steering wheel, every at-fault crash is a crash of Tesla’s driving system, and only Tesla has the data to price that risk, so Templeton expects Tesla’s own insurance to end up as the main option. Robocar crashes may also cost more than ordinary ones, because the liable party is a manufacturer with deep pockets. 
There are two ways to read the launch. Read one way, it is a real step: the first car without driver controls sold to private buyers, with Tesla effectively carrying the driving risk. Read another way, it is a small, invite-only pilot in a small service area, with no numbers attached, which can look bigger than it is. And the terms are hard to like from the buyer’s side. Tesla can raise the price, shrink the map, restrict the car remotely or switch off its autonomy, and the owner has few remedies against any of it. The buyer pays $30,000 up front and carries the insurance, while almost all of the control stays with Tesla, for a car that does nothing without the subscription. That is a lot of trust to ask of a customer.
I’d count it as a real step once deliveries reach visible numbers in the coming months. Until then, I’m keeping the verdict open. And either way, the contract is the part I’d read twice before signing.
The open regulatory question hasn’t gone away either. As we reported in CW37, NHTSA opened a probe one day after the Cybercab’s debut into how Tesla self-certified it as compliant with federal safety standards. Tesla had until 30 October to answer under oath. The agency has not announced a finding. Selling the car to private owners raises the stakes of that answer.
While Tesla keeps expanding in the US to keep pace with Waymo, it is also talking to regulators on the other side of the world.
Tesla in Australia
In a submission to the Australian Senate’s Select Committee on Productivity, Tesla asked the government to bring the planned Automated Vehicle Safety Law (AVSL) forward from 2029 to 2027. Its warning: Australia risks falling behind the rest of the world on driverless cars.
Tesla’s argument has three parts:
The framework is slow. The National Transport Commission first proposed an AV framework in 2016, Tesla points out, and progress “remains fragmented and continues to face uncertain timelines.”
Two years earlier is worth a lot. By Tesla’s estimate, accelerating the law by two years could unlock more than $10 billion in safety, productivity, environmental and economic benefits, and save hundreds of lives. Tesla also cites federal government research suggesting autonomous vehicles could save up to 800 lives a year once fully adopted, more than two a day.
Its own data. Based on Australian FSD (Supervised) data, Tesla says collisions fell by 40% compared with Teslas driving without FSD engaged, harsh braking by 90% and harsh acceleration by 86%.
Some context for those numbers. In Australia, FSD is classified as a Level 2 driver-assistance system, so the driver must stay attentive and in control at all times. It launched there in September 2025, and Teslas have since driven more than 160 million kilometres with it activated on Australian and New Zealand roads. That is real usage at scale. But the safety figures are Tesla’s own and have not been independently verified. And comparing FSD-on with FSD-off driving has a known weakness: drivers tend to switch the system on in easier conditions, such as highways, which flatters the comparison.
The hurdles are mostly legal. Without a change in the law, Tesla has no legal pathway to run a commercial driverless service in Australia, and Australia’s timeline has moved before. Earlier targets of 2020 and 2026 have given way to a conditional rollout in selected locations from 2027, and there is still no confirmed date for a full national framework. A spokesperson for Federal Transport Minister Catherine King said only that the Commonwealth is working with the states, territories and the NTC to finalise the AVSL.
Tesla isn’t the only one waiting. Earlier this year, Waymo lodged a “high-level proposal” with Transport for NSW to trial robotaxis in Sydney, set up an Australian branch and met with carmakers about possible partnerships. Questions have been raised about its plan to use left-hand-drive vehicles in a country that drives on the left. Transport for NSW says no decision has been made, and that any service would need “a rigorous trial period” and must comply with the state’s Point to Point transport laws.
But in Europe, Tesla keeps making progress with TAD.
Finland becomes number eleven
Finland’s transport authority Traficom granted provisional approval for Tesla Assisted Driving on Wednesday. That makes Finland the eleventh country to recognise the Dutch approval, after the Netherlands, Belgium, Croatia, Czechia, Denmark, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Slovakia and Slovenia. As we reported in CW27, Finland had already signalled in June that it might move before an EU-wide decision.
Source: Reuters
Traficom named two reasons. The first is the changes Tesla made: the rename from “Full Self-Driving” and the tighter speed settings we covered in CW46. The second is timing. “We wanted to go a little bit ahead to allow drivers to use the system on our roads before the really bad winter conditions arrive,” said Traficom’s Jukka Juhola.
He also explained why Finland felt comfortable with the risk. Only 8,000 to 9,000 Teslas in Finland can run TAD, about 0.3% of the national car fleet. A small number of cars makes a national experiment manageable.
And then the important caveat: Traficom stressed that its decision does not commit Finland to vote yes at EU level. That matters, because a rejection at EU level would invalidate all national approvals already granted.
Here is the CW46 scoreboard, updated. A qualified majority needs 15 of 27 member states representing at least 65% of the EU’s population. If all eleven vote yes, along with Germany and Italy, Tesla would be at 13 countries and about 47%, still two countries and about 18 percentage points short. And Finland has just told us not to count it yet. The next meeting at which a vote could happen is in December.
There is also news on my favourite Tesla topic.
The SpaceX merger: December, or early 2027
I have written about a possible combination of Tesla and SpaceX since CW29. This week Bloomberg reported that the talks are progressing, and that the two companies could announce what would be the largest deal ever as soon as December. A slip into early 2027 has also been discussed. Boards and advisers are working out the terms and the governance of the combined company, including how many SpaceX shares Tesla investors would receive.
The size: SpaceX is worth more than $1.9 trillion after its June IPO, Tesla about $1.5 trillion. Together, roughly $3.4 trillion.
The obstacles are the ones we have flagged before:
China. The companies need a way to ring-fence Tesla’s operations in China. In CW32 we reported the Wall Street Journal story that some Tesla executives had been told to prepare for a possible separation of the China business. Musk called it “fake news.” The issue hasn’t gone away.
National security. SpaceX is a major US government and defence contractor, and Tesla runs a large, profitable manufacturing footprint in China. JPMorgan analyst Rajat Gupta called these “key dynamics that are at odds with each other.”
Politics. In CW44 I wrote that the deal could face more regulatory pressure if Democrats won more seats in the midterms. They did. Democrats took control of Congress and have pledged to look into Musk’s business and political dealings. Bloomberg calls the merger a major test case for political sentiment after the election.
And the reason I keep coming back to this deal hasn’t changed either. As we laid out in CW29 and again in CW44, control is the core of it: Musk holds roughly 20% of Tesla, while SpaceX’s dual-class structure gives him more than 80% of the voting rights. A combination on SpaceX’s terms would change that balance at Tesla.
The next date to watch is Tesla’s annual shareholder meeting on 3 December.
Now let’s move from Tesla to its biggest rival.
Waymo: the school bus problem is back
Waymo had an uncomfortable week with its regulator. NHTSA’s Office of Defects Investigation opened a recall query on 6 November to “assess the adequacy of the remedy” for Waymo’s school bus recall.
Quick recap. As we reported in CW43, the Austin school district flagged Waymo vehicles illegally passing stopped school buses late last year. Waymo filed a voluntary software recall, completed the update on all affected vehicles by 17 November 2025, and the NTSB opened its own investigation in January.
What is new is NHTSA’s finding that a majority of the reported incidents happened after the software fix. Austin ISD officials reported at least five incidents in a letter filed with NHTSA.
Waymo’s response: it has made “significant improvements to vehicle behavior around school buses,” has had no citations since March 2026, and will respond to the query.
In CW43, we presented the recall as Waymo’s answer to the problem. This week the regulator is asking whether that answer was good enough. I think it deserves the same standard we apply to Tesla: a fix is a claim until the incident data says otherwise.
Waymo builds an accessibility network in London
Meanwhile in London, Waymo formed the Waymo Accessibility Network UK with ten British disability organisations, including the Royal National Institute of Blind People, Guide Dogs UK and Age UK. They will help shape the vehicles, the app and boarding before Waymo carries public riders. The commercial launch Waymo has targeted for late 2026 still depends on licensing from Transport for London and approvals under the Automated Vehicles Act.
Another US competitor of Tesla and Waymo also had news this week.
Zoox grows in Las Vegas and arrives in Atlanta
Zoox expanded its Las Vegas service beyond the Strip into Downtown and the Arts District on Thursday. It did three things at once:
A bigger area: Downtown and the Arts District join the Strip, the Convention Center, most major hotels, partner venues such as Sphere, T-Mobile Arena, Resorts World and AREA15, and Harry Reid International Airport, added in September
Longer hours: rides now run from 6 a.m. to 3 a.m., seven days a week
A new vehicle: the redesigned robotaxi unveiled in June, with a lighter interior and more comfortable seating, is now in service. It is the production-intent version Zoox plans to build at scale in Hayward, California, at up to 100 robotaxis a week, subject to regulatory approval
Zoox did not say how many vehicles serve the larger area, or which Downtown destinations were added.
The context explains the timing. The expansion comes days before Formula 1 race week in Las Vegas (19 to 21 November), where Zoox signed a multi-year partnership on 5 November and plans to carry racegoers from 2027. And as we reported last week, Zoox is due to go live on Uber in Las Vegas later this month. That deal was announced in March for “this summer,” so it is running a few months late.
The fleet is still small. The Robotaxi Tracker counted 62 Zoox robotaxis in service in Las Vegas and the San Francisco Bay Area on 5 November, up from 57 three months earlier. CEO Aicha Evans puts the total at about 150 across several US cities. The tracker only counts vehicles its users have spotted, so the truth is probably somewhere in between. Either way, a bigger map with longer hours will be served by a few dozen cars.
In Atlanta, Zoox’s purpose-built robotaxi, with four seats and no steering wheel or driver’s seat, is now on the streets. Zoox has tested in the city since spring 2025 and starts around Atlantic Station, Woodward and Home Park in northwest Atlanta. Public rides are not available yet: the first ones go to Zoox employees and their family and friends, and the “Explorers” early rider programme follows in 2027. Atlanta joins Austin and Miami, where Zoox started carrying employees in March, as its newest markets. Paid public rides still exist only in Las Vegas. In San Francisco, Zoox offers free rides to Explorers members and still needs a California permit to charge.
The real picks-and-shovels supplier of the AV industry also had news this week (next to the news about its now former CEO). This time it isn’t about chips.
NVIDIA: from 34 billion to 2 billion parameters
NVIDIA released Alpamayo 2 Edge, an open driving model small enough to run in real time on DRIVE AGX Thor, its in-car computer.
How it works:
It takes in surround-camera video, navigation instructions and the car’s recent motion, plans a driving path and explains its decisions in plain language. NVIDIA calls this “Chain-of-Causation” reasoning.
It only reasons out loud when the situation calls for it. In routine driving, it outputs a “nominal” signal instead, which saves compute.
It has 2 billion parameters, against 34 billion for Alpamayo 2 Super, from which it was distilled. Latency from camera to trajectory is 160 to 190 milliseconds, depending on how much reasoning it does.
It is available now on Hugging Face.
Source: NVIDIA.
The size is the story. NVIDIA itself describes Super as a development tool for the data centre: a “teacher” that labels data, evaluates and trains smaller models, and that so far has only been validated on an H100 server GPU. Edge is the version meant for the car. It isn’t a finished product yet: NVIDIA calls it “a starting point for fine-tuning,” and every developer still has to test and validate it for its own vehicle. But it is the first Alpamayo model sized for NVIDIA’s in-car computer, and since August the whole family can be used commercially.
That fits what we wrote in CW33: NVIDIA gives away the model to sell the silicon. And it sells it twice, the data-centre GPUs to train and distil, and Thor to run the result in the car. The lock-in is softer than it sounds, though. The weights are open, so NVIDIA’s advantage is the tooling around the model, from the simulator to the deployment recipe for Thor, more than any exclusivity.
On performance, NVIDIA compared Edge with Qwen-Drive-1.0, a model twice its size, and reports 15.15 km between at-fault incidents against 0.25 km in its AlpaSim simulator. That is a large gap, but it is NVIDIA’s own test, in NVIDIA’s own simulator, so I’d treat it as a claim until someone else reproduces it.
Source: NVIDIA
In the same week, Autobrains (EP18 Autonomy Insiders [ https://substack.com/redirect/9dee9030-c3f1-440e-9753-e3b2c62376df?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]) deepened its NVIDIA partnership. It takes the large Alpamayo 2 Super model and distils its reasoning into small, specialised “Driving Agents” that run in real time in production vehicles, so the full foundation model never has to run in the car. CEO Igal Raichelgauz calls this a shift “from a data flywheel to an AI flywheel.” Autobrains points to its Level 4 programmes with VinFast in Southeast Asia and with Uber in Munich. A quick reality check from the KBA register (more on that in the next section): in Germany, Autobrains currently holds four locally restricted Level 3 testing permits, and no Level 4.
And in Seoul, Kakao Mobility, Korea’s largest ride-hailing app, signed an MOU with NVIDIA on the sidelines of NVIDIA AI Day to develop a Level 4 system that meets global standards. Kakao brings its own end-to-end driving models, NVIDIA its computing platform and the Halos safety system. There is no timeline. As we reported in CW32, Korea still runs its taxi system on manned vehicle quotas, with paid autonomous transport limited to designated pilot zones, so the regulatory path matters at least as much as the model.
The last US news comes from Uber.
Uber: parking spots for robotaxis
Uber launched Uber Parking in 15 US cities this week, including Atlanta, Austin, Dallas, Los Angeles, Miami, New York and San Francisco. Riders can find, book and pay for a parking spot from the app’s home screen. It builds on Uber’s acquisition of SpotHero, announced in February, which brings a network of about 13,000 garages, lots and valet services in more than 400 cities.
The first users are commuters who drive their own cars. But Uber was clear that it also wants to use the spots for its AV partners, as staging and waiting locations for robotaxis. That cuts congestion and deadheading, the miles a robotaxi drives empty between rides.
It connects directly to what Uber told us last week. In its Q3 remarks, Uber said it expects “an increasing share of the value” in autonomy to go to “distribution, infrastructure, and commercial execution.” A network of parking spots in exactly the cities where AVs operate is that infrastructure, in its most literal form.
What this means and what to watch
First, Tesla has matched Waymo on hours and, on paper, on the registry. What it has not matched is density. Watch wait times at night, and whether Tesla starts reporting active vehicles or paid miles again, because those will tell us what 24/7 really means.
Second, about two-thirds of Tesla’s map is real, and that is still a lot. The tracker gives critics a number and gives Tesla a defence at the same time. Watch the next round, especially Austin on the new 443 square mile map and San Antonio.
Third, the Cybercab is moving from fleet asset to product. Watch how many invites turn into deliveries, what insurance costs, whether Tesla touches the price or the map, and above all NHTSA’s answer on how the car was certified.
Fourth, December is a big month for Tesla. The EU vote on TAD could come in December, and the merger announcement could too. Watch whether Finland votes yes, and what Tesla’s shareholders hear on 3 December.
Fifth, Waymo’s school bus query is a test of how software recalls work. If NHTSA finds the remedy inadequate, the question moves from Waymo to the recall process itself for AV software.

Verne heads for Berlin, and the ID. Buzz gets a new driver
Two job ads in Berlin
There is news from Berlin.
Two job postings from Verne, the Croatian robotaxi company, appeared recently: a Fleet Operations Manager and an AV Operator, both based in Berlin. 
Electrive asked Verne directly, and a spokesperson confirmed what the ads suggest: “Germany remains a priority market for Verne’s international expansion, as we previously announced. We are beginning to build our operational presence in Germany, and the two advertised roles in Berlin are part of this work.”
The postings tell us what kind of operation Verne has in mind:
The Fleet Operations Manager runs a vehicle depot: the vehicles, the AV operators, workshop technicians, charging infrastructure and spare parts, plus daily maintenance and releasing vehicles for service. Applicants need experience managing a fleet of 20 to 50 vehicles.
One KPI stands out: the number of completed revenue-generating trips per vehicle per day. That is not a test-programme metric. It is the metric of a commercial robotaxi service.
The AV Operator sits in the Autonomous Driving department and looks like a mix of safety driver and test engineer: operating prototypes for data collection, safety and risk assessments, diagnosing problems in the field, and calibrating LiDAR, radar, cameras and GPS.
So this is clearly a robotaxi operator preparing for a launch, with both a test function and a commercial function being set up in the same city.
From a European robotaxi to the Zagreb trio
It’s worth remembering how Verne started, because it wasn’t like this.
Verne was founded in 2019 (as Project 3 Mobility) by Mate Rimac, Marko Pejković and Adriano Mudri, with the idea of a European robotaxi: its own purpose-built, fully driverless two-seater without steering wheel or pedals. In June 2024, it named Germany as one of its first three markets, alongside the UK.
As we reported in CW16, that plan came with a €179.5 million grant from EU recovery funds, with a hard deadline of 31 March 2026. The vehicle wasn’t ready in time. Days before the deadline, Verne brought in ready technology from Pony.ai and a platform from Uber, and launched in Zagreb. I said at the time that it left a bitter aftertaste: EU money meant for a homegrown robotaxi ended up launching Chinese software in a Chinese car on an American app.
What is live in Zagreb today is that combination:
Pony.ai provides the virtual driver, on the Arcfox Alpha T5
Verne owns and operates the fleet, and brought the local regulatory know-how
Uber provides demand: since 19 August, riders can book the service in the Uber app, as well as in Verne’s own app
Since September, Verne and Pony.ai have also offered fully driverless rides for invited riders on a 22 kilometre route between the airport, Verne’s headquarters and the city centre. And Uber has since become an investor in Verne, alongside the Rimac Group, which still holds 42%.
The same trio has now announced Manchester (CW43), where mapping and autonomous testing are already underway, with no launch date yet.
Let’s be precise about what we know and what we don’t.
What we know: Verne is building operational structures in Berlin, and the KPI points to a commercial service. What we don’t know: which virtual driver, which vehicle, when, which operating area, or how many vehicles. Verne hasn’t commented on talks with German or Berlin authorities either.
And there is one thing we can check. I went through the KBA register of testing permits again, with the latest snapshot from 5 November. Neither Verne nor Pony.ai nor Uber holds a German testing permit, at any level, current or expired. A KBA testing permit is the prerequisite for a local approval of a specific testing and operating area. So on paper, Berlin is at step zero.
Will the trio do it again?
Looking at Verne’s partner bench, I think it is plausible that the trio from Zagreb and Manchester will work together again in Berlin.
Pony.ai has said Germany is on its list. When I had CEO James Peng on Autonomy Insiders two weeks ago (EP34 [ https://substack.com/redirect/2ab5e7c0-a614-4841-aa22-18681c5de6a3?j=eyJ1IjoiMXlnZjcifQ.KOUIeBfeCRzhIwFnKXyQigR4j0VpF-SZzascYhSZunw ]), I asked him directly. “The short answer is yes,” he said, and then explained why it isn’t simple. Germany’s 2021 Level 4 law, in his view, is “a regulation for large scale deployment. But we can’t get there until we have a small scale first.” What Germany needs is “more simplified regulation for kicking start level four operation,” and he sees initiatives in that direction. “With that in place, we’re definitely looking into entering some of the German cities. Of course, Germany is the largest economy in Europe. Of course, that’s the market that we have to enter.”
Uber is already active in Germany, with robotaxi programmes in Munich with Momenta and with Autobrains, and it is now a Verne shareholder. Berlin would fit.
But there is a crack in the trio assumption, and it comes from the vehicle. The open question is whether Berlin would get the Chinese Arcfox, as in Zagreb, Verne’s own purpose-built two-seater, or something else entirely. And according to Mate Rimac, Verne’s “final” two-seater is built on the Mobileye Drive platform. So if Verne brings its own vehicle to Berlin, the driver might not be Pony at all. The vehicle question and the driver question are one question.
It is very early, and two job ads are an early sign. But it’s an exciting one to follow, because it would be the first time the Zagreb model, operator first, comes to Germany.
What the KBA register says
Since we had the register open anyway, here is the update from our last full count in CW36 (snapshot 18 August), using the same method: current permits only, counted as of this issue’s publish date.
What stands out:
Volkswagen took 12 of the 17 new nationwide Level 4 permits. Its share had been falling as new holders arrived (69% in CW32, 61% in CW36). Now it is rising again.
Momenta tripled its nationwide Level 4 permits from one to three, and remains the only foreign full-stack robotaxi developer with nationwide Level 4 in Germany.
Bertrandt is the only new holder of nationwide Level 4.
Mercedes-Benz was the busiest filer, with 17 new permits, all locally restricted and almost all Level 3. That pattern looks like private-car testing.
Waymo still holds none, eleven weeks after announcing Munich.
The standing caveat applies: a testing permit is not a type approval and not an operating permission for a commercial service.
Volkswagen also produced news this week, though indirectly.
May Mobility puts its driver on the ID. Buzz
In the past few weeks we have speculated a lot about what happens to MOIA. What we have also said repeatedly is that many players like the ID. Buzz as a vehicle platform. In CW44, NVIDIA described Volkswagen Commercial Vehicles’ ID. Buzz AD platform as designed “to support multiple autonomous-driving systems.”
This week brought a signal that this still holds. May Mobility plans to bring the Volkswagen ID. Buzz to global robotaxi platforms, powered by its own autonomous driving system:
More than 10,000 autonomous ID. Buzz vehicles through 2031
On-road testing in the US from early 2027, first production vehicles delivered in 2028
ECARX, the Geely-affiliated automotive technology company, integrates May’s autonomy kit into the AV-ready ID. Buzz and coordinates the supply chain, together with Charlton Intelligence.ai
Target: all-in cost savings of 50% or more compared with May’s current-generation vehicle
May’s CEO Edwin Olson: “Scaling autonomous ride-hail depends heavily on the vehicle platform.”
To see what this announcement means, it helps to go back to May’s SPAC presentation from September, because the ID. Buzz fits into it almost exactly.
First, the vehicle May teased. In its investor deck, May shows its current platform, the Toyota Sienna, and next to it a car under a cover: the “Next Generation of May,” expected to be unveiled in the second half of 2026. The promises next to it: “OEM integration eliminates costly post-production upfitting,” and “volume agreements unlock supply chain pricing.”
Source: May Mobility Investor Deck
Two months later, in the second half of 2026, May announces an “AV-ready” ID. Buzz that comes integrated from the factory, with ECARX and Charlton handling the supply chain. May hasn’t said that the car under the cover is the ID. Buzz. But the timing and the description match, so that is how I read it. It would also address a risk May lists itself in the deck: its dependence on its partnerships with Toyota and NTT. A second carmaker reduces that dependence.
Second, the cost. As we reported in CW39, citing Walter Piecyk, May’s bill of materials was about $270,000 per vehicle, a figure that largely reflects development costs at very small volumes. May’s own deck promises a cut of more than 50%, and its chart is more ambitious than the headline. Read against the axis, it shows roughly $260,000 today (about $160,000 for the autonomy kit and $100,000 for the base vehicle), about $195,000 within 12 months, and a 2028 target of around $80,000. May labels the figures as illustrative.
Source: May Mobility Investor Deck
And 2028 is exactly when the first production ID. Buzz vehicles are due. This week’s release promises all-in savings of 50% or more against the current vehicle, which matches the headline of the deck. If May gets close to the chart instead, around $80,000 would put it below estimates for a Waymo (under $100,000), though still at roughly 2.7 times what Tesla is believed to pay for a Cybercab (under $30,000).
Here is the part I’m least sure about. The ID. Buzz isn’t a cheap base vehicle: it starts at $59,995 in the US. A fleet buyer at volume pays less than list price, so that number is only a guide. But if the base vehicle cost anything close to it, an $80,000 target would leave around $20,000 for the autonomy kit and its integration, against roughly $160,000 for the kit today. That is the number I would watch most closely.
Third, the scale. May runs about 100 vehicles at three sites today. Its deck targets 300 to 400 by the end of 2027, “thousands” by the end of 2028 and “tens of thousands” by 2030. More than 10,000 ID. Buzz vehicles would be a hundred times today’s fleet, and production starts in the year the deck needs the jump to thousands.
Source: May Mobility Investor Deck
Fourth, who pays for the cars. May doesn’t plan to own them. In its asset-light model, the fleet operating partner takes on the vehicle costs, from financing and depreciation to insurance and maintenance, while May keeps the remote supervision and software.
Source: May Mobility Investor Deck
At around $80,000 per vehicle, 10,000 ID. Buzz vehicles would mean roughly $0.8 billion in vehicle capital that someone else has to raise. 
The announcement talks about global platforms. May’s partners include Uber (commercial operations in Arlington, Texas, from 2027), Lyft (Atlanta) and Grab, plus Toyota and NTT in Japan. And as we reported in CW27, May signed a partnership with the Chinese platform CaoCao to bring robotaxis to international markets, “beginning with Europe.”
That deal is still at the stage of feasibility studies and pilots, and this week’s release doesn’t mention Europe. So this is my inference. May hasn’t said it. But it is quite possible that one day we see ID. Buzzes with May’s driver here in Europe, too.
I’ll have May Mobility’s CEO Edwin Olson on Autonomy Insiders in the coming weeks, and I’m really looking forward to going into the details with him on all of this.
What this means and what to watch
First, Germany is moving from announcements to hiring. Verne’s Berlin roles are the first concrete sign that the Zagreb operator model is coming here. Watch for the first KBA permit with Verne’s or Pony.ai’s name on it, because nothing in Berlin can start before that.
Second, the vehicle decides the partner. If Verne brings the Arcfox, Pony.ai is the obvious driver. If it brings its own two-seater, Mobileye comes into play. Watch which vehicle shows up in the first Berlin test photos.
Third, the ID. Buzz is becoming a multi-driver platform. Mobileye drives it for MOIA in Hamburg (or maybe soon Wayve?), May now plans to drive it from 2027. Watch whether a third driver appears on it, and whether that tells us more about MOIA’s future.
Fourth, May’s 2028 cost target is the number to hold it to. Around $80,000 per vehicle would put May below Waymo’s estimated cost and make 10,000 ID. Buzz vehicles financeable for its partners. Watch what May says about the vehicle price, and whether the SPAC closes in Q1 2027 as planned.
Fifth, Volkswagen is pulling ahead again on permits. 12 of 17 new nationwide Level 4 permits in under three months. Whatever happens with MOIA, the group is by far the most active Level 4 tester in Germany.

Saudi Arabia publishes rules for driverless passenger transport
Saudi Arabia’s Transport General Authority approved executive regulations for passenger transport by self-driving vehicles, effective 6 January 2027. Operators need at least five vehicles, a safety officer per vehicle (in the car or remote), and a three-year licence limited to an approved area. They can apply to run without an onboard officer, subject to the authority’s assessment. Operators can use their own apps or licensed ride-hailing platforms, and in-car advertising is allowed. That gives the Kingdom a written path from supervised to driverless, which is more than most markets have.
