GTC week: Europe’s robotaxi race gets crowded, and NVIDIA sits underneath most of it
GTC Berlin made this one of the busiest European weeks of the year, and almost every European announcement had the same supplier somewhere in the fine print. I was at the GTC, and it was a great event!
But the first news has nothing to do with GTC. 
As usual, we start with Waymo.
Waymo arrives at the Las Vegas airport
Waymo now takes riders to and from Harry Reid International Airport in Las Vegas. Pick-ups and drop-offs happen at dedicated “autonomous vehicle zones” at Terminal 1 and Terminal 3. Waymo starts with a small group of riders before opening the airport to everyone.
It is Waymo’s sixth major airport, after Phoenix Sky Harbor, San José, San Francisco, San Antonio and, last week, Nashville. And it comes fast. Waymo opened Las Vegas to the public in mid-September and already serves a 24-square-mile area there. Five weeks later, it has the airport.
In CW37 we wrote that “Zoox has Harry Reid to itself for now,” and that the highest-value curb in the business would not stay uncontested for long. It didn’t. Zoox still serves the airport, and with Tesla and Uber holding permits for thousands more vehicles in the city, it will not be the last operator to show up at the terminals either.
Last week I wrote that I expect every new Waymo market to have an airport on its roadmap from day one. Las Vegas took about five weeks.
Waymo wants to quintuple its fleet
Waymo’s story remains expansion, and according to Semafor it now has a number attached. Citing people familiar with the matter, Semafor reports that Waymo plans to grow from about 4,000 vehicles today to 8,000 by the end of this year and 20,000 by the end of 2027. Waymo declined to comment.
The money is already there. Waymo recently borrowed $5 billion from private credit lenders, its first debt raise, and without a guarantee from Alphabet. The structure also allows Waymo to borrow more in the future by securitising the leases on its fleet. Waymo said it raised the funds to grow in the US and internationally.
The 2027 number gets the headline, but the year-end number is the harder one. Going from 4,000 to 8,000 by New Year means adding as many vehicles in roughly ten weeks as Waymo has put on the road in its entire history so far. That is only possible if the new vehicles, the Zeekr-built Ojai and the first Hyundai IONIQ 5s due this quarter, arrive and get commissioned at a pace we haven’t seen before. 
One caveat: these are anonymous sources, not a target Waymo has confirmed.
And another piece of Waymo’s European expansion was announced this week.
Waymo is coming to Madrid
After London and Munich, Waymo is heading to Spain, starting with the Region of Madrid. Waymo aims to welcome its first riders in 2027.
Waymo’s Spain page and its FAQ answer most of the practical questions, so here is what they say.
The vehicles. In the coming days, Waymo’s electric Jaguar I-PACEs will start appearing on the region’s streets.
The phases. Waymo starts with manual driving. Then it moves to autonomous driving with trained local validation drivers behind the wheel. Only after that comes fully autonomous operation. Until then, no commercial rides.
The area. Waymo will drive in several municipalities across the region, initially including “but not limited to” Madrid City, Alcobendas and Getafe.
The hours. Testing will “typically” run 24 hours a day, seven days a week, day and night, under the guidelines of the Directorate-General for Traffic (DGT).
The rules of the road. The Waymo Driver has to learn local traffic law, signs based on the 1968 Vienna Convention on Road Signs and Signals, and the lane discipline and right-of-way rules for pedestrians and cyclists that are common in cities like Madrid. That is a good reminder that a European launch is more than a new map.
The operator. Waymo says it is “engaging with local partners” and “investing in the local operations needed to support our service.” It does not name a fleet operator.
We set a standard for announcements like this one: an international expansion is an ambition until the cities, the regulator and the local fleet partners are disclosed. Madrid names the cities, the regulator, the vehicle and the year. The operator is the missing piece. In Toronto, Waymo named Element two years before the first paid ride. In Madrid, we don’t know yet who will charge, clean and maintain the cars.
But Waymo will not be alone in Madrid.
WeRide, Uber and the local fleet operator AVOMO got there first. As we reported in CW38, the three received Spain’s first national Level 4 permit from the DGT in September, for up to 20 vehicles in designated areas of Greater Madrid. WeRide provides the driving technology, Uber the demand and AVOMO runs the fleet. According to Gasgoo, the three still aim to launch Spain’s first commercial robotaxi service in Madrid by the end of 2026, subject to completing operational preparations and the remaining regulatory requirements.
Frank McCleary of Arthur D. Little told Zag Daily that Madrid could help validate the commercial operating model, while London tests technical capability. “The bigger signal is that European regulators are beginning to establish credible pathways towards accelerating commercial deployment rather than languishing in endless, isolated pilots,” he said.
So once again, Waymo arrives in a market outside the US where someone else is already preparing to carry riders. In CW39 we saw the same pattern in Singapore. Waymo’s technology lead is not in question, but abroad it is often the second or third name on the permit list.
And Madrid’s list got longer still this week.
Bolt and Lucid choose NVIDIA for their European robotaxis, starting in Madrid in 2027
We covered the Bolt and Lucid deal in CW39: at least 25,000 fully autonomous vehicles on Lucid’s upcoming mid-size platform, with NVIDIA Hyperion for compute and sensors. What was missing was an important piece: who writes the driving software.
Back then, the most common guess was Nuro. I wrote: “I do not think it will be Nuro.” It isn’t.
This week, Bolt announced that it has selected NVIDIA’s Alpamayo Stack, NVIDIA’s full-stack driving software, for its European autonomous ride-hailing programme, starting with Lucid.
Here is what was announced:
The stack. Alpamayo Stack runs on NVIDIA Hyperion and combines what NVIDIA calls reasoning AI with classical perception and planning. On top sits NVIDIA Halos, NVIDIA’s safety system, which brings together safety-engineered compute, system software, AI guardrails and validation tools.
The start. The programme begins in Madrid in 2027, initially gathering real-world driving data to develop and validate the software, subject to regulatory approval. So no riders at the start.
The next markets. Germany and France are named as key markets for future deployments. Bolt’s Jevgeni Kabanov said that “Germany will also be a priority for what comes next.”
The scale. The programme lays the foundation for a rollout of “at least 25,000” Level 4-ready Lucid vehicles equipped with Hyperion, as part of Bolt’s plan to put 100,000 autonomous vehicles on its platform across Europe and the wider UNECE region by 2035.
The roles are clearly split. Bolt Autonomous Driving Solutions leads development and commercialisation of the service, bringing its fleet management, its regulatory work and its local market knowledge. NVIDIA and Bolt will adapt the driving software to European roads together and integrate it into vehicles from several manufacturers, with Lucid as the first. Lucid, through Lucid Technologies, provides the software-defined vehicle. Lucid’s Kay Stepper called next year’s start of data collection one of “the first steps.”
Two details are worth a closer look.
First, the wording. The release frames 25,000 as a floor rather than a ceiling. That is a meaningful choice of words, although a press release floor is, not the same as a firm order, and as of CW39, no money had changed hands.
Second, the numbers for Lucid. In CW39 we added up Lucid’s robotaxi order book at roughly 60,000 vehicles. Uber’s programme with Lucid and Nuro now calls for at least 35,000 Gravity vehicles globally, and Bolt adds at least 25,000. Both demand partners now have a named driver: Nuro for Uber in the US, NVIDIA for Bolt in Europe.
What we left out in CW39 is how large that number is for Lucid. Lucid delivered 15,841 vehicles in all of 2025 and 10,852 in the first nine months of 2026. So 60,000 robotaxis is almost four years of Lucid’s current deliveries. As a signal of demand for a purpose-built robotaxi platform, that is significant. As a production plan, it is a big step for a company of Lucid’s size.
There is also a timing question. The Bolt deal was designed around Lucid’s mid-size platform, whose first vehicle has slipped into 2027, “most likely the second half.” If that platform isn’t ready in time, Electrek notes, Madrid’s first data-collection cars would have to be existing Gravity or Air vehicles.
But Bolt wasn’t done this week.
Bolt and Stellantis want to bring autonomous mobility to Paris with Pony.ai
The next announcement takes us to France, one of the two priority markets named in the Lucid release. This one, though, comes with different partners.
Métropole du Grand Paris, Bolt and Stellantis announced plans to introduce autonomous mobility services across Greater Paris. They submitted the project, called the Greater Paris Autonomous Driving Living Lab, to the European Commission’s Autonomous Drive Ambition Cities (ADACities) initiative.
Here is the plan:
The scope. Métropole du Grand Paris represents Paris and its 129 surrounding municipalities, home to 7.2 million people.
The timeline. Starting in 2028, initial deployments are planned in up to five municipalities in north-west Greater Paris. The ambition is to expand to around 30 municipalities by 2030, moving towards commercial operations by 2030, subject to approvals.
The use cases. Autonomous services should complement public transport rather than compete with it: first- and last-mile trips, access to employment hubs, off-peak travel and major events.
The evaluation. The Métropole will oversee an independent evaluation covering operational safety, service quality and user acceptance, integration with public transport, social and environmental effects, and the economics of a wider rollout.
The roles:
Métropole du Grand Paris coordinates with local authorities, provides the framework for real-world testing and oversees the evaluation.
Bolt, again through Bolt Autonomous Driving Solutions, leads service integration and deployment planning.
Stellantis provides vehicles built on its L4-Ready Platforms, plus design, engineering, manufacturing and vehicle integration.
And the driving software? The release is careful here. It says Pony.ai “has been identified as a potential provider of the autonomous driving technology, which will be powered by NVIDIA Hyperion and the NVIDIA Halos safety system.” From my own conversations, I understand Pony.ai is more than a potential provider: it is set to be the virtual driver for Greater Paris, running on NVIDIA as described.
That makes sense. As we reported in CW25, Bolt, Pony.ai and Stellantis already run a pilot together in Luxembourg, with Pony.ai’s seventh-generation stack on a Stellantis L4-ready van. Paris is the natural next step for the same trio, with one difference worth noting: here, Pony.ai’s driver runs on NVIDIA’s compute and NVIDIA’s safety system.
So Bolt is hedging its bets on both sides. On the vehicle side it works with Lucid and Stellantis, and on the driver side with NVIDIA and Pony.ai. With no stack yet proven in a European city, I think that is the right call for a demand platform, even if it means running two validation programmes in parallel before carrying a single driverless passenger.
Bolt’s partners are hedging too, and Stellantis had a second France announcement on the same day.
Wayve, Stellantis and NVIDIA team up for autonomous driving across France
At the Assises du Véhicule Autonome in Paris, on 23 October, Wayve, Stellantis and NVIDIA announced a collaboration with the French government to advance the deployment of autonomous vehicles in France and accelerate development across the EU.
Here is who does what:
Stellantis brings its L4-Ready Platforms and its vehicle engineering and manufacturing.
Wayve brings the Wayve AI Driver.
NVIDIA brings the compute and safety layer: the Wayve AI Driver will integrate NVIDIA Halos OS on the NVIDIA Hyperion compute and sensor architecture.
Local French partners will lead the engagement with unions, city authorities and mobility stakeholders, and they are the ones responsible for transport operations.
France’s Transport Minister Philippe Tabarot said France is committed to adapting its regulatory framework “while maintaining strict safety and data sovereignty requirements.”
This one is a framework rather than a deployment. There is no city, no date, no fleet size and no named operator yet. But it builds on an existing partnership, as Stellantis and Wayve have already shown Fiat 500e and Maserati Grecale development vehicles running the Wayve AI Driver.
Look at the two announcements side by side. On the same day, in the same country, Stellantis appeared with two different driving systems: Pony.ai with Bolt for Greater Paris, and Wayve with the French government. Both are set to run on NVIDIA Hyperion and Halos.
And Stellantis’ partner Pony.ai made more news this week, but for that we have to go to another continent.
Pony.ai and inDrive want to bring robotaxis to Kazakhstan in 2027
inDrive is a ride-hailing company best known for letting passengers and drivers negotiate the fare. It is bigger than many people outside its markets realise: according to the company, gross bookings reached $6.4 billion in 2025, up 30%, revenue grew 31% to $601.6 million, and inDrive was profitable. Sensor Tower ranks it the world’s second most downloaded mobility app. It is also not new to robotaxis: in December it signed an agreement with Saudi Arabia’s AI Driver to explore deploying WeRide vehicles in Riyadh, with a pilot targeted for the first half of 2026. 
Now inDrive has signed a memorandum of understanding with Pony.ai and NVIDIA. Under the MoU, the three will evaluate the technical, operational, regulatory and commercial feasibility of offering Level 4-ready vehicles through the inDrive app.
Where and when. Testing is set to begin in Kazakhstan’s capital, Astana, in the first half of 2027, according to inDrive CTO Yuri Misnik.
How. Initially with a safety operator, with fully driverless rides as the goal.
Who does what. Pony.ai provides its seventh-generation robotaxis. inDrive offers them through its app and operates them in Kazakhstan. NVIDIA supplies Hyperion and the Halos safety system.
“Our approach is capital-light by design,” said Misnik.
Kazakhstan is preparing for this. The government announced plans in September to launch autonomous taxi services in 2027, with pilots in Almaty, Astana and Kosshy, and it has introduced rules that allow autonomous vehicles to be tested on certain public roads. According to inDrive, Astana was chosen for its regulatory readiness, after Kazakhstan created its first structured legal framework for driverless transport. Yandex is already testing a driverless vehicle in Almaty.
Astana is also a demanding place to drive. The city of about 1.7 million faces months of snow and ice. “Launching here will provide an important opportunity to evaluate autonomous driving technology in challenging winter conditions,” said inDrive founder and CEO Arsen Tomsky. I agree. A Pony.ai Gen-7 that works through an Astana winter is a strong argument in every cold-climate city on Pony.ai’s list.
And Kazakhstan keeps attracting Chinese robotaxi companies. As we reported in CW30, Baidu signed an Apollo Go MoU for Kazakhstan in July, the first Chinese robotaxi entry into Central Asia. Pony.ai makes it two in three months.
The deal also fits Pony.ai’s international push. The company targets more than 3,500 robotaxis in over 20 cities by the end of 2026.
NVIDIA argues that Hyperion lowers the cost of building a robotaxi, because companies get a ready-made foundation of sensors and compute. Ali Kani explained why that matters in markets like this one: “Those savings are particularly relevant in emerging markets, where ride-hailing is already relatively affordable and autonomous services need to compete on cost.” The announcement coincided with Jensen Huang’s first visit to Kazakhstan.
If Astana works, the companies will look at other Kazakh cities and at other countries where inDrive operates, 48 according to the company.
Which brings us to the common denominator of this week.
NVIDIA is becoming the backbone of autonomous driving 
Go back through this week’s announcements and look for NVIDIA:
Bolt and Lucid: Alpamayo Stack on Hyperion, with Halos.
Bolt and Stellantis in Paris: Pony.ai’s driver, powered by Hyperion and Halos.
Wayve and Stellantis in France: the Wayve AI Driver on Hyperion, with Halos OS.
inDrive and Pony.ai in Kazakhstan: Hyperion and Halos.
Einride: Alpamayo on Hyperion for autonomous trucks (more in Quick Takes).
And at GTC itself: Mercedes-Benz extended its work with NVIDIA on Hyperion and Alpamayo Stack for its Level 3 and Level 4 development. Volvo Cars is adopting Hyperion, Halos OS and Alpamayo Stack. Volkswagen Commercial Vehicles showed its ID. Buzz AD on Hyperion. TIER IV and the Technical University of Munich plan to use Hyperion for autonomous public transport in Germany. And Aptiv, Valeo and Schaeffler are building Hyperion-based computers that carmakers can integrate.
NVIDIA also used GTC to move further into safety. Its new Halos SAFE framework, developed with dSPACE, IVEX and TÜV Rheinland, connects simulation, in-the-loop testing, road testing and fleet monitoring to produce traceable safety evidence. In Europe, safety evidence is what type approval runs on.
NVIDIA has always been an important supplier to AV companies. What is changing is how far up the stack it goes. When NVIDIA first laid out its full-stack plans, I wrote that it now in some ways competes with its own customers. And in CW34 I described the logic: NVIDIA gives away the model to sell the silicon.
This week adds three things.
First, NVIDIA is now the named driver of a Level 4 robotaxi fleet programme. Mercedes and Volvo are driver assistance first, with Level 4 as a future goal. Bolt chose Alpamayo as the virtual driver for a fleet of at least 25,000 robotaxis.
Second, NVIDIA now supplies and competes with the same company, inside the same customer. In Bolt’s portfolio, NVIDIA’s Alpamayo Stack now sits next to Pony.ai’s driver. In the same week, Pony.ai’s deals in Paris and Kazakhstan both run on NVIDIA’s Hyperion and Halos.
Third, NVIDIA sits underneath both answers. In France, Stellantis picked two different drivers on the same day, and NVIDIA is in both.
I got a small taste of the software myself. At GTC, I took a test drive in NVIDIA’s L2+ system together with Philipp Raasch , and we were both impressed by how well it handled difficult situations, including very narrow streets with oncoming traffic. 
That was a supervised system, so it says nothing directly about driverless operation. But it showed me that the software is further along than many people assume.
But here is what makes NVIDIA’s role interesting. Buying driving software isn’t new. Wayve, Momenta and others already license their stacks to carmakers.
The difference is in the business model. For them, the software is the business. For NVIDIA, the business is the chips and the compute underneath, and automotive is roughly 1% of NVIDIA’s revenue. It doesn’t need to make much money on the stack itself.
That shifts the economics. Software-only players face pricing pressure from a competitor that can treat software as a way to sell silicon. Carmakers and platforms get easier access to strong urban driving capability, but they also become more dependent on one platform.
So what does NVIDIA actually want? There are two ways to read this.
Read one way, NVIDIA should want as many AV companies as possible to survive, because every one of them buys its chips. That aligns its incentives with Uber and Bolt, who also want a fragmented market with many drivers to choose from.
Read the other way, its own stack lets NVIDIA capture the highly profitable recurring software revenue and still put its chips into every vehicle.
I lean towards the first reading. Alpamayo’s weights are open, Hyperion explicitly runs other companies’ drivers like Wayve’s, and Halos SAFE evaluates anyone’s stack. That looks like a platform strategy with a reference driver attached. But I hold that view loosely. If NVIDIA starts taking a share of fleet revenue, through per-mile fees or revenue sharing rather than licences and compute, I would read it the other way. I’m curious to see how this develops.
One last note on GTC. Last week I wrote that we should watch which European carmakers stand on NVIDIA’s stage, and whether Volkswagen names a technology partner for MOIA. The first part is answered: Mercedes, Volvo, VW Commercial Vehicles, Bolt with Lucid, and Einride. The MOIA question is still open. There was one small detail for our Wayve and MOIA theory, though. NVIDIA said VW Commercial Vehicles’ ID. Buzz AD platform on Hyperion is designed “to support multiple autonomous-driving systems.” The link to MOIA is my own inference, and a long way from a contract, but a platform built for more than one driver is a platform that could take a second one.
What this all means and what to watch
First, Madrid is becoming Europe’s test case. It now has three robotaxi programmes, Waymo, WeRide with Uber and AVOMO, and Bolt with NVIDIA and Lucid, and no paying riders yet. Watch whether WeRide and Uber launch commercially before the end of the year, as promised. 
Second, watch who operates Waymo’s Madrid fleet. In Toronto, Waymo named its operator two years ahead. Madrid is the first recent Waymo market where that piece is missing.
Third, watch Waymo’s fleet count, not its city count. If Semafor is right, Waymo has to double its fleet in about ten weeks. A year-end figure well short of 8,000 would tell us the commissioning bottleneck is real. A figure close to it would mean Waymo’s scaling problem has moved from vehicles to permits.
Fourth, NVIDIA is now underneath both answers to Europe’s software question, which lets it win without having to pick a winner. The pressure lands on software-only players like Wayve, Momenta, Pony.ai and Nuro, which have to justify a price against a competitor that can treat its software as a way to sell chips. Watch whether NVIDIA’s terms ever move from licences and compute to a share of fleet revenue.
Fifth, Lucid has to build what it has sold. Roughly 60,000 robotaxis on order is almost four years of current deliveries. Watch the mid-size platform date. If it isn't ready when Madrid starts in 2027, the first cars will be existing Gravity or Air vehicles, and the order book becomes a production question.
Sixth, MOIA is still open. No technology partner was named at GTC. I still think it will be Wayve.

Tesla Q3 2026 Earnings Deep Dive
Tesla’s robotaxi miles are growing quickly. The number that would tell us whether anyone is paying for them is no longer in the shareholder letter.
The numbers: better margins, lower profit
Tesla reported $28.05 billion in revenue for the third quarter, flat year over year and in line with the $28.06 billion Wall Street expected. Deliveries came in at 486,532 vehicles, down 2.1% year over year but well ahead of consensus. Tesla also delivered about 22,000 more cars than it built, bringing inventory down.
Source: App Economy Insights  
The margins recovered:
Total gross margin rose to 19.6%, up from 16.8% in Q2 and 18.0% a year ago.
Automotive gross margin excluding regulatory credits reached 18.1%, up from 16.3% in Q2 and 15.4% a year ago. Tesla credits a better regional mix and more FSD subscription revenue.
Regulatory credits brought in only $113 million, down 73%. With US automakers no longer paying federal fuel-economy penalties, that line isn’t coming back.
So why did profit still fall? Because spending grew faster than gross profit. Operating expenses rose 39% to $4.78 billion, and research and development alone jumped 69% to $2.76 billion, as Tesla spends on AI, Optimus and the Cybercab. Operating income fell 56% to $718 million, an operating margin of 2.6% (5.8% a year ago). GAAP net income dropped 43% to $787 million. Non-GAAP earnings of $0.48 per share beat the $0.45 consensus.
Free cash flow came in at $1.53 billion, after a negative $1.09 billion in Q2. Tesla ended the quarter with $45.4 billion in cash and investments. Capital spending was $4.73 billion, more than double a year ago, but lower than the $5.79 billion of Q2.
That brings us to a detail most coverage missed. Earlier this year, Tesla guided for more than $25 billion in capex for 2026. On the call, CFO Vaibhav Taneja said Tesla is “revising our CapEx guidance to be approximately $20 billion for the current year.” With $13 billion spent in the first nine months, that implies roughly $7 billion in Q4. The lower spending also flatters this quarter’s free cash flow. Tesla also secured the capacity to borrow up to $30 billion to support future capex, but says it doesn’t plan to draw on it this year.
Energy was the weak spot on revenue, down 16% to $2.88 billion despite higher deployments, which Tesla partly blames on tariff-related customer refunds. Services and other was the bright spot, up 33% to $4.61 billion with a record $734 million in gross profit. The Robotaxi business sits in that line.
And Taneja made clear where the money is going: “This trend of growing OPEX would continue till we commercialize these new products.”
One of the biggest bets is autonomy and the Cybercab, and there was news here too.
The Cybercab heads to Dallas
Tesla’s Cybercab is leaving Austin. According to Ashok Elluswamy, Tesla’s head of AI, it launches in Dallas next month and should reach other cities by the end of the year. Tesla’s Robotaxi service already runs Model Ys in six US metros.
The ambition is not small. “We’re starting with getting people from one place to the other, but we want to basically own transportation of any kind on Earth,” Taneja said on the call. Tesla showed a multi-passenger concept called the Robovan in 2024, but it is unclear whether these comments mean Tesla plans to offer other vehicle types any time soon. Elon Musk went one step further: “I think Tesla will end up doing a majority of all passenger miles on earth in any country where it is allowed to operate freely.”
More miles, less disclosure
Tesla says its Robotaxi fleet reached nearly 2 million cumulative unsupervised miles by the end of the quarter, and that accumulated unsupervised miles grew eightfold in Q3. 
Source: Tesla Q3/2026 Update Deck
Musk said the Cybercab fleet in Austin has “grown by a factor of 10 since launch about a month ago.” He did not say from what base.
What Tesla didn’t say is how many of those miles were paid. In its Q2 letter, Tesla showed a chart of “Cumulative Paid Robotaxi Miles.” That chart implied paid miles fell from about 1.1 million in Q1 to about 700,000 in Q2, roughly minus 36%, while Tesla went from four markets to seven. This quarter, TechCrunch noticed, the chart is gone. In its place is “Cumulative Unsupervised Robotaxi Miles,” which counts how far the cars drive, whether or not anyone is on board.
There are two ways to read this. Read charitably, paid miles are a misleading metric for a fleet in ramp-up, when a growing share of driving is Cybercab calibration and repositioning. Read skeptically, the one robotaxi series that was going the wrong way disappeared the quarter after it did. Whatever the motive, I think the effect is the same: less visibility at the moment Tesla asks investors to believe in exponential growth.
In CW31 I wrote that until Tesla publishes quarterly paid miles and an active vehicle count, every claim about exponential growth is unfalsifiable. 
Asked about paid rides, Taneja said Tesla is “seeing utilization improving,” without numbers. Asked about the cost per mile, he was more direct: “Are we at our goal of 30 cents today? No. But do we see a path to get to that? Yes, we do.” According to TechCrunch, Musk said the Robotaxi business won’t be noticeable in Tesla’s financials until Q2 2027, “and probably a big deal by Q3 or Q4 of next year.”
And last week I wrote: “I don’t expect Tesla to disclose its remote intervention numbers on Wednesday, or anytime soon.” It didn’t. Not in the letter, not on the call.
What is actually limiting the Cybercab?
On scaling, the executives described three different constraints.
Taneja pointed to regulators: “They think this is one of the easiest products for them to scale in terms of manufacturing. So scaling would not be that much of a challenge. I think the key thing which we’re trying to make sure is that we have all the regulatory approvals where we can start deploying the cybercabs as fast as we can produce them.”
Elluswamy pointed to the software: “It’s not purely regulatory or something like that. It is technology-driven, but we do see that pretty quickly become regulatory, perhaps in Q1, Q2 timeframes.” Before scaling further, Tesla wants to unlock 24/7 operation, higher-speed roads, highways, and more weather and lighting conditions, hopefully by the end of this year.
Lars Moravy, Tesla’s SVP of vehicle engineering, gave the most revealing answer. The Cybercab line’s listed capacity is more than 125,000 vehicles a year, and its “actual capacity” is “significantly higher.” But: “Right now, we’re building at the rate that the Robotaxi service needs the vehicles.”
You could read that as a disagreement. I read it differently: they are describing the same bottleneck from different chairs. If the factory is building to the pace of the service, production is not the constraint. The constraint is how many cars the service can put to work, and that comes down to software, operations and permission. Regulators matter too. NHTSA is investigating how Tesla certified the Cybercab, which has no steering wheel or pedals, as compliant with federal safety standards, and Tesla has until 30 October to respond.
Where the executives clearly did not line up is ownership. Moravy said: “We’re trying to get cybercabs to customers by the end of this year, 2026.” Musk, on the same call: “The vast majority of the cybercab stuff will be essentially Tesla owned and, and transport as a service.”
One more detail on what the software is still learning. Elluswamy explained that Tesla initially ran the Robotaxi service from 6 a.m. to 9 p.m., then extended it to 10 p.m. and 11 p.m., and “pretty soon” to 2 a.m. “That is driven by the capability of the AI software,” he said. In CW42 I argued that darkness alone can’t explain where the cut-off sits, and I leaned, as speculation, towards operations setting the hours. Tesla’s own explanation is now on the record, and it points to the software. I’ll take that at face value, and watch whether the 2 a.m. extension arrives.
Unsupervised FSD for customers in Q2 2027, with a catch
The question retail shareholders wanted answered was when FSD goes unsupervised in customer cars. Elluswamy’s answer: around Q2 2027.
But there is a catch. “The only additional requirement is to make sure that, you know, for example, the customer model-wise can be part of the geofences and the fleet management that Tesla has for it to be fully autonomous. We suspect somewhere around Q2 next year is when we can turn on some of the first customer vehicles to be part of the Robotaxi fleet.”
So the first step is not a car that drives its owner anywhere. It is owners adding their cars to Tesla’s Robotaxi network, inside Tesla’s service areas, run by Tesla’s fleet management. A customer’s car would drive itself unsupervised only where Tesla’s Robotaxi already operates.
That is still a long way from what Elon Musk has promised. In July 2025 he said unsupervised FSD would be available “for unsupervised personal use by the end of this year in certain geographies.” In April this year he said it would “probably” come in Q4 2026. And owners of HW3 cars have already been told their computers can’t run unsupervised FSD at all.
FSD itself keeps growing as a business. Tesla reports 1.74 million paid customers, a figure that includes upfront purchases, and half of them now pay by subscription. More than 75% of North American deliveries in Q3 signed up for a paid FSD subscription at delivery, up from around 55% in Q2. Taneja added an important caveat himself: every car comes with a free month of FSD. So this is a sign-up rate, not a retention rate, and Tesla doesn’t disclose churn yet. When New Street’s Pierre Ferragu estimated FSD at close to $1 billion in annual recurring revenue, Taneja said: “I think the numbers which you shared are correct.”
Tesla is building its own maps
Another interesting detail came from the shareholder Q&A. A retail investor wrote that mapping “continues to be a problem” for FSD, listing speed limits, parking and typical routes, and asked why Tesla doesn’t build its own maps.
Elluswamy agreed: “You’re totally right that we shouldn’t be depending on external people’s maps. So we are building our own maps, but not like the same way as [Waymo]. We are building an entirely new architecture with essentially AI-based mapping and navigation.” The maps will learn from the whole fleet and from each driver’s preferences, and respond to voice. According to Electrek, this starts at the beginning and end of trips with FSD v15.
That is a notable shift in tone. At Autonomy Day in 2019, Musk called high-precision lane maps “a huge mistake” and “a really bad idea” that makes a self-driving system “extremely brittle.”
To be fair to Tesla, this is not an HD map in the sense Musk criticised. Tesla has always used navigation maps, and building its own instead of licensing them is not a contradiction in itself. Every serious self-driving programme uses maps, and a map built from millions of cars could be a real advantage.
What is interesting is which problems the map is meant to fix. A speed limit sign is about as simple as road information gets. Last month, a Belgian road safety group found that FSD drove too fast in 55% of the 30 km/h zones it tested in Brussels, averaging 44 km/h, partly because the car showed a 50 km/h limit. If the fix for that is a better map, then the vision system is not doing everything Tesla said it would. And it adds one more item to the list of things Tesla once dismissed and is now adopting, alongside geofenced service areas.
And yes, the merger talk is back
Tesla’s call came a day after SpaceX reported its own results, and the bigger headline came from there. According to the Financial Times, SpaceX and Tesla are studying a merger, and both companies have formed special committees to look at it. With Tesla valued at roughly $1.5 trillion and SpaceX at about $2 trillion, according to the BBC, it would be the largest merger ever.
“There is a lot of merit to combining the two companies as we are pretty much in the superintelligence singularity,” Musk said. And: “There is a lot to be gained from co-operating on developing superintelligence software, chip design, the Terafab, robotics, mass to orbit with the [Starlink] constellation connectivity.”
He was also careful. “I think it would be a good idea, but at the end of the day the shareholders have to be supportive of this,” he said, adding that as CEO of both companies he cannot take part in the discussions. “The special committees have not reached agreement on the terms of any transaction and obviously there is no guarantee that they will.” Calling the merger a certainty would be “overstating” things, he said. On Tesla’s own call, he described the topic as “tap dancing through a minefield,” and Tesla took no questions on it.
In CW29 I wrote that it was fairly clear to me Musk would attempt this, and that I could not tell you when. The special committees are the first formal step. The attempt has started. The timing is still open.
Control is the core of it. In CW29 I listed four reasons for Musk to merge the two companies rather than simply keep them working together, and control was the most important one. Musk holds roughly 20% of Tesla, with a single share class. At SpaceX, a dual-class structure gives him 42% of the shares but more than 80% of the voting rights. A combination on SpaceX’s terms would take him from minority voting power at Tesla to majority control of the combined company.
The BBC now makes the same point: the merger “would give Musk greater voting control over the combined company than he currently has at Tesla, a factor the special committee assessing the deal would have to consider.” Dan Sheehan of Telos Wealth Advisors put it plainly: “The deal itself doesn’t add to Musk’s wealth, but it concentrates it further in one stock.” Tesla shareholders, he added, would likely end up with “a smaller slice of a much larger and more complex business.” That is also why the special committees exist: the person who gains most in control is the CEO of both companies.
The hurdle. According to the BBC, half of Tesla’s shareholders would need to back the deal, a threshold Musk himself called “quite a high hurdle.” Tesla shareholders have backed him on big asks before. Last year, they approved a compensation package valued at around $1 trillion.
One of my reasons has weakened. In CW29, my first reason was capital: analyst consensus then expected Tesla’s free cash flow to turn negative in 2026, and Tesla’s cushion looked like it was thinning. This quarter, Tesla generated $1.53 billion in free cash flow and secured $30 billion in borrowing capacity. That makes the capital argument less urgent than it looked in July. The control argument is as strong as it was.
What it would mean for autonomy. For readers of this newsletter, this is the more important question, and there are two ways to read it.
Read optimistically, Tesla’s autonomy programme would get direct access to SpaceX’s capital and compute. SpaceX’s AI business alone brought in $7.6 billion in the third quarter. And the Terafab, which Musk says needs “tens of billions” through early 2028 and “probably north of 100 billion long term,” would no longer require a negotiation between two companies over who pays for what.
Read skeptically, the Cybercab becomes one line item in an AI infrastructure company, competing for capital with data centres, chips and rockets. David Nicholson of The Futurum Group put it bluntly: vehicles seem almost like “an afterthought.”
I don’t know yet which reading wins. That depends on terms nobody has seen.
The risks we flagged before still apply. In CW32 we reported on a Wall Street Journal story that some Tesla executives had been told to prepare for a possible separation of the China business, to clear a path for a merger. Musk called that report “fake news.” The underlying issue remains: SpaceX is a major US defence contractor, and Tesla manufactures at scale in China. According to the BBC, the deal could also face more regulatory pressure if Democrats win more seats in next month’s US midterm elections.
What this means and what to watch
First, Tesla can build Cybercabs faster than it can put them to work, and on Wednesday its own head of vehicle engineering said so. The binding constraint is the service: software, operations and permission. Watch Dallas. A Cybercab launch that grows like Austin would be the first real evidence that the model travels.
Second, the disclosure gap got wider. No paid miles, no active fleet count, no robotaxi revenue, no remote intervention numbers. Until Tesla publishes at least one of them, I’ll treat claims of exponential growth as unverified, not as wrong.
Third, unsupervised FSD for customers is now a fleet product. Q2 2027 means owners joining Tesla’s network, inside Tesla’s zones. Watch whether that date holds, and whether the zones grow fast enough to make it matter to an average owner.
Fourth, watch NHTSA. Tesla’s answer on the Cybercab certification is due on 30 October, and NHTSA’s proposal to drop the brake-pedal requirement for driverless vehicles is still pending. Those two decide how fast the Cybercab can scale legally.
Fifth, the money is moving. Capex guidance is down to about $20 billion for 2026, but Tesla has $30 billion of borrowing capacity and Musk talks about Terafab costs in the tens of billions. Watch whether 2027 guidance makes the cut look like a delay rather than a saving.
Sixth, the merger is a control question first. Watch the terms the committees propose, whether Tesla holders are offered a premium for giving up voting power, and how the approval threshold is defined.

💡 Quick Takes
Two US carmakers make the case for owning, not hailing
In the same week, two US carmaker leaders argued that autonomy will mostly live in cars people own. GM’s Sterling Anderson, a former Tesla executive, Aurora co-founder and a candidate to become GM’s next CEO, told the Financial Times that “there’s a degree of autonomy inherent to owning the car. You’re not at the whims of a network.” He called Level 3 a bad solution, because it relies on a distracted human taking over at a moment’s notice, and said GM plans Level 4 highway driving in the Cadillac Escalade IQ by 2028. Would GM enter the robotaxi business? “I’m not making any announcements, but there would be nothing stopping us.” Rivian’s RJ Scaringe made the same point with numbers at TechCrunch Disrupt: 95% of US miles are driven in vehicles people own, and even with Level 4 that share “may go from 95% to 90%, it may go from 95% to 80%.” He is already thinking about an “R4” designed for personally owned autonomy. Most of the industry’s money still flows into robotaxis. Two of America’s carmakers are betting that the bigger market sits in the driveway.

Rivian has acquired Third Dimension, a 15-person UK startup that uses AI to turn images and video into realistic 3D worlds. The goal is simulation: taking real driving scenarios from Rivian’s fleet, which uploads more than 400,000 sequences a day, and making them editable, with different camera angles, other road users, lighting or weather. “For us, it’s about taking real scenarios, but making them simulatable,” Philbin said. Mobileye gets its first TÜV SÜD certificate for driver assistance
TÜV SÜD has certified that Mobileye’s development process for automatic emergency braking (AEB) conforms with ISO 21448, the standard for Safety of the Intended Functionality (SOTIF). SOTIF covers the risks that arise even when a system works as designed, such as sensor limits and difficult real-world conditions, and complements functional safety, which deals with hardware and software faults. The certificate covers AEB only, but Mobileye says the same processes sit behind its wider ADAS and autonomous driving work, and it previously received a TÜV SÜD recommendation for the safety management system behind its Level 4 Mobileye Drive. AEB is already mandatory on new cars in the EU and will be on new US light vehicles by September 2029. With cheaper Chinese ADAS chips such as Axera’s winning business abroad, independent certification gives Mobileye a way to compete on more than price.

NHTSA proposes cyan lights for driverless cars
US regulators have proposed a new category of cyan exterior lamps to mark vehicles driving with an automated driving system. One or two green-blue lights would sit on the front, sides and rear, at least 15 inches above the ground, so police and other road users can tell a driverless car from a human-driven one. The lights would be optional. It is part of NHTSA’s wider effort to remove barriers that decades-old safety standards create for autonomous vehicles, alongside June’s proposal to allow vehicles without a brake pedal, which matters for Tesla’s Cybercab. The proposal is open for public comment, and finalising rules like this often takes months.
